English hides a distinction here, and the distinction is the entry.

*Washing* in English is ordinarily an action. Somebody washes something. So when Titus 3:5 says that God saved us through the washing of rebirth, an English reader naturally hears a process — a cleansing that happened, described in general terms.

The Greek noun is not an action word. *Loutron* is a **bath**. The thing, not the doing. The water you get into.

That sounds like a small point until you notice what it does to the sentence. A process can be spiritual, internal, and located wherever you prefer to put it. A bath is somewhere you are taken. It has water in it. Somebody else usually fills it.

Two occurrences in the New Testament, and between them they say everything the confessional tradition has wanted to say about baptism — one supplying the water, the other supplying the word.

## The Word

The Greek word is λουτρόν (*loutron*), pronounced in the Erasmian convention as *LOO-tron*, with the accent on the first syllable. It is a second-declension neuter noun.

The formation matters. It is built on *louō* (λούω), to bathe, with the suffix *-tron*, which in Greek marks an **instrument or a place** — the means by which, or the site where, something is done. An *arotron* is a plough, the thing you plough with. A *theatron* is a theatre, the place where you watch. A *loutron* is the bath: the thing you wash in, or the place where washing happens.

The verb family repays attention, because Greek distinguishes three kinds of washing that English collapses into one word.

*Louō* (λούω) — to bathe the **whole body**. Used of washing a corpse for burial (Acts 9:37), of the jailer at Philippi washing the wounds of Paul and Silas (Acts 16:33), and at Hebrews 10:22 of bodies washed with pure water. The proverb Peter quotes about a sow returning to the mud uses it too.

*Niptō* (νίπτω) — to wash a **part**: hands, feet, face.

*Plynō* (πλύνω) — to wash **objects**, especially clothing. Revelation has the saints washing their robes.

John 13:10 uses two of them in one sentence, and the distinction is the whole point of the verse: the one who has *bathed* — *louō*, the whole body — needs only to *wash* his feet, *niptō*. Whatever happened in the bath does not need repeating. What is left is the day's dirt.

Also in the family: *apolouō* (ἀπολούω), to wash away, which appears at Acts 22:16 — get up, be baptized, and have your sins washed away, calling on his name — and at 1 Corinthians 6:11, you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified.

**Background.** The word belonged to ordinary life before it belonged to theology. A *loutron* was a bath, and in the Greek world a public one; bathing was a civic amenity and a social occasion rather than a private routine.

The Septuagint uses it in a pastoral image — flocks coming up from the washing — and behind the New Testament's use stands the whole world of Israel's purification washings: the ritual immersions of Leviticus, the requirement to wash after contact with a corpse, and the promise of Ezekiel that God will sprinkle clean water on his people and they will be clean. Water that makes a person fit to approach God was not a new idea in the first century. What was new was the claim about what one particular bath does.

## Range of Meaning

*A bath — the ordinary sense.* The base meaning, and it is never abandoned. Neither New Testament use is metaphorical in the way English readers often assume; both refer to something that involves water.

*The washing of rebirth.* Titus 3:5. See [*paliggenesia*](/greek/paliggenesia/) for the second noun in the phrase.

*the washing of the water by the word* Ephesians 5:26.

That is the entire New Testament range: two verses, both baptismal, neither of them describing anything else. There is no third use to complicate the picture and none to soften it.

## Where You’ll Meet It

*Titus 3:5.* "He saved us, not by the righteous deeds we had done, but according to His mercy, through the washing of new birth and renewal by the Holy Spirit."

The grammar is worth walking slowly. The subject of the verb is God. The object is *us*. The ground is mercy, stated twice — once by exclusion, once by assertion. And the instrument is *dia loutrou*, through a bath, using the ordinary Greek preposition for the means by which something is done.

No clause in that sentence has the saved person doing anything. The one thing he might have contributed — works of righteousness — is named and excluded.

*Ephesians 5:26.* "…to sanctify her, cleansing her by the washing with water through the word,"

This is the verse that supplies what Titus assumes, and the two should always be read together. Titus says a bath; Ephesians says a bath *of water* with *the word* — the noun is [*rhēma*](/greek/rhema/), the spoken word, the utterance.

Water alone is a bath. Water with the word is this.

Note also who is being washed. Ephesians 5 is about Christ and the church, and the washing is the bridegroom's act toward his bride, aimed at presenting her without spot or wrinkle. The image is nuptial rather than merely hygienic — a bath before a wedding, not a scrubbing after work.

*Acts 22:16.* "And now what are you waiting for? Get up, be baptized, and wash your sins away, calling on His name.’" Ananias to Saul, using the wash-away verb. Whatever Paul later wrote about baptism, this is what was said to him at his own.

*1 Corinthians 6:11.* "And that is what some of you were. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified, in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God." Three aorist passives in a row. Something was done to these people, and they are the objects of every verb.

*Hebrews 10:22.* "let us draw near with a sincere heart in full assurance of faith, having our hearts sprinkled to cleanse us from a guilty conscience and our bodies washed with pure water." Inner and outer named together in one sentence, without embarrassment and without collapsing either into the other.

*Ezekiel 36:25.* "I will also sprinkle clean water on you, and you will be clean. I will cleanse you from all your impurities and all your idols." The promise the New Testament writers had in their ears.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear


*It is not the water that does it.* The Small Catechism's answer is the most quoted sentence in Lutheran sacramental theology and it is often quoted at half length. Baptism is not simply water, but water comprehended in God's command and connected with God's word — and it is not the water that does these things, but the Word of God which is in and with the water, and faith which trusts this Word.

Ephesians 5:26 is the apostolic form of exactly that sentence. A *loutron* of water, with the word. Take the word away and you have a bath; leave the word in and you have what Titus says you have.

This is why the Lutheran position is not the superstition it is sometimes taken for. Nobody in these Confessions claims that water has properties. The claim is about a promise attached to an element by command, which is the same structure as the Supper and the same structure as absolution.

*A bath is somewhere you are put.* The noun's ordinary meaning does quiet work here. You do not perform a bath on yourself in the way you perform a decision. An infant can be bathed. A grown man can be bathed. The bath does not require anything of its occupant except that he be in it.

That is precisely the confessional argument for why baptism can be administered to those who cannot yet speak, and more importantly for why it holds when the one baptized later doubts himself. If the sacrament were chiefly a testimony, its worth would rise and fall with the sincerity of the testifier. Because it is a bath God gives, the question of what the recipient was feeling is not the question on which it turns.

## So What

*And you go on returning to it.* The Catechism asks what baptizing with water signifies and answers with a description of a lifetime — the old Adam drowned by daily contrition and repentance, and a new man daily emerging and rising.

That is where John 13:10 belongs. The one who has bathed does not bathe again; what remains is the day's dirt, and it is washed off by returning to what the bath already did. A Christian is not baptized repeatedly. He lives out of a bath he was put into once, which is a different thing from having filed a transaction and moved on.

The paragraph in Titus keeps this from becoming complacent. It opens by describing its readers as foolish, disobedient, and hating one another, and the writer includes himself. Then the bath. Then heirs, in hope. Nothing in between depends on how the week went, and nothing in it suggests the week does not matter.
