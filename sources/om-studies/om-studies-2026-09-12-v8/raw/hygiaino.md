"Sound doctrine" has become one of the harder phrases in the English theological vocabulary to hear freshly. It arrives pre-loaded. Depending on the reader's history it means *correct*, or it means *ours*, or it means *the thing the angry man on the internet says he has and you do not*.

The Greek behind it is a doctor's word.

*Hygiainō* is what a physician says about a patient who is well. It is the ordinary word for bodily health, and English still carries it: *hygiene* comes from the same root. When the Pastoral Epistles want to describe true teaching, they do not reach for a word meaning *accurate* or *orthodox* or *traditional*. They reach for the word a first-century doctor would use about a man who has recovered.

And having chosen a medical word, the letters commit to it. False teaching in the Pastorals is not merely wrong; it is *diseased*. The false teacher has a morbid craving. His words spread like gangrene. His hearers have itching ears. Somebody's conscience has been cauterized. The metaphor is sustained across all three letters with a consistency that is hard to miss once you are looking for it, and it changes what the phrase is claiming.

Sound doctrine is not primarily a statement about whether the teaching corresponds to reality. It is a statement about what the teaching *does to the people who receive it*.

## The Word

The Greek word is ὑγιαίνω (*hygiainō*), pronounced in the Erasmian convention as *hoo-gee-EYE-no*, with the accent on the third syllable. It is a regular contract verb, and in the Pastorals it appears overwhelmingly as a present active participle — *hygiainousa*, *hygiainontes* — functioning adjectivally: *healthy* teaching, *healthy* words, *healthy* faith.

The word family:

*Hygiēs* (ὑγιής) — the adjective, *healthy, whole, sound*. This is the word used repeatedly in the healing narratives: the man at the pool is made *whole* (John 5:6–15); the withered hand is restored *sound* as the other (Matthew 12:13). It appears once in the Pastorals, at Titus 2:8, of *a sound message that cannot be criticized*.

*Hygieia* (ὑγίεια) — *health* as a noun; the source of English *hygiene*, by way of Latin. Not used in the New Testament, but it is the abstraction standing behind everything the verb does.

The counter-vocabulary is where the metaphor earns its keep, because the Pastorals build a matching set of *illness* words:

*Noseō* (νοσέω) — *to be sick*. At 1 Timothy 6:4 the man who teaches otherwise is described as *sick about* controversies and word-battles. English translations reach for "morbid craving" or "unhealthy interest," and they are trying to render a verb that plainly means *he is ill*.

*Gangraina* (γάγγραινα) — *gangrene*. At 2 Timothy 2:17, the teaching of Hymenaeus and Philetus will spread like gangrene — a medical term of art, describing tissue death that advances through a body. The word survives untranslated into English.

*Kaustēriazō* (καυστηριάζω) — *to cauterize, to brand with a hot iron*. At 1 Timothy 4:2, of consciences seared. Also a medical procedure. The point of cauterizing is that the tissue afterward has no feeling.

*Knēthō* (κνήθω) — *to itch, to tickle*. At 2 Timothy 4:3, of hearers with itching ears who accumulate teachers to suit their desires. A symptom, and notably a symptom in the *audience* rather than the teacher.

That last one is worth pausing over. The medical vocabulary is not applied only to false teachers. It is applied to congregations.

## Range of Meaning

*Physical health.* The base sense, and the dominant one outside the Pastorals. Luke — traditionally a physician, and the New Testament writer who uses the word most outside these letters — has it in its plain medical use: those who are *healthy* have no need of a physician (Luke 5:31); the centurion's servant is found *well* (Luke 7:10); the prodigal is received back *safe and sound* (Luke 15:27).

*General wellbeing, as a greeting.* Third John 2 uses it conventionally, as a letter-writer's wish that the recipient may be well in body as his soul is well. Ordinary Greek correspondence used the word this way constantly; it is the ancient equivalent of "hope this finds you well."

*Teaching that is healthy.* The distinctively Pastoral use, and the one that has entered English as *sound doctrine*. Applied to *didaskalia* (teaching) at 1 Timothy 1:10; 2 Timothy 4:3; Titus 1:9; 2:1. Applied to *logoi* (words) at 1 Timothy 6:3 and 2 Timothy 1:13. Applied to *pistis* (faith) at Titus 1:13 and 2:2.

The spread across those three nouns is the interesting datum. It is not only the content that can be healthy. The *words* can be healthy, and the *faith* can be healthy. The metaphor covers the whole transaction — what is taught, how it is said, and the condition of the person holding it.

## Where You’ll Meet It

*1 Timothy 1:10.* The first appearance, and it comes at the end of a hard list. Paul catalogs the people the law is for — the lawless, the ungodly, murderers, the sexually immoral, kidnappers, liars, perjurers — and closes the list with a general clause: and whatever else is contrary to *sound teaching*. Then, immediately, verse 11: according to the glorious gospel of the blessed God, with which I have been entrusted. The standard against which everything in the list is measured turns out to be the gospel itself. Sound teaching, in its first use, is defined by what it is *according to*.

*1 Timothy 6:3–4.* "If anyone teaches another doctrine and disagrees with the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ and with godly teaching, he is conceited and understands nothing. Instead, he has an unhealthy interest in controversies and disputes about words, out of which come envy, strife, abusive talk, evil suspicions," The clearest place where the two halves of the metaphor stand side by side: *healthy* words on one side, and on the other a man who is *ill* — and specifically ill with an appetite for argument. The diagnosis is not that he holds wrong opinions. It is that he wants the fight.

*2 Timothy 1:13.* "Hold on to the pattern of sound teaching you have heard from me, with the faith and love that are in Christ Jesus." The word here is *hypotypōsis* — a pattern, an outline, a model to be copied. Health has a shape, and the shape was received from somebody.

*2 Timothy 4:3.* "For the time will come when men will not tolerate sound doctrine, but with itching ears they will gather around themselves teachers to suit their own desires." The most quoted of the group, and the most commonly misused. Note where the pathology sits. The people are not deceived by a clever teacher; they *collect* teachers, plural, to match desires they already have. The market is demand-driven. Whatever else this verse is, it is not a verse about somebody else.

*Titus 1:9.* "He must hold firmly to the faithful word as it was taught, so that he can encourage others by sound teaching and refute those who contradict it." Placed at the end of the elder qualifications, and it is the only qualification in the list that concerns competence rather than character. Both halves are named — encourage, and correct — and in that order.

*Titus 2:1–2.* "But as for you, speak the things that are consistent with sound doctrine." Then, immediately, older men are to be *sound in faith, in love, and in endurance*. The same word, twice in two verses, applied first to the teaching and then to the people. That is the argument of the whole metaphor in miniature: healthy teaching is teaching that produces healthy people.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear


*Doctrine is medicine, and the pastor is a physician of souls.* The image is old in the Christian tradition and thoroughly at home in Lutheran pastoral theology — the *Seelsorger*, the curate of souls, whose work is *cura animarum*, the *cure* of souls in the medical sense of that word. Luther's pastoral letters read like a physician's: diagnose accurately, apply the right remedy, and know the difference between the patient who needs the law and the patient who needs the gospel. Nothing else in the Lutheran tradition depends more directly on this word's medical force.

It follows that the right distinction of law and gospel is not a technique for producing correct sermons. It is triage. Giving the law to a terrified conscience and the gospel to a comfortable one is not a stylistic misjudgment; it is a physician handing out the wrong medicine, and the Formula of Concord's article on law and gospel treats it with the seriousness that description implies.

## Where People Get It Wrong

*Pure doctrine is a pastoral concern before it is an intellectual one.* Confessional Lutherans are sometimes accused — not always unfairly — of an interest in doctrinal precision that outruns its usefulness. The medical metaphor supplies both the defense and the limit. The defense: a physician who is casual about which drug he dispenses is not warm-hearted, he is dangerous, and doctrinal indifference in a pastor is of the same kind. The limit: a physician who enjoys the diagnosis more than the recovery has stopped practicing medicine. Both failures are real and the second is the one that quotes this word most often.

Augsburg Confession VII is the relevant restraint. For the true unity of the church it is *enough* to agree concerning the teaching of the gospel and the administration of the sacraments — *satis est*. The word is doing real work. It marks where health has been reached and further intervention becomes its own disease.

## So What

*Health is measured by what the teaching produces.* Titus 2 puts sound teaching and sound people in adjacent verses, and the connection is causal rather than coincidental. The confessional Lutheran instinct is to press on which direction the causality runs. It does not run from the health of the people back to the validity of the teaching — that would make the gospel's truth depend on its results, and it manifestly does not. It runs forward: teaching that is healthy tends, by the Spirit's work through it, to produce people who are.

Which is why the Small Catechism is the shape it is. Its plainness is not a concession to the unlearned. It is a prescription written to be taken.
