John 1:1 introduces the question at the center of this word study:

> “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” (John 1:1, BSB)

The Greek is *En archē ēn ho logos, kai ho logos ēn pros ton theon, kai theos ēn ho logos*. The BSB's final clause, “the Word was God,” makes the deity of the Word explicit. The Word is distinguished from the One He is with, yet is Himself God.

The dispute concerns the last clause, *theos ēn ho logos*. The Jehovah’s Witness interpretation treats the Word as a lesser divine being. The BSB affirms His full deity: “the Word was God.” The grammatical discussion below explains why the absence of the article before *theos* does not make the Word one god among others.

Most of the time, disagreement among English translations is a disagreement about emphasis or tone. Here, the disagreement is about the deity of Christ. *Theos*, the most foundational word in the New Testament's vocabulary of God, sits at the center of the Christian confession — and at the center of the heresies the church has been required to refute. The related entry on [Logos](/greek/logos/) explores the Word made flesh. This entry explores *theos*, the God whose Word the Word is.

This entry is about that word.

## The Word

The Greek word is θεός (*theos*), pronounced in the academic Erasmian convention used here as *theh-OSS*, with the accent on the second syllable. The diphthong *eos* is two distinct vowels: *theh-OSS*, not *theez*.

The word family is enormous. The most important relatives:

*Theotēs* (θεότης) — deity, the abstract quality of being God. Used at Colossians 2:9 (“for in Christ all the fullness of the Deity dwells in bodily form”). See the related entry on [Theotes](/greek/theotes/).

*Theiotēs* (θειότης) — divinity, divine nature. Used at Romans 1:20 ("his eternal power and divine nature"). Often treated as a near-synonym of *theotēs*, though confessional theologians have at times distinguished them carefully.

*Theios* (θεῖος) — divine, the adjectival form. Used at 2 Peter 1:3-4 ("his divine power … partakers of the divine nature") and at Acts 17:29 in Paul's Areopagus speech.

*Theopneustos* (θεόπνευστος) — God-breathed, inspired. The famous word from 2 Timothy 3:16 (“All Scripture is God-breathed”), built from *theos* + the *pneō* root (to breathe). A *hapax legomenon* (a word occurring only once) in the New Testament, but theologically foundational.

*Atheos* (ἄθεος) — without God, godless. Used at Ephesians 2:12, where Paul describes the Gentiles before Christ as "hope and without God in the world." The English word *atheist* derives from this Greek root, though the New Testament sense is closer to *estranged from God* than to *denying God's existence*.

The origin of *theos* is disputed; despite the resemblance, it is not the same etymological line as Latin *deus* or Sanskrit *dyaus*. The etymological background is interesting but does not determine the New Testament meaning. By the time of the New Testament, *theos* was simply the standard Greek word for God or a god — used by pagans for their deities and by Greek-speaking Jews and Christians for the God of Abraham.

The Septuagint background is critical for understanding the New Testament's use of *theos*. When Greek-speaking Jews translated the Hebrew Old Testament into Greek (the project we know as the LXX), they faced a choice about how to render the Hebrew words for God. Hebrew uses two main terms: *Elohim* (the generic word for God or gods) and the personal divine name *YHWH* (the Tetragrammaton, usually transliterated *Yahweh*, sometimes rendered *Jehovah* in older English Bibles). The LXX translators settled on a convention: *theos* would render *Elohim*, and *kyrios* (lord, master) would render *YHWH*.

This convention shapes the entire New Testament. When a New Testament writer says *theos*, he is using the Greek equivalent of *Elohim*. When he says *kyrios*, he is using the Greek equivalent of *YHWH*. The two words name the same God — but with different shades of emphasis. *Theos* names God in His divine being; *kyrios* names God by His personal name. The entry on [Kyrios](/greek/kyrios/) treated this distinction at length.

The implication for *theos* in the New Testament is this: when the New Testament uses *theos*, it is drawing on the LXX's vocabulary for the God of Israel — the *Elohim* of the Old Testament Scriptures. It is not introducing a new God. It is naming the same God, in the same vocabulary, with the added testimony that this God has now spoken finally in His Son.

## Range of Meaning

*Theos* in the New Testament covers a meaningful range:

*The one true God.* The dominant New Testament usage. *Theos* names the God of Israel, the Father of Jesus Christ, the Creator and Redeemer. Hundreds of occurrences. This is the meaning a reader of the New Testament should assume by default.

*A pagan god.* Used negatively or polemically. Acts 14:11 — the people of Lystra cry out that “The gods have come down to us in human form” 1 Corinthians 8:5 — “For even if there are so-called gods, whether in heaven or on earth (as there are many so-called gods and lords),” The New Testament uses the word for false gods only to expose their falsity, never to legitimate them.

*Divine beings or angels.* Rare and contested. Some passages in the LXX use *theoi* (plural) for what the Hebrew calls *elohim* — beings in the divine council, possibly angels. The New Testament does not develop this usage in any substantial way.

*Christ.* This is the theologically loaded usage that drives much of this entry. *Theos* is applied directly to Jesus Christ in several New Testament passages — John 1:1, John 1:18, John 20:28, Romans 9:5, Titus 2:13, 2 Peter 1:1, Hebrews 1:8. Seven direct attributions in passages that are not theologically disputable in their meaning, even if some are disputable in their punctuation. The pattern is too consistent to dismiss.

*The Holy Spirit.* By implication. Acts 5:3-4 — Peter says to Ananias, “You have not lied to men, but to God!” after first identifying the lie as a lie “to the Holy Spirit” (Acts 5:3, BSB) and withhold some of the proceeds from the land’” The two statements equate. The Spirit is *theos*.

*The belly.* Philippians 3:19 — Paul's ironic description of the enemies of the cross: “Their end is destruction, their god is their belly, and their glory is in their shame. Their minds are set on earthly things.” Clearly satirical; the New Testament will sometimes use the word ironically to expose disordered worship.

*The “god of this age.”* 2 Corinthians 4:4 — Paul describes Satan as “the god of this age” who blinds the minds of unbelievers. who blinds the minds of unbelievers. Used by analogy, not by ontology. Satan exercises rule over the unregenerate world, but he is not *theos* in the same sense the Father is.

The dominant usage — by far — is the first one. When the New Testament says *theos* without qualification, it means the one true God of Israel, the Father of Jesus Christ.

## Where You’ll Meet It

*John 1:1.* “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” The foundational New Testament text for understanding *theos* in its relation to the Word. The Greek deserves close attention: *en archē ēn ho logos, kai ho logos ēn pros ton theon, kai theos ēn ho logos.*

The three-clause structure is precise. First clause: *en archē ēn ho logos* — "in the beginning was the Word." Second clause: *kai ho logos ēn pros ton theon* — "and the Word was with the God" (note the article — *ton theon*, "the God," referring to the Father specifically). Third clause: *kai theos ēn ho logos* — “the Word was God” (no article — *theos*, predicate nominative). The grammar carries the doctrine. The Word is distinguished from the Father (Clause 2: the Word was *with* the Father). The Word is identified as God (Clause 3: the Word *was* God). Trinitarian doctrine is not imposed on the verse by later church councils; it is right there in the Greek grammar.

*John 20:28.* “Thomas replied, ‘My Lord and my God.’” In Greek: *Ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou.* This is the first New Testament instance of a believer addressing Jesus directly as *theos*. Notice the construction: both nouns carry the article and the personal pronoun. Thomas is not making an abstract theological statement; he is making a personal confession. *My* Lord and *my* God. Jesus accepts the address without correction — and the contrast with Revelation 22:9, where the angel refuses worship, is important. Jesus accepts what an angel must refuse. The text is one of the strongest in the New Testament for the deity of Christ.

*Romans 9:5.* "Theirs are the patriarchs, and from them proceeds the human descent of Christ, who is God over all, forever worthy of praise! Amen." The verse is famously disputed at the level of punctuation: should "who is God over all" refer back to Christ, or should the sentence end after “Christ,” followed by a new doxology to the Father? The Greek favors the first reading on grammatical and stylistic grounds — Paul's word order, the natural flow of the participial phrase, and the comparative weight of doxologies elsewhere in the Pauline corpus. Most modern translations agree. Paul calls Christ *theos*.

*1 Corinthians 8:6.* "yet for us there is but one God, the Father, from whom all things came and for whom we exist. And there is but one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom all things came and through whom we exist." This is Paul's Christianized version of the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4 — “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is One.”). Paul takes the Hebrew Shema's two key terms — *Yahweh* (= *kyrios* in LXX) and *Elohim* (= *theos*) — and distributes them across Father and Son: *theos* belongs to the Father, *kyrios* to the Son. But the monotheistic confession of the Shema remains intact: one God, one Lord, and the God and the Lord are the same God. Paul is doing in two verses what the church would take centuries to articulate in creeds.

*Hebrews 1:8.* “but about the Son He says: ‘Your throne, O God, endures forever and ever, and justice is the scepter of Your kingdom’” The author of Hebrews quotes Psalm 45:6 (LXX 44:7) and identifies the addressee as the Son. In other words: the Father addresses the Son as *theos*. The Old Testament's *Elohim* of the Psalm becomes, in the New Testament's reading, an address from the First Person of the Trinity to the Second. The text would not work this way if the Son were not fully *theos*. The verse is one of the cornerstones of the New Testament's case for the deity of Christ.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Three emphases bear repeating, because the New Testament’s use of *theos* puts each one beyond dispute.

*One God.* The Christian confession is monotheistic. There is one God, not many. Augsburg Confession I 1 opens by confessing, in accord with Nicaea, the one divine essence that is truly God. Confessional Lutherans share this confession with the historic church catholic, against ancient polytheism and modern syncretism alike. The New Testament's *theos* — when it refers to the true God — is always this one God.

*Three persons of the one God.* The same New Testament that confesses one *theos* applies the name to Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The Trinity is not a later doctrinal accretion imposed on the biblical text; it is the necessary reading of the New Testament's actual grammar. The Father is *theos* (every page of the New Testament). The Son is *theos* (John 1:1, John 20:28, Romans 9:5, Titus 2:13, 2 Peter 1:1, Hebrews 1:8). The Spirit is *theos* (Acts 5:3-4 by direct implication). One *theos*, three persons. Augsburg Confession I 2 continues by confessing three coeternal persons of the same essence and power: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

*The God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob is the Father of Jesus Christ.* The same God who spoke to the patriarchs and gave the Law at Sinai is the God who has now spoken in His Son (Hebrews 1:1-2). This is the necessary implication of the New Testament's vocabulary: when New Testament writers say *theos*, they are using the LXX's word for the *Elohim* of the Old Testament. There is not a new God in the New Testament; there is the same God, more fully revealed. Marcionism — the second-century heresy that pitted the God of the Old Testament against the God of the New — has died many deaths over the centuries but remains tempting in some popular American Christianity. The lexicon refutes it: *theos* in the New Testament is *Elohim* in the Old.

The pastoral payoff is straightforward. When you confess "I believe in God," the Christian content of that confession is the Trinity. Not a generic monotheism. Not a vague spiritual presence. Not a personal philosophy. The Christian's God is the God who has acted in the history of Israel, who has spoken in Jesus Christ, who continues to act through His Spirit. When the apostles write *theos*, they mean this God and no other. When confessional Lutherans confess *theos*, we mean the same.

## Where People Get It Wrong

The misreadings of *theos* are some of the oldest in the history of the church. Each has a modern form.

*The Jehovah’s Witness reading of John 1:1.* The New World Translation, the Bible produced by the Watch Tower Bible and Tract Society, renders John 1:1c as “the Word was a god.” The argument hangs on the absence of the article (*theos* in this clause, without the article *ho*). The Watch Tower has trained its publishers to point to this absence as evidence that the Word is not *the* God but only *a* god — a created being, a lesser divine entity, but not Yahweh.

The argument is grammatically untenable. In Greek, predicate nominatives preceding the verb routinely lack the article without becoming indefinite. This is a well-established feature of Greek syntax, sometimes called Colwell’s Rule.[^jeg-2-27] The same construction appears at John 4:24 (“God is spirit” — *pneuma ho theos*, anarthrous predicate nominative), at John 1:14 (“the Word became flesh” — anarthrous *sarx*), and dozens of other places where no one would suggest the predicate noun is indefinite. The grammar requires the third clause of John 1:1 to read either “the Word was God” or “the Word was divine” (qualitative emphasis). It cannot grammatically read “the Word was a god.”

*Arianism, ancient and modern.* The fourth-century heresy that taught the Son was a created being — the first and highest of God’s creatures, but a creature nonetheless. The Council of Nicaea (AD 325) condemned this view, asserting that the Son is *homoousios* (of the same substance) with the Father. Modern Arianism survives in various forms — Jehovah’s Witnesses, some Unitarian-Universalist traditions, occasional academic theologians. All of them must explain away the New Testament’s direct application of *theos* to the Son. None of them does so without strain.

*Modalism, ancient and modern.* The opposite error: not denying the deity of the Son but denying the distinction of persons. On a modalist reading, “Father, Son, and Spirit” are three masks or modes the one God wears at different times. The same God plays different roles in the divine drama. The historic church condemned this view in the third century (against Sabellius); the Athanasian Creed names the error explicitly. Modalism appears today in Oneness Pentecostalism and in some popular preaching that conflates the persons of the Trinity. The New Testament’s use of *theos* will not bear it: in John 1:1 the Word is *with* the Father (clear distinction of persons) and *is* God (clear unity of essence). One or the other modalism denies; both the New Testament confesses.

*Henotheism.* The belief that there is one supreme God among many lesser gods. Common in ancient Near Eastern religion; surviving in popular American spirituality more often than people realize. Mormon theology teaches a form of it; so does the “God among gods” theology of various New Age writers. The New Testament’s *theos* — heir of the Hebrew Shema’s *Yahweh is one* — admits no rival. *For us there is one God* (1 Corinthians 8:6), and the so-called gods of the nations are not gods at all (1 Corinthians 8:4-6).

*Generic theism.* The vague belief in “God” that names no particular God. The most common American religious posture. When a person says “I believe in God” without specifying which God or what kind of God, the confession is not yet Christian. The New Testament does not allow this generic confession; it always specifies. *Theos* in the New Testament is *the God who raised Jesus from the dead* (Romans 10:9), *the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ* (2 Corinthians 1:3), *the only true God* and *Jesus Christ whom he has sent* (John 17:3). To confess *theos* in the New Testament sense is to confess the Trinity.

*The* *“**God of the philosophers**”* *temptation.* Drawing the Christian doctrine of God primarily from philosophical reasoning (the unmoved mover, the necessary being, the cosmic intelligence) rather than from the biblical narrative. The danger is real even within Christian theology: a God reasoned to without the gospel will not save anyone, because the God who saves is known only through Christ. Confessional Lutherans have historically been wary of natural theology that proceeds far without the revealed Word. Luther’s *theologia crucis* makes the point with characteristic sharpness: God is known truly only in the cross of Christ, not in the abstractions of unaided reason. The *theos* of the New Testament is not the philosopher’s God; He is the God of the cross.

## So What

The Sunday school teacher angle is direct: when you teach about God in your adult class, what God are you teaching about? American religious culture defaults toward a “generic theism” — God as a vague benevolent presence, a higher power — and even regular churchgoers carry that default into their hearing of Scripture. When you read “God so loved the world” (John 3:16, BSB), the word “God” in your students’ ears may default to that generic deity, not to the Father of Jesus Christ who spoke through the prophets and acted in history.

The teaching application is straightforward. When you meet the word “God” in a text, ask: *which person of the Trinity does this passage name, and what is His relationship to the others?* The Greek often helps — *theos* with the article (*ho theos*) frequently names the Father specifically, while anarthrous *theos* often carries qualitative emphasis (“divine in nature”). And the Old Testament background is essential: *theos* translates *Elohim*, and the God of Israel is the same God now revealed as the Father of Jesus Christ.

For Bible study leaders, the application extends to the doctrine of God. The first article of the Apostles’ Creed — “I believe in God, the Father almighty” — is not a generic statement about a higher power but a specific confession of the Trinity’s first person, in the company of the Son and the Spirit. The Creed is structurally Trinitarian even where the word *Trinity* never appears.

In daily life, the implication is pastoral. When a neighbor says “I believe in God,” the Christian’s response is not to dismiss it but to ask what is meant. The deity the New Testament confesses has a name and a face: the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has acted in history and spoken in His Son. He is not far from any of us (Acts 17:27) — but neither is He vague. The witness is to name Him rightly, and to invite the neighbor to know Him truly.

The related entry on [Logos](/greek/logos/) explores the Word; this entry explores *theos*, the God whose Word it is. These two are not in competition but in communion: the Word is God (John 1:1), and God speaks His Word (Hebrews 1:1–2). In His Word made flesh, God has spoken Himself to us.

[^jeg-2-27]: Colwell’s Rule, named for E. C. Colwell’s 1933 article in the Journal of Biblical Literature, observes that “definite predicate nouns which precede the verb usually lack the article” (E. C. Colwell, “A Definite Rule for the Use of the Article in the Greek New Testament,” Journal of Biblical Literature 52 [1933]: 12-21). The rule has been refined and qualified since Colwell, most importantly by Philip Harner (“Qualitative Anarthrous Predicate Nouns: Mark 15:39 and John 1:1,” Journal of Biblical Literature 92 [1973]: 75-87), who argued that the anarthrous predicate noun in John 1:1c is best read as qualitative (“the Word was divine in nature”) rather than as either definite or indefinite. Either reading establishes the deity of the Word; the JW indefinite reading is not among the grammatically defensible options.
