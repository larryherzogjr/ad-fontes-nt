Most of us hear freedom as the absence of any master. If nobody has authority over the final decision, we think we are free. Paul's account of sin asks a different question: whose claim already governs the life you are living?

In Romans 6, release from sin does not leave a person suspended between two equal options. It brings that person into belonging to God.

## The Word

**δοῦλος** (*doulos*) means *slave* or *bondservant*. In the ancient social setting it named someone under a master's authority, not simply an employee hired for a task. The word's force includes a lack of independence that the English word *servant* can obscure.

That does not mean every translator who writes *servant* is smuggling in a doctrine of free will. English conventions differ, and context can still convey the relationship. The important question is whether we hear the dependence and belonging that Paul's argument requires.

## Range of Meaning

The New Testament uses the word for actual slaves and for a person's relation to Christ or sin. Those uses must be distinguished. Paul can address an enslaved Christian's social circumstances and, in the same passage, describe a legally free Christian as Christ's slave.

The spiritual image does not make human ownership of another person a model of Christian goodness. First Corinthians 7:21–23 recognizes the opportunity for freedom and tells Christians not to become slaves of human beings. Christ's claim is not permission for a human master to become absolute.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Romans 6:16–22 contrasts slavery to sin with slavery to righteousness and God. The change is a deliverance for which Paul gives thanks:

> “But now that you have been set free from sin and have become slaves to God, the fruit you reap leads to holiness, and the outcome is eternal life.” (Romans 6:22, BSB)

In John 8:34–36, Jesus names the bondage of those who practice sin and identifies the Son as the one who sets them free. The remedy comes from the liberator, not from the slave announcing independence.

First Corinthians 7:22 brings Christ's claim into ordinary social life. Philippians 1:1 uses *douloi*, the plural, when Paul and Timothy identify themselves as slaves of Christ Jesus. Belonging to Christ is central to their calling.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The sinner's will is not neutral toward God. Sin is more than a set of isolated mistakes a detached chooser can stop making whenever he likes. It is a bondage from which Christ releases us.

This does not abolish ordinary human choosing. People make decisions about work, meals, civic responsibilities, and care for their neighbors. The question is whether those natural capacities can free the sinner from unbelief and establish righteousness before God. They cannot do what belongs to Christ's saving work.

After release, willing and acting remain real. Paul tells those freed from sin to present themselves in service to righteousness. Grace does not cancel the Christian life; it creates its new direction.

## Where People Get It Wrong

If *doulos* becomes merely a temporary helper, the picture can suggest that the sinner is free to resign from sin without being rescued. Paul instead describes a transfer of belonging. The old bondage is broken by a gift received, not by an autonomous will standing above both masters.

But the image can also be pushed too far, as though Christians had no renewed will or their actions no longer mattered. Paul explicitly calls them to serve. The Lord frees people for a life of willing obedience, not for a vacancy where human life used to be.

## So What

When sin claims to be your only possible master, hear the Son who sets captives free. When independence sounds like salvation, remember that belonging to Christ is life.

The companion entry [Nekros](/greek/nekros/) gives Paul's other severe diagnosis. Together the images direct us to the Savior who does what the captive and the dead cannot do for themselves.
