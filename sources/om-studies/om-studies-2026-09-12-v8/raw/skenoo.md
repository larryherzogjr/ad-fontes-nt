John does not introduce Jesus as a general feeling that God is near. The Word becomes flesh and lives among us. The language of dwelling recalls the tent where God's glory was present among Israel, now bringing that history into the confession of the incarnate Son.

## The Word

**σκηνόω** (*skēnoō*) means *to dwell*, with the image of pitching a tent or tabernacling. It is related to **σκηνή** (*skēnē*), a tent or dwelling.

John 1:14 uses the verb for the Word's residence among us. The tent image helps us hear a biblical connection; it does not mean Jesus' humanity was a temporary covering that he later discarded.

## Range of Meaning

Exodus 40:34–38 describes the glory of the Lord filling the tabernacle. Israel's life in the wilderness was accompanied by this appointed place of God's presence. John joins the Word's dwelling with the glory the witnesses saw.

Revelation 7:15 uses *skēnoō* for the protection of the One seated on the throne; the BSB renders it *will spread His tabernacle over them*. Revelation 21:3 uses it when announcing God's dwelling with His people in the new creation.

The contexts develop the image differently. Dwelling, shelter, and God's presence belong together, but no single English rendering reproduces every association in each passage.

## Where You’ll Meet It

John writes:

> “The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us. We have seen His glory, the glory of the one and only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth.” (John 1:14, BSB)

The sentence continues with the glory of the Son who comes from the Father, full of grace and truth. John 1:18 then directs us to the Son who makes the unseen God known.

Revelation 21:3–4 looks toward the completion of life with God: his people dwell with him, and death, mourning, and pain are removed. The promise is not simply that human beings learn to feel less distant from him. He acts to make all things new.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The incarnation gives faith a definite Savior. The eternal Son truly became human; God has made himself known through this person and his saving work. We do not need to find a truer account of God's mercy behind Jesus.

That teaches a fitting posture toward the sacraments as well. Christ directs us to particular gifts and attaches particular promises to them. We receive Baptism and the Supper because he instituted them, not because we have derived their effectiveness from a tent metaphor.

The analogy must keep that distinction. Water, bread, and wine do not become additional incarnations. The Son's unique incarnation and his sacramental gifts are related through his own Word and promise.

## Where People Get It Wrong

A familiar translation such as *dwelt among us* can be read so generally that the tabernacle connection disappears. Attending to the verb and the accompanying reference to glory restores that biblical setting.

The opposite mistake confines God to a location as though he ceased to be present elsewhere. God's universal presence and his promised presence for our salvation are not rivals. The question for a troubled conscience is where he has promised to give his mercy.

## So What

You need not search for God by measuring whether a place or moment feels sufficiently sacred. Attend to the Son who has made the Father known and to the gifts he has commanded his church to give.

[Naos](/greek/naos/) follows another biblical image for God's dwelling. Both entries direct attention to his gracious action among his people, rather than to our success in making him seem near.
