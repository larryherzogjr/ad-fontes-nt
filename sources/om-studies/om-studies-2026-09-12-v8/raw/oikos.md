Acts repeatedly brings the gospel into households. Lydia is baptized with her household; the jailer's household hears the Word and receives Baptism. These accounts show the gospel entering shared domestic life, while leaving some questions about the households' members unanswered.

## The Word

**οἶκος** (*oikos*) means *house* or *household*. It can name a dwelling or the people who belong to a domestic community. A household in the ancient world could include dependents and servants as well as relatives.

The related word **οἰκία** (*oikia*) also has house and household uses. Paul uses that word, rather than *oikos*, for the household of Stephanas in 1 Corinthians 1:16. The English expression can therefore represent more than one Greek noun.

## Range of Meaning

Context determines whether a building or its people are in view. In the household baptism accounts, the concern is the people, not a religious action performed on a structure.

First Timothy 3:15 extends the image to the church as God's household. Belonging to this household brings an ordered common life under God's care. The image does not make every feature of an ancient domestic arrangement a command for church government.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul and Silas tell the jailer:

> “They replied, ‘Believe in the Lord Jesus and you will be saved, you and your household.’” (Acts 16:31, BSB)

The next verse says they speak the word of the Lord to everyone in his house. Baptism follows that night. The promise reaches the household through the ministry of the Word; the account does not portray the jailer's faith as a substitute for everyone else's relation to Christ.

Acts 16:15 reports Lydia's baptism and that of her household without listing their ages. Acts 18:8 says Crispus believes in the Lord with his whole household, then describes many Corinthians hearing, believing, and being baptized.

These details should all be heard. Some accounts explicitly mention household hearing and belief; others give fewer details. None supplies a complete register of the people present.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Household Baptism fits the church's confession that God's promise is given within the ordinary relationships of human life. Children are not outsiders to whom grace must remain unavailable until they acquire an adult capacity for explanation.

But *oikos* does not mean *a household necessarily containing infants*. These verses do not identify the age of every person baptized. They should not be asked to prove by themselves what they leave unstated.

The Lutheran case for infant Baptism rests on the wider scriptural teaching about Baptism, God's promise, and faith as his gift. It does not depend on inserting an infant into a narrative that does not explicitly mention one. Nor does it treat Baptism as salvation through the borrowed faith of a household head.

The household accounts also resist reducing Christian life to isolated decisions. The Word is spoken where people live together, and its gifts reshape their shared life.

## So What

Let the gospel be heard in your household: in prayer, instruction, forgiveness, and the congregation's ministry. These are fitting forms of care for the people entrusted to you.

You cannot believe on another person's behalf or control the outcome of every conversation. You can bring your family to the gifts Christ has appointed and speak his promise faithfully. The Savior of your household remains Jesus.
