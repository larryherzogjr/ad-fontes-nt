A cross raises its victim above the ground. A throne raises a ruler to honor. John's Gospel places a startling connection between those images in Jesus' own speech: his being lifted up in death will reveal the saving work for which he came.

## The Word

**ὑψόω** (*hypsoō*) means *to lift up* or *to exalt*. It can describe physical elevation or the bestowal of honor. John uses the verb in sayings whose physical reference to crucifixion carries a larger significance.

The double meaning belongs to the passages, not to a rule that every occurrence must refer to both a cross and a throne.

## Range of Meaning

In John 3:14, Jesus compares the lifting up of the Son of Man with Moses' lifting up of the serpent in the wilderness. In 8:28, his being lifted up is connected with the revelation of his identity. In 12:32–33, the evangelist explicitly identifies the kind of death Jesus means.

Acts uses the verb for exaltation at God's right hand (2:33; 5:31). Philippians 2:9 uses a related, stronger compound, **ὑπερυψόω** (*hyperypsoō*), rendered *highly exalted*. It should not be counted as an occurrence of the simple verb merely because the theological subjects overlap.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Jesus says:

> “‘And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw everyone to Myself.’” (John 12:32, BSB)

John immediately explains that Jesus is indicating how he will die. The surrounding passage speaks of his approaching hour and the glorifying of the Father's name. The cross is the place of shame where the Son accomplishes the work that reveals God's saving glory.

Acts 2:32–36 announces the resurrection and exaltation of this same crucified Jesus. From the Father's right hand he pours out the promised Spirit. The one publicly rejected is proclaimed Lord and Christ.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

John's presentation of glory at the cross must be heard alongside the distinction between Christ's humiliation and exaltation. Crucifixion remains the depth of his suffering and obedience. Calling it the revelation of saving glory does not make it the full exercise of majesty that belongs to his exalted state.

The distinction matters because the suffering was real. Christ did not merely appear vulnerable while untouched by death. It also matters because resurrection is a real victory, not simply a later recognition that the cross was meaningful.

[Tapeinoō](/greek/tapeinoo/) follows the humiliation described in Philippians. Together the passages direct us to the one Lord who truly died and now lives and reigns. We do not need to flatten their different emphases to confess the whole of his work.

## So What

God's saving glory can be hidden beneath an appearance that human judgment finds contemptible. That does not make every humiliating experience a secret triumph. It directs faith to this particular crucified Lord and his promise.

The Jesus lifted up on the cross is the Jesus exalted at the Father's right hand. His authority belongs to the one who gave himself for sinners. You may approach him with the confidence that his reign does not cancel his mercy.
