You may know that God promises resurrection and still wonder what that promise has to do with the ordinary weakness of this week. The body still aches. Prayer still wanders. The future sounds certain in church and remote on Monday.

Paul answers with an image drawn from a transaction already begun. God has not merely spoken about a distant inheritance. He has given his Spirit now.

## The Word

**ἀρραβών** (*arrabōn*) means *pledge*, *down payment*, or *first installment*. In a transaction, an initial payment or pledge gives present assurance concerning what remains to be completed.

Paul applies the word to the Holy Spirit. The person who gives is God; what he gives is his Spirit; the promised completion is our inheritance and redemption. The image directs attention to the giver's commitment.

## Range of Meaning

The word's commercial background helps us understand the relationship between present possession and future completion. Something has really been given, although the whole transaction is not yet complete.

The metaphor has limits. The Spirit is not a fraction of God, and resurrection is not a commodity we purchase. Nor does a business word by itself explain every question about perseverance. Paul takes the relationship of pledge and fulfillment and uses it to describe God's gracious action toward his people.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Second Corinthians 1:22 joins God's sealing with his gift of the Spirit in our hearts. Ephesians 1:13–14 likewise connects the Spirit's seal with the inheritance still to come. The companion entry [Sphragizō](/greek/sphragizo/) follows that sealing language.

Second Corinthians 5 gives the future a bodily shape. Paul describes our present groaning and our longing for mortality to be swallowed up by life. Then he writes:

> “And it is God who has prepared us for this very purpose and has given us the Spirit as a pledge of what is to come.” (2 Corinthians 5:5, BSB)

The promise concerns more than feeling better while the body dies. God is preparing his people for life beyond death, and his Spirit is already given to them in the midst of their groaning.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

God gives the Spirit through the gospel and his means of grace. In Acts 2:38–39, the apostolic preaching connects Baptism and forgiveness with the gift of the Holy Spirit. The Christian can therefore return to God's promise in Baptism instead of searching for a private guarantee apart from his Word.

The three *arrabōn* passages do not themselves spell out a baptismal rite. They name the Spirit as God's present gift. Reading them with the baptismal promises gives their assurance a concrete pastoral address: the God who promises the inheritance has already acted toward you.

The giver is faithful. Our confidence rests in him, and the Spirit keeps directing faith to Christ. The deposit is not our strength of conviction offered to God as evidence that we deserve the rest.

## Where People Get It Wrong

The Spirit really does bring joy, comfort, and hope. The mistake is identifying his pledge with a measurable intensity of feeling. If that were the guarantee, a flat or fearful week would seem to cancel God's commitment.

Paul locates the pledge in the Spirit himself. In 2 Corinthians 5, people who have received that pledge still groan. Present weakness does not show that the promise was never theirs.

Nor should the image become permission to abandon faith. It comforts Christians by pointing them to God's faithfulness; it does not make his Word and gifts unnecessary after an initial experience.

## So What

Bring your weak faith to the promise rather than postponing the promise until your faith feels strong. Hear Christ's forgiveness. Remember what God has given in Baptism.

Your future is not resting on your ability to feel resurrected today. The Spirit is with God's people while they wait. As [firstfruits](/greek/aparche/) points toward the harvest, his present gift directs you toward the life God has promised to complete.
