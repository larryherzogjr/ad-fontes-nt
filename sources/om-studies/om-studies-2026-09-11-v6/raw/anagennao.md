Peter writes about new birth to Christians who know suffering. Their hope is alive because Jesus is alive. Its foundation reaches beyond the conditions that make their present life painful.

## The Word

**ἀναγεννάω** (*anagennaō*) means *to beget anew* or *give new birth*. It is a verb occurring in 1 Peter 1:3 and 1:23. Both passages identify a life whose source is God's action rather than human self-improvement.

In verse 3, God is described as the one who has given new birth. In verse 23, the readers are described as having been born anew. The change in grammatical construction does not change the giver of the life.

## Range of Meaning

The two occurrences provide complementary contexts. First Peter 1:3 connects new birth with God's great mercy, a living hope, and Jesus Christ's resurrection. Verse 23 connects it with imperishable seed and the living, enduring Word of God.

We should not make those descriptions compete. The risen Christ is the ground of hope; the Word through which he is proclaimed is the means named in the later passage. Peter is not offering two unrelated accounts of regeneration.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Peter says that the readers have been born anew:

> “For you have been born again, not of perishable seed, but of imperishable, through the living and enduring word of God.” (1 Peter 1:23, BSB)

The following quotation contrasts perishing human life with the Lord's enduring Word. Peter then identifies that Word with the good news proclaimed to his hearers. The seed is not a hidden divine fragment inside people waiting to be discovered.

Earlier in the chapter, suffering tests faith while believers rejoice in a Savior they have not seen. New birth therefore does not function as a promise that earthly circumstances will become comfortable. It establishes a hope that suffering cannot make obsolete.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Augsburg Confession V identifies the gospel and sacraments as the means through which the Holy Spirit gives faith. Peter's statement directs us toward that external Word. The Spirit's work is personal and inward in its effects without needing to bypass words that can be spoken and heard.

This also guards the relation between preaching and Baptism. Confessing new birth through the Word does not exclude Baptism, since Baptism is water joined to God's command and promise. Conversely, honoring Baptism does not make the preached gospel powerless to create faith.

The source of life remains God's mercy through Christ. We should not turn Peter's seed image into a biological explanation of grace or imagine that the baptized person now possesses an indestructible spiritual substance that makes unbelief impossible.

## Where People Get It Wrong

New birth can be reduced to a fresh attitude. Peter speaks of God's action and an imperishable hope, not merely a more optimistic outlook.

Another error uses the gift to excuse lovelessness. Verse 22 calls for sincere love of fellow believers immediately before the statement about new birth. The life given by the Word is to take shape in the community that hears it.

## So What

When your circumstances make hope feel fragile, return to what does not depend on their improvement: Christ is risen, and his gospel endures.
