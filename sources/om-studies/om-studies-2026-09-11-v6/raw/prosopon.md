A familiar word can acquire a precise use when the church needs to answer a precise question. *Face* and *person* belong to ordinary speech. In the confession of the Trinity, *person* helps distinguish Father, Son, and Holy Spirit without dividing the one God into three gods.

## The Word

**πρόσωπον** (*prosōpon*) means *face*, *countenance*, or, in suitable contexts, *person*. It can also refer to someone's presence. English translations vary because ordinary expressions about faces do more than describe facial features.

The later theological use of *person* should not be read backward into every occurrence of this Greek word.

## Range of Meaning

In 2 Corinthians 4:6, the face of Christ is the place where God's glory is known. In 2 Corinthians 1:11, the plural refers to the many people involved in thanksgiving. Galatians 2:6 uses an expression about accepting a person's face to deny partiality. Hebrews 9:24 speaks of Christ appearing in God's presence for us.

Greek also used *prosōpon* for a mask. That fact does not make the church's confession of three persons a confession of three masks. A word's available senses are not all present whenever it is used, and theology requires us to attend to the sense actually confessed.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul describes God shining in our hearts to give:

> “For God, who said, ‘Let light shine out of darkness,’ made His light shine in our hearts to give us the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.” (2 Corinthians 4:6, BSB)

The phrase directs us to Christ as the revelation of God. It is not yet a dictionary definition of the technical term *person* used in later Trinitarian debates.

Hebrews 9:24 gives another pastoral setting. Christ appears before God on our behalf. The expression concerns his presence and intercession, rather than a description of God's physical face.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Athanasian Creed confesses one God while distinguishing the persons: the Father is not the Son, and the Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son. Their divine being is one, and their glory is equal (3–6). The distinctions are real; they are not successive roles played by one person.

The church's terminology also distinguishes the question of person from the question of nature. In speaking of Christ, we confess one person in two natures. His humanity does not introduce a second personal subject alongside the eternal Son. See [Physis](/greek/physis/).

[Hypostasis](/greek/hypostasis/) likewise has a history of biblical and later theological uses. These terms became useful through careful definition and shared confession, not because each biblical occurrence already carried the entire later vocabulary.

This precision serves worship and consolation. The Son who becomes human is truly distinct from the Father who sends him, and truly shares the Father's deity. We do not have to choose between those truths.

## So What

When you hear of Christ praying to the Father or appearing before him for you, you are not listening to a divine performance with only one participant. The Son truly acts on your behalf before the Father.

And when you hear God's glory made known in Christ's face, you need not suspect a lesser substitute. The one who makes the Father known is his own Son, true God and your Savior.
