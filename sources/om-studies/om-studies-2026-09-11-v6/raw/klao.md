Breaking bread is an ordinary action with a prominent place in the church's account of the Lord's Supper. The action matters, but identifying a meal requires more than finding the verb. We must attend to who is eating, what is said, and how the passage describes their sharing.

## The Word

**κλάω** (*klaō*) means *to break*. In the passages considered here, it describes breaking bread. The related noun **κλάσις** (*klasis*) names the breaking itself.

Luke 24:30 uses the verb when Jesus breaks bread at Emmaus; verse 35 uses the noun as the disciples describe how they recognized him. Acts 2:42 likewise uses the noun in its account of the church's common life.

## Range of Meaning

Breaking bread can belong to an ordinary meal. The expression does not carry a complete doctrine of the Supper every time it appears. Acts 2:46 describes believers sharing food in their homes with gladness, while Acts 2:42 places the breaking of bread alongside the apostles' teaching, fellowship, and prayers.

Acts 20:7 describes a gathering on the first day of the week to break bread. Such settings explain why Christians hear an association with the Supper. They also require interpretation of the whole account, rather than a rule that turns the expression into an unvarying technical label.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul asks:

> “Is not the cup of blessing that we bless a participation in the blood of Christ? And is not the bread that we break a participation in the body of Christ?” (1 Corinthians 10:16, BSB)

Here the context is explicit. Paul joins the bread to participation in Christ's body and the cup to participation in his blood. He then connects the one bread with the many who are one body.

First Corinthians 11:23–26 recounts the institution of this meal. Jesus takes bread, gives thanks, breaks it, and speaks the words that identify his gift. The action is received together with his declaration and command.

At Emmaus, the risen Jesus is recognized in the breaking of the bread after opening the Scriptures to the disciples. The narrative directs attention to the Lord who makes himself known. The verb alone does not settle every question about that meal's relation to later celebrations of the Supper.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Christ gives more than an occasion for remembering absent friends or expressing human solidarity. In the Supper he gives his body and blood according to his words. Paul's language of sharing supports this concrete confession of the gift.

The church's unity is therefore received before it is displayed. The many come to the one Lord and his gift; they do not create him by assembling or agreeing with one another.

Breaking the bread serves its distribution. We need not develop a theory in which a particular manner of breaking makes the sacrament effective or reenacts the physical details of the crucifixion. Christ's words and institution provide its certainty.

## So What

When you receive the Supper, attend to what Christ says he gives. The visible actions serve that promise, directing you toward his body and blood given for you.

Then recognize the neighbors who receive beside you as people sustained by the same mercy. Their needs and burdens belong within the life of a congregation fed by one Lord. The meal's gift has consequences for how its recipients live together.
