A worship service may leave someone encouraged, unsettled, or simply tired. Those reactions matter, but they do not by themselves tell us whether the church has been built up. Paul gives a more definite criterion: can the congregation understand the message and receive the truth it needs?

## The Word

**οἰκοδομή** (*oikodomē*) means *building* or *building up*. When English Bibles use *edification*, the building image is still there. Something is being strengthened so that it can stand and grow.

In Paul's teaching about worship, the building is the congregation. The related verb **οἰκοδομέω** (*oikodomeō*) describes the act of building it up. He uses both noun and verb throughout 1 Corinthians 14.

## Range of Meaning

Ephesians 4:12 and 4:16 describe the building up of Christ's body. Christ supplies the gifts through which the members grow together in truth and love. Maturity includes resisting deceptive teaching and serving one another.

Romans 14:19 and 15:2 apply the image to relationships among Christians. The strong must consider the good of neighbors whose consciences are weak. Building up cannot mean displaying one's freedom without regard for what it does to another person.

The word therefore reaches beyond the delivery of information. Understanding serves faith and love within a body whose members need one another.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul gives this direction for the congregation's gathering:

> “What then shall we say, brothers? When you come together, everyone has a psalm or a teaching, a revelation, a tongue, or an interpretation. All of these must be done to build up the church.” (1 Corinthians 14:26, BSB)

Earlier in the chapter, he explains why understandable speech matters. Without interpretation, someone else cannot understand what is said or join the thanksgiving with an Amen. The speaker may be giving thanks, but the neighbor is not being strengthened.

Paul would rather speak five intelligible words to instruct others than ten thousand words in a tongue they cannot understand (14:19). The comparison puts the neighbor's reception ahead of the speaker's impressive experience.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The church has something to say: God's judgment against sin and his promise of forgiveness in Christ. Preaching, readings, prayers, and hymns should deliver that message clearly enough for people to hear and learn it.

This is one reason explanation and catechesis matter. A rich confession that nobody has been taught to understand may become unfamiliar sound. Teaching its words helps the congregation receive and confess the treasure already there.

The same concern shapes how members treat one another. Christian maturity is not demonstrated by winning every argument over a permissible practice. Love asks what will strengthen the neighbor in Christ.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Feeling uplifted can accompany edification, but the two are not identical. A comforting falsehood may feel wonderful. A true word that exposes sin may initially hurt. The proper question is what was taught and whether it served repentance, faith, and love.

Intelligibility alone is also insufficient. Error can be expressed very clearly. Paul seeks understandable speech that actually instructs the church in the truth; he does not exchange truthful teaching for a pleasing presentation.

## So What

Ask what the congregation is learning to believe, pray, and confess. That question is more useful than deciding whether every moment produced a desired feeling.

When a word or practice is unfamiliar, seek an explanation. When you teach or serve, consider the person receiving it. Building up happens among neighbors, under Christ's Word, as his gifts strengthen faith and put love to work.
