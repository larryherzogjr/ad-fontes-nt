Philippians 2 contains one of the New Testament's most discussed expressions. The difficulty deserves honesty. It also belongs to a passage whose movement is clear: Christ takes the servant's place, obeys to the point of death, and is exalted.

## The Word

**ἁρπαγμός** (*harpagmos*) is a noun found only in Philippians 2:6 in the New Testament. It is related to the language of seizing or grasping. Proposed translations include *something to be grasped*, *something to hold onto*, and *something to exploit for advantage*.

A related verb does not settle which nuance a noun has in a particular construction. Nor can one English gloss resolve the whole Christological passage. We must read the expression alongside the form of God, equality with God, the form of a servant, and Christ's obedience.

## Range of Meaning

The interpretive question concerns how Christ regards equality with God. Does the expression emphasize something not seized, something not clung to, or an advantage not used for self-serving gain? Translations differ in how they represent that relationship.

The NET retains the grasping language. Other translations make the sense of exploitation explicit. Either way, the passage should not be reduced to a dictionary entry with a doctrine attached afterward.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul says that Christ:

> “did not regard equality with God as something to be grasped” (Philippians 2:6, NET)

The sentence continues by describing his emptying himself through taking a servant's form and becoming human. The participles explain that descent in terms of what he takes and how he obeys. They do not say that his divine nature is discarded.

The surrounding appeal concerns the congregation's life together. Paul calls believers away from selfish ambition and toward attention to others. Christ's action grounds the appeal, but its saving depth exceeds an ordinary example of politeness. His obedience reaches the cross.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Son does not have to stop being God in order to become our servant. His humiliation is the humiliation of the one person who is truly divine and truly human. The Formula of Concord's treatment of Christ's person distinguishes the personal union from his state of humiliation.

That distinction protects both sides of the passage. We do not make his humanity a disguise through which nothing genuinely human is suffered. We also do not make the incarnation a temporary loss of deity. The one who takes the servant's place is the Lord who saves us.

Christ's self-giving then frees Christians to serve without turning every relationship into a contest for advantage. That ethical application proceeds from his gift; it does not replace his atoning work with a general lesson in humility.

## Where People Get It Wrong

An appeal to the word's root can create false certainty about a disputed phrase. A different mistake treats the disputed translation as though everything in the passage were equally uncertain. The surrounding statements about Christ's service, obedience, death, and exaltation still have to be heard.

## So What

Read Philippians 2:1–11 as a whole. Receive its proclamation of Christ before using it to assess someone else's humility.

Then consider where your own desire for status obstructs service. The Savior who stooped to the cross has given you more than a better example: he has given himself. [Kenoō](/greek/kenoo/) continues the discussion of his emptying himself.
