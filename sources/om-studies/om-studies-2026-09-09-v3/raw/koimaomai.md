At a Christian funeral, calling death sleep does not mean that nothing terrible has happened. It means the person who has died is entrusted to the Lord who raises the dead. The promise allows grief to be spoken without making death the final word.

## The Word

**κοιμάομαι** (*koimaomai*) means *to sleep* or *to fall asleep*. The New Testament uses it both for ordinary sleep and as a description of death.

John 11 deliberately brings the two senses together. Jesus says Lazarus has fallen asleep; the disciples think he means ordinary rest. Jesus then states plainly that Lazarus has died. Context identifies the metaphor.

## Range of Meaning

First Corinthians 15:6 describes some witnesses of the resurrection as having fallen asleep. Verses 18 and 20 apply the expression to those who have died in Christ, and verse 51 places it within the promise of bodily transformation.

Acts 7:60 also uses the verb at Stephen's death, though the NET translates the meaning directly as *he died*. The English rendering need not repeat the sleep image in every passage for the Greek connection to be present.

The word alone is not a complete doctrine of death. Its Christian comfort comes from the resurrection announced alongside it.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul writes about those who have died:

> “the dead in Christ will rise first.” (1 Thessalonians 4:16, NET)

He has already described them as asleep and has grounded hope in Jesus' death and resurrection. His concern is that the congregation not imagine deceased believers have missed the Lord's coming.

Paul does not forbid grieving. He distinguishes their grief from grief without hope, then tells them to encourage one another with the promise that they will be with the Lord.

First Corinthians 15 makes the same foundation indispensable: if Christ has not been raised, those who have fallen asleep in him have perished. The metaphor supplies no independent rescue apart from the risen Christ.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Christian hope concerns the resurrection of the body. The person whose death we mourn is not abandoned to the grave, and the body's future is not merely an illustration of something that happens inwardly.

At the same time, the sleep metaphor should not be made to settle every question about awareness between death and resurrection. Philippians 1:23 speaks of departing to be with Christ. Both that promise and the promised raising of the dead must be heard.

The church's comfort is therefore personal and concrete. Christ keeps those who belong to him, and he will raise them. We need not explain the intermediate state as though we had observed it in order to trust his word.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Treating *sleep* as a technical proof of unconsciousness asks too much of a metaphor. Treating it as mere politeness toward death asks too little of the resurrection passages in which it appears.

The comparison serves the promise. Death is real, but Christ can awaken those whom death has taken.

## So What

Grieve the person you miss. You do not betray faith by feeling the separation keenly.

Then hear the words Paul gives for mutual consolation. The dead in Christ will rise. That promise rests in the Lord who died and rose, not in your ability to make the funeral feel hopeful.
