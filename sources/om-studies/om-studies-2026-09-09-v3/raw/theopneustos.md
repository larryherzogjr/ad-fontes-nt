The doctrine of Scripture has been argued over for four centuries, and the verse everyone reaches for contains a word that appears exactly one time in the Bible.

That is not a scandal, but it should slow us down. A word used once cannot be triangulated against its other uses. There is no second passage to check it against, no development across the corpus, no author using it differently in an earlier letter. Whatever *theopneustos* means, it has to be got at through its parts, its background, and the single sentence it stands in.

And the single sentence is itself the site of an old translation dispute — not about whether Scripture is inspired, which the sentence assumes rather than argues, but about where the verb *is* belongs. That question has been quietly settled by most modern translations in a way that reads well and may not be right, and readers who have never been told the question exists deserve to know about it.

## The Word

The Greek word is θεόπνευστος (*theopneustos*), pronounced in the Erasmian convention as *the-OP-nyoo-stos*, with the accent on the second syllable. It is a verbal adjective — a compound of *theos* (θεός), *God*, and the root of *pneō* (πνέω), *to breathe* or *to blow*.

Verbal adjectives ending in *-tos* are ordinarily passive in force: *gnōstos* is *known*, *agapētos* is *beloved*, *eklektos* is *chosen*. On the ordinary pattern, *theopneustos* means *God-breathed* in the sense of *breathed out by God* — not *God-breathing* in the sense of *conveying God's breath to the reader*. The distinction matters. The passive sense locates the divine action at the origin: Scripture is what God breathed out. The active sense would locate it at the point of reception: Scripture is what breathes God into you. Both are true theologically. Only one is what the word says.

The word family:

*Pneuma* (πνεῦμα) — *breath, wind, spirit*. The New Testament's word for the Holy Spirit is the ordinary word for breath, and the overlap is not a pun but a doctrine. The Nicene Creed's *Dominum et vivificantem* — the Lord and giver of life — is built on it.

*Pneō* (πνέω) — *to blow*. Used of wind (John 3:8, where Jesus exploits the same overlap: the *pneuma* blows where it wishes).

*Emphysaō* (ἐμφυσάω) — *to breathe into*. Not a cognate of *theopneustos*, but the theological neighbor. It is the Septuagint's verb at Genesis 2:7, where God breathes into the man's nostrils and he becomes a living being, and it is John's verb at John 20:22, where the risen Christ breathes on the disciples and says, "Receive the Holy Spirit." The three moments belong together: God breathes and there is a man; God breathes and there is Scripture; Christ breathes and there is a church with authority to forgive.

Outside the New Testament, the adjective is attested in Hellenistic Greek — used of dreams thought to come from a god, and of certain healing properties attributed to divine agency. This background is worth knowing and worth not overreading. It confirms that the word was available and that it meant *originating from a god* rather than *inspiring*. It does not import a pagan theory of ecstatic possession into 2 Timothy, and Paul's sentence gives no hint of one; the prophets of Israel remained fully themselves while they wrote.

## Range of Meaning

With one occurrence, there is no range in the ordinary sense. What there is instead is a syntactic question that produces two different sentences.

*"All Scripture is God-breathed and profitable…"* The predicate reading. Greek: *pasa graphē theopneustos kai ōphelimos*. There is no verb in the clause; Greek routinely omits *is*, and the reader must supply it. On this reading, the supplied *is* comes before *theopneustos*, and the verse makes two assertions about all Scripture: it is God-breathed, and it is useful. This is the reading of nearly every modern English translation.

*"Every God-breathed Scripture is also profitable…"* The attributive reading. On this reading, *theopneustos* modifies *graphē* rather than being predicated of it, and the supplied *is* comes later. The verse then makes one assertion — about the usefulness of Scripture — and treats its God-breathed character as already agreed. This was the reading of the Revised Version's margin and of a number of serious commentators, and the *kai* ("and" / "also") sitting between the two adjectives is what gives it grammatical traction.

Two things should be said plainly about this. First, the difference is real and a reader is entitled to know the choice was made. Second, it decides almost nothing doctrinally. The attributive reading does not create a category of non-God-breathed Scripture; it assumes the God-breathed character rather than asserting it, which is if anything a stronger presupposition. Paul is not arguing that Scripture is inspired. He is telling Timothy what to do with writings whose character neither man doubts. A doctrine of Scripture built on winning this syntactic point would be a doctrine built on sand — and it does not need to be, because the assumption underneath both readings is the same.

There is a second, smaller question in the same phrase. *Pasa graphē* can be rendered *all Scripture* (the collection as a whole) or *every Scripture* (each individual passage). Greek allows both with an anarthrous noun. The distributive sense — *every* — is if anything the more natural, and it is the more demanding: not merely that the collection taken together is God-breathed, but that each part is.

## Where You'll Meet It

*2 Timothy 3:16.* "Every scripture is inspired by God and useful for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness." The one occurrence. Note what surrounds it. Verse 15 has just told Timothy that he has known the *hiera grammata* — the sacred writings — from infancy, and that they are able to make him wise *for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus*. Verse 17 states the purpose: that the man of God may be equipped for every good work. The inspiration claim is bracketed on one side by salvation in Christ and on the other by equipping for work. It is not a claim about the text in isolation. It is a claim about what the text does, and the first thing named is Christ.

*Genesis 2:7 (Septuagint).* The background text. God forms the man from dust and *breathes* into him, and the man becomes a living being. The dust does not become alive by being well-arranged. It becomes alive because God breathed. The parallel to Scripture is exact and worth pressing: ink and papyrus and a human author's vocabulary and circumstances are all really present, as the dust was really present, and none of them accounts for what the thing became.

*John 20:22.* "He breathed on them and said, 'Receive the Holy Spirit.'" John chooses the Genesis verb. The risen Christ breathes, and what follows is the authority to forgive sins. The same divine breath that made a man and gave a Scripture now constitutes a church with something to say.

*2 Peter 1:21.* The complementary text, and the one that supplies what *theopneustos* leaves unspecified. “for no prophecy was ever borne of human impulse; rather, men carried along by the Holy Spirit spoke from God.” *Theopneustos* names the product; 2 Peter names the process. The prophets were *carried* — the verb is used of a ship under sail — which is neither dictation nor mere religious insight.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear


*Scripture is the norm that norms and is not normed.* The Formula of Concord's Rule and Norm states the confessional Lutheran position without hedging: Scripture alone is the judge, rule, and standard by which all teachings are to be evaluated; the creeds and the Confessions are witnesses, testifying to how Scripture has been understood, and they hold their authority derivatively. This is what *sola Scriptura* actually claims, and it is narrower and more defensible than the caricature. It does not claim that Scripture is the only source of any true statement, nor that tradition is worthless, nor that a reader with no history behind him will read well. It claims that when a dispute arises, one text settles it, and the reason one text can settle it is *theopneustos*.

*The Word does what it says.* Lutheran theology is unusual in how much weight it puts on the efficacy of the Word — not merely its truth. Scripture and preaching are not information about grace; they are means by which the Spirit delivers it. The breath image carries this. Breath is not a description of life. Breath is how life gets into a body. Confessional Lutherans read 2 Timothy 3:15 accordingly: the sacred writings *are able to give you wisdom for salvation*, and the ability belongs to the writings, not to the reader's technique.

## So What

*Scripture holds Christ, and is honored by being read for him.* Luther's image of Scripture as the cradle in which Christ lies is the necessary companion to a high doctrine of inspiration, and it is the corrective to a specific danger. It is possible to hold the highest possible view of the Bible's inspiration and still read it as a rulebook, a code, a repository of principles for living — to venerate the cradle and never look at the child. Paul's own sentence forbids it. The sacred writings make wise *for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus*. That is the stated purpose, given before the inspiration claim rather than after it.

This is where confessional Lutheran teaching parts company with a strain of evangelical bibliology that shares its formal doctrine. The disagreement is rarely about whether Scripture is God-breathed. It is about whether the God-breathed text is chiefly a source of instruction for the Christian life or chiefly the place where Christ is delivered to sinners. The Pastorals answer the question in the same breath in which they raise it.
