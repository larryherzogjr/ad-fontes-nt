Paul traces our adoption in Christ to God's good pleasure. That can sound unsettling if we imagine divine pleasure as a passing mood. In Ephesians, however, it names the gracious purpose God has made known in his Son, the purpose for which the church gives thanks.

## The Word

**εὐδοκία** (*eudokia*) means *good pleasure*, *favor*, or *delight*. Context tells us whose pleasure or desire is meant and what gives it expression.

In Ephesians 1:5 and 1:9, the word belongs to God's gracious will and purpose in Christ. It does not name a quality in us that persuades him to become gracious.

## Range of Meaning

Matthew 11:26 uses the word in Jesus' praise of the Father for revealing what was hidden from the wise to little children. The NET renders it *gracious will*. Philippians 2:13 speaks of God working in believers both their willing and their action for his good pleasure.

These uses join God's initiative to what he gives and accomplishes. They do not invite us to search for a secret preference unrelated to Christ or to treat his favor as payment for our prior goodness.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Ephesians places the revelation of God's purpose:

> “according to his good pleasure that he set forth in Christ” (Ephesians 1:9, NET)

The surrounding paragraph speaks of adoption, redemption through Christ's blood, forgiveness, and the promised Holy Spirit. God's good pleasure has this saving content. It is not an empty claim that whatever happens must be pleasant to him in the same way.

Luke 2:14 requires a textual distinction. Some manuscripts read the nominative *eudokia*; others have the genitive *eudokias*, differing by a final sigma. The NET follows the genitive and speaks of peace among people with whom God is pleased. The sense is his favor toward them, not peace earned by their goodwill.

The variant affects how the phrase is construed. It deserves explanation, but it does not change the angel's announcement that the Savior has been born.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Formula of Concord XI directs the discussion of election to Christ rather than to speculation about God's hidden counsel. The Solid Declaration identifies Christ as the book of life in whom we should seek the Father's eternal election (XI.13).

God's good pleasure therefore gives the frightened conscience somewhere to turn: the Savior promised in the gospel. It does not send you inward to determine whether your motives were sufficiently pleasing before God chose to show mercy.

Philippians also keeps grace and new obedience together. God works in believers so that they will and act. His favor is not earned by that obedience, but neither does his work leave Christian life empty of love and service.

## So What

You do not need to make yourself the explanation for God's mercy. Ephesians gives you a better reason to trust it: his gracious purpose in Christ.

Receive that mercy where he promises it, and let thanksgiving move into the work your neighbor needs. The God whose pleasure is gracious is also at work in the people he has made his own.
