Paul's warning before Communion can leave a careful Christian looking in the wrong direction. You hear that you must discern the body, and begin measuring your concentration, your knowledge, or your success in overcoming sin. How much discernment is enough?

Begin with what Paul tells you to discern. The warning has an object. Before it becomes a question about the quality of your examination, it is a question about the gift you are receiving.

## The Word

**διακρίνω** (*diakrinō*) means *to distinguish, discern, or judge*. It belongs to the same family as *krinō*, to judge, but its uses often involve making a distinction or evaluating something. A reader need not know Greek to understand the ordinary action: recognize what stands before you and treat it accordingly.

In 1 Corinthians 11:29 the form is *diakrinōn*, a present participle. Paul describes someone eating and drinking without discerning the body. The grammar names a failure in receiving; it does not say that the communicant's discernment brings Christ's body into existence.

## Range of Meaning

The verb is not a technical word used only at the altar. In 1 Corinthians 4:7 it concerns what distinguishes one person from another. In 14:29 it describes evaluating what prophets say. In 11:31 Paul applies it to examining ourselves.

Those uses give us a range, not a doctrine hidden inside a dictionary definition. The context must tell us what is being distinguished, and why that distinction matters. At the Supper, Paul has already named the body and blood of the Lord.

## Where You’ll Meet It

> “For the one who eats and drinks without careful regard for the body eats and drinks judgment against himself.” (1 Corinthians 11:29, NET)

The NET's phrase *careful regard* brings out the practical force of discerning. This is recognition that changes how a person receives.

Read backward to verses 23–27. Paul recounts Christ's words over the bread and cup, then warns about guilt concerning his body and blood. Read forward to verse 31: self-examination belongs here too. The paragraph does not offer a choice between recognizing Christ's gift and examining our treatment of it.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The warning fits the reality of the gift. Christ gives his body with the bread and his blood with the wine. Receiving without faith does not turn that gift into ordinary food; it makes the reception perilous. The communicant's confidence rests in Christ's words, while his warning calls for repentance.

This also explains why pastoral preparation matters. Teaching about the Supper is not an entrance examination designed to keep needy sinners away. It helps us recognize what Christ gives and receive it according to his institution.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Some interpreters emphasize the congregation as Christ's body. Their concern has a real basis: the Corinthians were humiliating poor members and eating selfishly. Paul will not let us honor the altar while despising our neighbor.

But making the body refer only to the congregation separates verse 29 from the body and blood just named in verse 27. The Lutheran reading keeps Christ's sacramental gift at the center and takes the congregation's mistreatment seriously as an abuse of that gift.

The other mistake turns discernment into moral self-certification. Paul's answer is examination followed by eating, not a claim to have become sinless. [Anaxiōs](/greek/anaxios/) explains the difference between an unworthy manner of receiving and the sinner who comes seeking forgiveness.

## So What

At the altar, you are not asked to make Christ present by thinking hard enough. You are taught to receive what he says is there.

Confess your sins. Hear his promise. Receive his gift with faith, and recognize the neighbor beside you as someone you may not despise. The Lord who warns against careless reception is the Lord who has prepared this Supper for sinners.
