The crowd's shout at Jesus' entry into Jerusalem carries a prayer within its praise. The king they welcome is the one from whom salvation is sought. Christians still sing that word as they prepare to receive the gifts of the Savior who came to give his life.

## The Word

**ὡσαννά** (*hōsanna*) preserves a Semitic expression in Greek letters. Its Hebrew background is a plea to save, often rendered *save now* or *please save*.

The familiar English word does not translate an ordinary Greek noun. Like [Abba](/greek/abba/), it carries the sound of an expression from another language into the New Testament's Greek text.

## Range of Meaning

Psalm 118:25 contains the plea for the Lord to deliver, followed in verse 26 by a blessing upon the one who comes in his name. Those verses stand behind the crowd's words in the Gospel accounts.

By the setting described in the Gospels, *hosanna* functions as an acclamation as well as carrying its background of petition. Calling it praise need not erase the request for salvation; translating it as a request need not erase the crowd's welcome.

The word alone does not tell us how fully each person understood Jesus' mission. John 12:16 explicitly says that even the disciples understood the significance of these events more fully after Jesus was glorified.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Matthew records the crowd shouting:

> “Hosanna to the Son of David!” (Matthew 21:9, NET)

Mark 11:9–10 connects the acclamation with the coming kingdom of David, and John 12:13 names Jesus as Israel's king. All three place the word within his entry into Jerusalem.

The Gospel story then shows how this king saves. The path leads through betrayal, suffering, and the cross. His kingship is not measured by the crowd's ability to keep cheering or by his escape from humiliation.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Sanctus joins the praise of God's holiness to the welcome of the coming Lord. In familiar Lutheran Communion orders, the congregation sings Hosanna as part of its preparation for the Supper. The prayer and the reception of Christ's gift belong together.

The confession is concrete: the crucified and risen Lord gives his body and blood as he has promised. Our singing does not produce his presence or persuade him to become a Savior. His institution and words give the gift its certainty.

The liturgical arrangement helps the congregation receive rather than merely observe. We ask for salvation from the Lord who has already accomplished it and who gives its benefits to sinners. Particular service orders may differ; the promise rests on Christ's words.

## So What

When you sing Hosanna, hear the petition as well as the praise. You are asking for what you need from the one able to give it.

You need not match the excitement of a crowd before receiving his mercy. The king who entered Jerusalem humbly knows how to come to weak and burdened people. His gift is firmer than the strength of your voice.
