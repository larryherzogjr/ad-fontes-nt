Paul calls governing authorities God's servants in a sentence about taxes. That may sound surprising if service to God is imagined as something confined to the sanctuary. Scripture gives ordinary public offices a place within God's care for the world.

## The Word

**λειτουργός** (*leitourgos*) means *servant* or *minister*, often with a public or religious setting. It is related to [Leitourgia](/greek/leitourgia/), service or worship. Its history lies behind later liturgical vocabulary, but its New Testament uses are broader than an office at the altar.

A shared word does not give every person it names the same authority. A ruler, an apostle, and Christ may all be described with service language while carrying out profoundly different work.

## Range of Meaning

Romans 13:6 uses *leitourgos* for governing authorities. Romans 15:16 applies it to Paul's ministry to the Gentiles. Philippians 2:25 calls Epaphroditus a minister to Paul's need, while Hebrews 8:2 speaks of Christ as minister in the heavenly sanctuary.

These uses should prevent both narrowing and flattening. The word is not limited to secular public service, and it does not turn secular office into a sacramental ministry. The passage identifies the calling and its gifts.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul writes that:

> “the authorities are God’s servants devoted to governing.” (Romans 13:6, NET)

Romans 13:1–7 concerns public authority, wrongdoing, punishment, taxes, and honor. Government's work has a temporal purpose. The passage does not commission rulers to forgive sins or create faith by force.

Philippians 2 supplies a different picture of service: Epaphroditus has risked himself in helping Paul. The term can honor practical assistance without making the helper a civil magistrate or a replacement for Christ's priesthood.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Augsburg Confession XVI affirms civil government and Christians' participation in lawful public responsibilities. It rejects the idea that Christian perfection requires abandoning ordinary households, possessions, and public life. Love is exercised within those callings.

That gives a Christian grounds for respecting public service without treating every official act as holy. An office can be God's good provision while its holder sins against the task entrusted to him. The confession explicitly limits obedience where a command would require sin.

Vocation is therefore both consolation and accountability. Ordinary work can serve God by serving the neighbor, and the person holding an office is responsible for its proper use. A title does not sanctify cruelty, dishonesty, or self-enrichment.

## Where People Get It Wrong

One mistake treats secular work as spiritually inferior until it acquires church language. Another uses God's institution of authority to shield every exercise of power from criticism. Neither follows from Paul's description of rulers as servants.

Service names responsibility. A ruler is not the owner of the people, and a public office is not a personal reward to be consumed at their expense.

## So What

Give thanks for honest work that protects and sustains your community. Carry out your own responsibilities with the same attention to the people they affect.

When public authorities fail, truthful criticism and lawful efforts to correct wrongdoing can serve the office's proper purpose. [Hypotassō](/greek/hypotasso/) considers obedience and its limits.
