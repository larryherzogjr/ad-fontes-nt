When Paul calls the church God's temple, he is saying more than that its members should be respectable. God dwells among his people by his Spirit. That gift makes their common life holy and makes the destruction of the congregation a serious matter.

## The Word

**ναός** (*naos*) means *temple* or *sanctuary*. In descriptions of the Jerusalem temple it commonly refers to the sanctuary building, while **ἱερόν** (*hieron*) commonly names the wider temple precincts.

That distinction helps a reader follow the setting. It should not become an inflexible rule that *naos* always means only the innermost Holy of Holies. Its uses must still be read in context, including the passages that apply temple language to Christ and his people.

## Range of Meaning

Matthew 27:51 uses *naos* when describing the temple curtain torn at Jesus' death. John 2:19–21 uses it in Jesus' saying about destroying the temple and raising it in three days; John explains that he was speaking of his body.

Paul applies the word to the church in 1 Corinthians 3 and 2 Corinthians 6. Ephesians 2:19–22 describes believers being built together into a holy temple, a dwelling place of God in the Spirit.

These passages share the theme of God's dwelling without making all their subjects interchangeable. Christ's body, the congregation, and the believer's bodily life each require attention to the particular argument.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul asks the Corinthians:

> “Do you not know that you are God’s temple and that God’s Spirit lives in you?” (1 Corinthians 3:16, NET)

The Greek addresses *you* in the plural while naming one temple. Paul is speaking to the congregation together. The surrounding discussion concerns those who build on the foundation of Jesus Christ and warns against destroying God's temple.

First Corinthians 6:19 also uses temple language, within Paul's instruction against sexual immorality. That passage requires care for bodily conduct; it should not replace the corporate point of chapter 3. Reading both passages preserves the connection between belonging to Christ and life with the members of his body.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The church is holy because God has made it his dwelling, not because its members have achieved a sufficient degree of moral excellence. The Spirit gathers people to Christ through the gospel and sustains their faith through his gifts.

That holiness does not excuse wrongdoing. It gives Paul's warnings their force. Teachers and members are handling people who belong to God; division, exploitation, and sexual sin cannot be dismissed as private preferences with no bearing on the body.

A church building serves the congregation's hearing, prayer, and reception of the sacraments. The building is not the whole meaning of God's temple. Nor does God's presence among his people make public worship unnecessary, as though each person could possess the church independently.

## So What

Treat your congregation as people among whom God is at work. Speak carefully about them, resist needless division, and support the teaching that builds on Christ.

Treat your own body with corresponding care. It belongs within the life Christ has redeemed, including how you live toward other people. Paul's temple language gives that care a foundation in God's gift rather than in appearance, fitness, or personal prestige.
