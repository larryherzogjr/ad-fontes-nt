Self-examination before Communion can be misunderstood as a search for enough goodness to deserve the sacrament. Paul's instruction has a different shape: examine yourself, and in this way eat and drink.

## The Word

**δοκιμάζω** (*dokimazō*) means *to test*, *examine*, or *approve after examination*. It is a verb used for assessing the character or fitness of something. The result is not always failure; testing can establish what is good.

The word does not prescribe one inward technique or a required level of anxiety. In 1 Corinthians 11, the examination must be understood from Paul's account of the Supper, the Corinthians' abuses, and the warning about unworthy reception.

## Range of Meaning

First Thessalonians 5:21 commands testing and holding fast to what is good. First John 4:1 calls for testing the spirits rather than believing every claim. Romans 12:2 connects discernment with recognizing God's will.

These uses require a standard outside the mere strength of an impression. Examination is not the same as assuming that whatever feels sincere must be true. God's Word supplies the measure for Christian discernment.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul writes:

> “A person should examine himself first, and in this way let him eat the bread and drink of the cup.” (1 Corinthians 11:28, NET)

The next verse warns about eating and drinking judgment without discerning the body. The surrounding chapter has already exposed divisions and contempt for poor members. Honest examination therefore concerns both recognition of the Lord's gift and repentance for conduct contradicting the meal.

The instruction does not end with examination as an achievement in itself. Paul directs the examined person toward eating and drinking. The warning serves a proper reception of what Christ gives.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Small Catechism's final question on the Supper identifies worthy preparation with faith in Christ's words concerning his body and blood given for the forgiveness of sins. Bodily preparation has a place, but it is not the foundation of worthy reception.

Faith includes recognizing the need for that forgiveness. A person who insists there is no sin to confess has missed the gift's purpose. Yet a person whose conscience is painfully aware of sin must not conclude that awareness itself makes Communion forbidden.

The Formula of Concord's treatment of the Supper explicitly distinguishes weak believers from unbelieving, impenitent recipients. Weak faith needs strengthening. The Lord does not withhold his medicine until the patient can prove that the illness has already gone.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Endless inward scrutiny can turn preparation into a demand for emotional certainty. The conscience then examines the examination and never arrives at Christ's promise. Pastoral care should bring that person back to the words that give the gift.

The opposite error skips repentance and instruction as though eating and drinking were automatically beneficial. Paul's warning rules out such carelessness without making spiritual self-confidence the qualification for admission.

## So What

Ask whether you confess your sins, trust Christ's promise, and desire to turn from the wrong you have done. Discuss uncertainty with your pastor rather than inventing a private standard of worthiness.

Then receive the sacrament according to Christ's institution. [Enochos](/greek/enochos/) explains the seriousness of Paul's warning; his direction to eat and drink keeps that warning ordered toward a faithful reception.
