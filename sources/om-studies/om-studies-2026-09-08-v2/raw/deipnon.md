A congregation can gather, eat together, and still need to hear Paul say that it is not really eating the Lord's Supper. That is the charge in 1 Corinthians 11. The Corinthians had plenty of activity. What they had lost was the difference between the Lord's meal and their own.

The name of the meal gives Paul the language for the correction.

## The Word

**δεῖπνον** (*deipnon*) means *supper*, ordinarily the substantial meal of the day. It can describe an ordinary dinner or a banquet. Nothing in the noun by itself tells us who hosts, who is invited, or what is given.

In 1 Corinthians 11:20, Paul supplies the decisive word: *kyriakon*, belonging to the Lord. This is the Lord's Supper. In the next verse, he contrasts it with each person's *own* supper. The contrast is between a meal belonging to Christ and meals being claimed for private advantage.

## Range of Meaning

The word can carry the fullness of a feast. In Luke 14:16–24, Jesus tells of a great banquet whose invited guests make excuses. The host sends his servant to bring in people who could never have treated an invitation as their social entitlement.

Revelation 19:9 uses the same noun for the wedding banquet of the Lamb. Here the feast belongs to the joy of the church's final gathering with her Lord. The ordinary word for supper can bear that promise without ceasing to be a word about eating together.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul writes:

> “For when it is time to eat, everyone proceeds with his own supper. One is hungry and another becomes drunk.” (1 Corinthians 11:21, NET)

The problem is concrete. People who have food consume it while others go hungry. Their gathering shames those who have nothing and shows contempt for the church of God.

Paul answers by recounting what he received and handed on: the Lord took bread, spoke his giving words, and took the cup after supper. He then commands examination, reception, and waiting for one another. The institution governs the congregation's practice.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Christ is the host of this meal. The pastor serves what Christ instituted; the congregation receives it. Our gathering, remembrance, and thanksgiving are real, but they do not originate the gift.

That is why the words over the bread and cup matter so much. They tell us whose body and blood are given, and for whom. The Supper remains Christ's even when those administering or receiving it need correction. His institution judges our practice rather than taking its meaning from our intentions.

This also gives fellowship its proper depth. We are gathered by a common Lord and receive his common gift. The rich Christian cannot turn that table into a private dinner while the poor Christian waits in shame.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Calling the Supper a memorial or a fellowship meal need not be wrong. Jesus commands remembrance, and Paul speaks about the congregation's life together. The problem comes when those descriptions make our remembering or our togetherness the whole substance of the meal.

Neither sincere memories nor a friendly atmosphere can replace what Christ gives. Nor should we describe the Corinthian offense merely as insufficient solemnity. Paul identifies selfish eating and the humiliation of the poor. A formally impressive service can still leave those sins untouched.

## So What

Receive the meal as something entrusted to the church. Its words and gifts precede your mood, your contribution, and your place in the congregation.

Then let that receiving shape how you treat the people who receive beside you. Wait for them. Make room for them. Refuse to turn possessions or standing into privileges at Christ's table. The [cup of blessing](/greek/poterion/) is given by the Lord whose Supper this is.
