A seal tells you whose word stands behind a document or whose claim rests on a possession. Its force does not depend on how impressive the recipient feels. It depends on the one who put it there.

Paul uses that ordinary image when he tells Christians what God has done for them through the Holy Spirit. The action has a giver, a recipient, and a future toward which it points.

## The Word

**σφραγίζω** (*sphragizō*) means *to seal* or *to mark with a seal*. A seal can identify ownership, authenticate something, or secure it. The setting determines which aspect comes forward.

In Ephesians 1:13 and 4:30, the form is *esphragisthēte*: you were sealed. It is aorist passive indicative. Paul states an act performed upon his hearers. He does not command them to produce a seal or to discover a spiritual technique by which they can acquire one.

## Range of Meaning

The New Testament uses the image in several settings. John 6:27 speaks of the Father setting his seal upon the Son. Revelation 7:3 pictures God's servants being sealed on their foreheads. The point in each passage comes from who seals whom and for what purpose.

We should not turn every occurrence into an identical account of Baptism. But these uses share the idea that a seal identifies someone through an act of the one who has authority. Paul uses that image to give Christians assurance about belonging to God.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Ephesians 1:13 joins hearing the gospel, believing in Christ, and being sealed with the promised Holy Spirit. Verse 14 then calls the Spirit the down payment of the inheritance. [Arrabōn](/greek/arrabon/) explores that companion image.

In 2 Corinthians 1:21–22, God is the one who establishes, anoints, seals, and gives the Spirit. The sentence keeps returning to his action.

> “And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, by whom you were sealed for the day of redemption.” (Ephesians 4:30, NET)

The seal points forward to redemption. Yet the same sentence warns against grieving the Spirit. Assurance and the call to repentance appear together, not in competing versions of the Christian life.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Spirit comes through God's means of grace. He is not an achievement for Christians who advance beyond the ordinary gospel. Acts 2:38–39 joins Baptism, forgiveness, and the gift of the Spirit in the apostolic promise.

That is why Baptism gives the Christian something concrete to remember. God put his name upon you through water and his Word. Your assurance need not wait for an experience equal in intensity to someone else's testimony.

Ephesians itself does not use the word Baptism in the sealing sentence. The baptismal connection comes from reading it with the New Testament's account of how God gives the Spirit. The comfort rests on God's giving, rather than on making a single metaphor carry every part of the doctrine.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Some Christians use sealing language for a distinct experience of assurance after conversion. They may be trying to describe a real strengthening of faith. God does comfort his people, and Christians need not deny the joy of such an experience.

But Paul addresses the congregation as people who have been sealed. He does not divide it into ordinary believers and a smaller group who have received the Spirit's authenticating mark. Later growth in assurance does not mean that God's earlier promise was incomplete.

Nor does being sealed make resistance to the Spirit harmless. Ephesians 4 calls the sealed to put away bitterness and malice and to forgive one another. The seal belongs to a living relationship with the God whose gifts faith receives.

## So What

When your assurance is shaken, hear the gospel again. Remember your Baptism. Ask your pastor to speak God's promise to you.

The question is not whether today feels marked by God. He has acted toward you in Christ. His Spirit directs you to that gift and sustains you in the life that belongs to it.
