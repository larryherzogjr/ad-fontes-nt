A wage is credited because work has earned it. Paul uses that ordinary transaction to explain something that does not operate like wages: God's declaration that the ungodly are righteous through faith. The contrast depends on the difference between a debt owed and a gift received.

## The Word

**λογίζομαι** (*logizomai*) means *to reckon*, *to credit*, or *to count*. It can describe accounting or the act of considering something. In Romans 4, Paul repeatedly uses it for righteousness credited to Abraham and to those who believe.

It is not simply another spelling of [Dikaioō](/greek/dikaioo/), *to declare righteous*. The two words contribute to the argument: God declares the sinner righteous, and Paul describes righteousness being credited apart from works.

## Range of Meaning

An accounting image does not settle the argument by itself. The decisive questions are what is counted, to whom, and on what basis. Paul contrasts payment due to a worker with righteousness credited to the one who believes God's promise.

He also speaks of sin not being counted against someone. Romans 4:6–8 joins credited righteousness with forgiven wrongdoing. Second Corinthians 5:19 likewise describes God reconciling the world in Christ without counting people's trespasses against them.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul writes:

> “But to the one who does not work, but believes in the one who declares the ungodly righteous, his faith is credited as righteousness.” (Romans 4:5, NET)

Abraham's example also appears in Galatians 3:6. Romans 4:9–11 emphasizes that the crediting preceded his circumcision. His standing was not earned by that later obedience.

The chapter closes by extending the promise beyond Abraham to those who believe in the God who raised Jesus (4:22–24). Paul directs attention to Christ's death and resurrection, not to the discovery of a sufficient moral balance within the believer.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Apology explains that faith justifies because it receives mercy and reconciliation for Christ's sake. Faith is not itself a worthy achievement substituted for other achievements (IV.86–87).

This is why imputation is no arbitrary fiction. God does not overlook an unpaid accusation by pretending it never mattered. Christ has answered for sin, and God's promise gives the sinner a real standing before him. [Katakrima](/greek/katakrima/) considers the resulting verdict of no condemnation.

The Spirit also renews the person who believes. That new obedience is real and necessary in Christian life, but it is not the account from which faith draws its righteousness before God. Growth follows the gift rather than becoming its foundation.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Some object that a declared righteousness leaves people inwardly unchanged. Roman Catholic teaching includes forgiveness and inward renewal together within justification; it does not simply postpone all justification until someone has gradually become good enough.

The Lutheran distinction is more precise: renewal must not become the ground of the verdict that comforts the conscience. We confess both forgiveness and transformation while locating our righteousness before God in Christ, received by faith.

## So What

Your progress can be examined honestly without becoming the final court of appeal. Repent of what is wrong and pursue what is good, but hear God's verdict from his promise.

The righteousness credited to faith is not a favorable estimate of your potential. It is God's gift for Christ's sake, addressed to sinners who need it now.
