A paycheck is not a present. When the hours have been worked and the wage agreed, the employer owes the payment. Calling it generosity would not change the worker's claim.

Paul uses that ordinary distinction to explain justification. But Jesus also promises rewards. Christians need to hear both without turning grace into wages or treating the promises as embarrassing language we ought to explain away.

## The Word

**μισθός** (*misthos*) means *wage*, *pay*, or *reward*. It can name what is paid for work or what is awarded to someone. The word does not tell us, all by itself, whether the recipient has placed the giver under an obligation.

That is why the setting matters. A wage owed under an agreement and a reward freely promised can both be called *misthos*. The relationship between giver and recipient must be read from the sentence.

## Range of Meaning

Romans 4 uses the ordinary wage relation to draw a sharp contrast with grace. Matthew uses reward language both for the approval hypocrites seek and for what the Father promises. First Corinthians 3 connects reward with the quality of a minister's work.

We should neither pour all these passages into one commercial arrangement nor give the word a harmless meaning whenever it troubles us. Scripture can deny that God owes us justification and still say that he rewards work done in faith.

## Where You’ll Meet It

> “Now to the one who works, his pay is not credited due to grace but due to obligation.” (Romans 4:4, NET)

The next verse describes the person who believes in the God who justifies the ungodly. Paul is excluding works as the basis of the sinner's righteous standing before God. The worker's claim to payment cannot explain justification by grace.

Matthew 5:12 promises reward to those persecuted for Christ. In Matthew 6:1–6, Jesus contrasts seeking human applause with living before the Father who sees in secret. Second John 8 likewise urges believers to remain watchful so that they receive a full reward.

First Corinthians 3:8, 14–15 concerns building upon the foundation of Christ. A worker can suffer loss while himself being saved. Paul distinguishes the evaluation of work from the foundation on which salvation rests.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

God's gracious acceptance of the sinner comes first. Christ's righteousness is received through faith, not supplied by a record of useful deeds. A reward promised to God's children does not become the price by which they buy their adoption.

Yet the Father delights in the works of his children and promises to reward them. Faith does not need to make those promises unreal in order to preserve grace. God freely promises, and he is faithful to what he promises.

This gives work significance without making the conscience keep a salvation ledger. The neighbor's need is real. The work done for that neighbor matters. Its worth does not depend on whether anyone notices it or pays it back.

## Where People Get It Wrong

One error constructs a system in which enough good works put God in our debt. Romans 4 directly rules out that account of justification. Even Christian work cannot replace the foundation of Christ.

The opposite error empties every reward passage of substance. But Jesus speaks to persecuted people and unnoticed servants precisely to assure them that their Father sees. We should not take away that comfort because the word can be misused.

Scripture does not give us a table for calculating heavenly rank. It does give promises, warnings, and encouragement to faithful service. Those can stand without becoming a second way of earning salvation.

## So What

Do the good your neighbor needs without asking whether it has earned you a place with God. Christ gives that place freely.

And when the work seems wasted, hear the Father's promise. What is hidden from other people is not hidden from him. The [unfading crown](/greek/stephanos/) belongs to his faithful giving, not to a debt you have forced him to acknowledge.
