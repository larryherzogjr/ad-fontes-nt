Christian hope includes the body. Paul does not promise only that something inward will survive while the mortal body becomes permanently irrelevant. He directs believers to the God who raised Jesus and will give life to their bodies too.

## The Word

**ἐγείρω** (*egeirō*) means *to raise*, *awaken*, or *rouse*. It is a verb used for ordinary rising as well as resurrection. A person may get up from sleep, rise from illness, or be raised from the dead.

The word's presence therefore does not by itself establish a resurrection claim. The object and context make that clear. When Paul speaks of Jesus raised from the dead, the qualification identifies the action being proclaimed.

## Range of Meaning

The Gospels use the verb in commands to people who are healed and in announcements of Jesus' resurrection. John 2:19–22 connects Jesus' saying about raising the temple with his body and his resurrection.

First Corinthians 15 repeatedly uses the verb in arguing that Christ's resurrection and the resurrection of the dead belong together. If Christ has not been raised, the apostolic proclamation and the hearers' faith have been emptied of their foundation.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul promises that:

> “the one who raised Christ from the dead will also make your mortal bodies alive” (Romans 8:11, NET)

Romans 8:11 names the indwelling Spirit within that promise. The sentence's conclusion speaks of life through the Spirit dwelling in believers. Father, Son, and Spirit are not competing agents with unrelated saving projects.

The following verses address life by the Spirit and the putting to death of sinful deeds. Future bodily resurrection does not make present bodily conduct irrelevant. Hope and the new life of obedience belong within the same argument.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Small Catechism's Third Article explanation confesses the resurrection of the dead and eternal life given to believers in Christ. Augsburg Confession XVII likewise confesses Christ's return, the raising of all the dead, and the distinction between eternal life and judgment.

This is more than the continued influence of Jesus' teaching or the preservation of a loved one's memory. Those memories can matter deeply, but they are not the event Paul announces. God raises the dead.

The promise also honors the created body. Bodily weakness and decay are real afflictions, not proof that embodiment was a mistake. Christ's saving work reaches the creature God made, rather than rescuing an allegedly good inward self from an inherently bad body.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Resurrection can be reduced to a metaphor for recovering optimism after disappointment. Scripture can use rising language figuratively, but Paul's explicit argument about Christ and the dead cannot be replaced by that application.

Another mistake promises immediate bodily restoration as the necessary proof of faith. Romans 8 still speaks of mortal bodies and suffering. The Spirit's presence guarantees God's promise without eliminating the waiting that belongs to Christian hope.

## So What

Care for the body entrusted to you and the bodies of those who need help. Their worth is not measured by strength, independence, or usefulness.

At a grave, let grief remain honest while hearing the promise of the risen Christ. [Anastasis](/greek/anastasis/) explores the corresponding noun; *egeirō* emphasizes the action of the God who raises.
