Feeling condemned and being under condemnation are not identical. A conscience can accuse a person whom Christ forgives, and a comfortable conscience can ignore real guilt. Romans directs us to a verdict that is more dependable than either feeling.

## The Word

**κατάκριμα** (*katakrima*) means *condemnation*, the adverse sentence and its consequence. The word occurs in Romans 5:16 and 18 and Romans 8:1.

Its setting is judgment, not merely emotion. Paul is describing what sin brings upon humanity and what Christ's saving work changes for those who belong to him.

## Range of Meaning

Romans 5 contrasts the consequence of Adam's trespass with God's gracious gift in Christ. Judgment brings condemnation; the gift brings justification and life. The contrast concerns humanity's condition before God, not only the psychological effects of wrongdoing.

The word's judicial force does not make the result abstract. Condemnation belongs with death in Paul's argument. Freedom from it belongs with the life God gives through Jesus Christ.

This also explains why a sentence of no condemnation cannot be reduced to advice to stop feeling bad. The promise reaches a deeper problem than an unpleasant mood.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul declares:

> “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.” (Romans 8:1, NET)

The words identify both the verdict and those to whom it applies: people in Christ. Paul does not say that sin has ceased to matter or that everyone is safe regardless of Christ.

The following verses explain God's action. He did what the law could not accomplish through our fallen flesh, sending his Son and condemning sin in the flesh. The announcement of freedom from condemnation rests on God's saving work, not on our refusal to acknowledge the accusation.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Justification is a real verdict for Christ's sake. [Dikaioō](/greek/dikaioo/) considers the declaration of righteousness, while [Logizomai](/greek/logizomai/) follows the crediting of righteousness apart from works. *Katakrima* names the condemning sentence from which Christ delivers us.

The word *now* gives the promise present force. The Christian does not have to wait for a sufficiently impressive record of growth before hearing God's forgiveness. Yet the life described in Romans 8 is also life in the Spirit, not a permission slip for unrepentance.

Accusations must therefore be answered with discernment. If you have harmed someone, confess it and seek to repair the harm. Christ's forgiveness is not a reason to dismiss the neighbor. But repairing the harm does not purchase the verdict that only Christ's work can give.

## So What

You may hear no condemnation even on a day when you feel deeply ashamed. The promise does not require you to produce a matching emotion before it can be true.

Bring the sin into confession and hear the forgiveness spoken for Christ's sake. Then attend to the neighbor and the work before you. You are not trying to overturn God's sentence through self-punishment or a better performance.

Romans 8:1 gives you words to return to when your feelings cannot settle the matter: the verdict belongs to God, and he directs you to Christ.
