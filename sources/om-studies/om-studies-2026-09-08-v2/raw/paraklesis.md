A Christian does not always need an explanation for why suffering happened. Often he needs a promise that remains true while the explanation is unavailable. Paul speaks of comfort received from God and then carried to others who are suffering.

## The Word

**παράκλησις** (*paraklēsis*) can mean *comfort*, *consolation*, *encouragement*, or *exhortation*. The setting tells us whether the word emphasizes strengthening, reassuring, or urging someone forward.

It belongs to the same word family as **παρακαλέω** (*parakaleō*), to encourage or appeal to someone, and [Paraklētos](/greek/parakletos/). The related words should not be treated as interchangeable titles. Here the focus is the consolation and encouragement God gives.

## Range of Meaning

Luke 2:25 describes Simeon waiting for Israel's consolation; the NET renders the phrase as Israel's *restoration*. His hope is fulfilled as he receives the child Jesus. Consolation has a saving reality behind it.

Acts 9:31 speaks of the church living in the Holy Spirit's encouragement. Romans 15:4–5 joins the encouragement of Scripture with the God who gives endurance and comfort.

Hebrews 6:18 grounds strong encouragement in God's unchangeable promise and his inability to lie. The hearer is given a reason to hold fast. The comfort does not depend on discovering a more cheerful interpretation of the circumstances.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul calls God:

> “the Father of mercies and God of all comfort” (2 Corinthians 1:3, NET)

He goes on to describe God comforting us in trouble so that we can comfort others with what we have received. Christ's sufferings and Christ's comfort are present together in the apostolic life.

This does not make suffering desirable or require a sufferer to begin helping everyone else immediately. It identifies the source of the comfort that Christians can eventually share. They pass on a gift; they do not manufacture it.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Smalcald Articles III.4 names the mutual conversation and consolation of Christians among the ways God gives the gospel, alongside the preached Word, Baptism, the Supper, and the keys.

That puts an ordinary Christian's speech in a serious and consoling place. You can remind a grieving neighbor of Christ's resurrection, speak forgiveness to someone burdened by guilt, or pray a scriptural promise with someone who cannot find words.

The authority lies in the gospel you bring. It does not depend on your having suffered an identical loss or found the perfect explanation. God's promise can be spoken simply and received through a familiar voice.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Comfort is not exhausted by a warm feeling, and encouragement is not merely a demand to try harder. A cheerful message can leave the actual fear unanswered. Christian consolation gives the hearer something trustworthy to receive.

This does not make affection, silence, practical help, or an assurance of concern worthless. Nor does the noun itself mean that all comfort must be verbal. Such care can accompany the specific gift of the gospel. Listening patiently may prepare the way for a few truthful words spoken at the right time.

## So What

You do not need to solve a neighbor's suffering before being present with him. Listen, help where you can, and give the promise that belongs to both of you.

When you are the one in need, let another Christian speak it to you. Comfort can come from outside your own exhausted thoughts. Christ remains faithful even when you cannot make yourself feel consoled.
