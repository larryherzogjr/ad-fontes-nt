Hebrews begins by directing our attention to the Son through whom God made the world. Before describing the cleansing he accomplishes, it tells us something about who he is: the radiance of God's glory. The identity of the Savior gives weight to the salvation he brings.

## The Word

**ἀπαύγασμα** (*apaugasma*) means *radiance* or *effulgence*. Its only New Testament occurrence is Hebrews 1:3. The NET chooses *radiance* for the image of glory shining forth.

That image should be read within the whole description of the Son. A lexical gloss alone cannot establish every claim the church makes about his eternal relation to the Father.

## Range of Meaning

Language about brightness can invite comparison with either light shining out or light reflected from something else. The point here is not that the English word *reflection* is forbidden in every explanation. It is that Hebrews presents much more than a creature receiving and passing along a little divine light.

The Son is the one through whom the world was made, who sustains all things, and who expresses God's being. Calling him a reflection becomes misleading if it reduces him to an external object illuminated by a God whose life he does not share.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Hebrews says:

> “The Son is the radiance of his glory and the representation of his essence” (Hebrews 1:3, NET)

The second description uses [Charaktēr](/greek/charakter/), the word for an exact impression or representation. The two images contribute to a single account of the Son's identity. Neither should be pressed into a physical model of the divine being.

The sentence continues with his sustaining work, cleansing for sins, and session at God's right hand. Hebrews brings the confession of who he is directly into the account of what he has done for sinners.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Nicene confession of Light from Light helps us hear this passage as part of Scripture's witness to the Son's full deity. He is eternally from the Father and shares the Father's divine being. He is not a later creature added to an otherwise complete divine life.

Athanasius used the radiance image in defending the Nicene confession: it expresses the Son's relation to the Father without making him a creature external to the Father's being. He also refused to impose bodily division on God (*On the Decrees of the Council of Nicaea*, 23–24).

The image serves the confession; it does not give us permission to calculate divine generation from the behavior of sunlight. Father and Son remain distinct persons, and the Son is truly God. See [Phōs](/greek/phos/) for the broader biblical language of light.

## So What

The one who cleanses sins is not a distant messenger with uncertain access to God's mercy. Hebrews identifies him as the Son who truly makes God's glory known and sustains the world he came to save.

That is why the opening of Hebrews belongs at the bedside and beside a troubled conscience. The cleansing it announces has the authority of this Son behind it. His mercy is no faint echo of a more trustworthy word elsewhere.
