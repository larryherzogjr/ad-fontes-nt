The heart in Scripture is more than the place where a person feels something deeply. It thinks, desires, believes, and resists. That makes the promise of a changed heart much larger than a promise of improved religious feelings.

## The Word

**καρδία** (*kardia*) means *heart*. In the passages considered here, it names the inner life of the person, including thought, desire, and trust.

The word does not exclude emotion, but neither does it isolate emotion from understanding and will. We should not import a modern contrast between a thinking head and a feeling heart into every biblical occurrence.

## Range of Meaning

Matthew 15:19 locates evil ideas and destructive actions in the heart. Romans 10:9–10 speaks of believing with the heart. The same word can therefore name the source of evil and the place where faith receives God's promise.

Second Corinthians 3:3 describes the Spirit writing on human hearts. Hebrews 8:10, citing Jeremiah's covenant promise, speaks of God's law inscribed there. These images describe God's action upon his people, not a naturally pure interior that needs only to express itself more freely.

Ezekiel 36:26–27 supplies another image: God gives a new heart and puts his Spirit within his people. The promise of renewal belongs to him.

## Where You’ll Meet It

At Philippi, Lydia listens to Paul's preaching:

> “The Lord opened her heart to respond to what Paul was saying.” (Acts 16:14, NET)

Opening the heart is therefore biblical language. The decisive question is who acts and through what means. Luke names the Lord as the one who opens Lydia's heart while she hears the apostolic message.

Romans 10 likewise places faith in relation to the proclaimed word about Christ. Believing in the heart is not a second, private route to God apart from what is preached. Faith receives the risen Lord made known through that word.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Conversion is God's work through his Word. A sinner does not first prepare an untouched chamber of the heart and then permit the Spirit to enter as a reward for that preparation. The Spirit creates the faith that receives Christ.

This does not make faith unreal or turn the believer into an unwilling object. Lydia truly responds. The account identifies the Lord's gracious action as the source of that response rather than making her response its own explanation.

Christian prayer and personal confession have a proper place. Their certainty, however, rests in Christ's promise, not in whether a person once used exactly the right invitation or experienced a sufficiently intense inward event.

## Where People Get It Wrong

An invitation to ask Jesus into one's heart can leave hearers treating a remembered prayer as the decisive proof of salvation. The pastoral problem is that the conscience returns to its own sincerity instead of to the Savior's promise.

It would also be inaccurate to claim that Scripture never speaks of an opened heart. Acts does so explicitly. Its account gives God the initiative and connects the opening with hearing the gospel.

## So What

You do not have to inspect your feelings until you discover a heart fit to receive Christ. Hear the Word in which he gives himself to sinners, and bring your need to him in prayer.

When your heart accuses you or wanders, return to that promise. The Lord who opened Lydia's heart is able to sustain faith through the gospel he still causes his church to proclaim.
