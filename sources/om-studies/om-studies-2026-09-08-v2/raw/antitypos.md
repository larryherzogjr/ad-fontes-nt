Peter's comparison between Noah and Baptism is more than a clever resemblance between two uses of water. He connects the deliverance of Noah's household with the saving gift now given through the resurrection of Jesus Christ.

## The Word

**ἀντίτυπος** (*antitypos*) is an adjective meaning *corresponding* or *antitypical*. It occurs in 1 Peter 3:21 and Hebrews 9:24. The relation it names must be understood from the things being compared.

The prefix does not mean that the later thing is simply opposed to the earlier one. Nor does the word establish a universal rule that an antitype is always less real than its type. It identifies correspondence within an argument.

## Range of Meaning

Hebrews 9:24 applies the word to handmade holy places in relation to the true heavenly reality. First Peter uses it within the correspondence between the flood and Baptism. The comparisons move through different subjects and should not be forced into an identical diagram.

The related noun [Typos](/greek/typos/) has its own range of patterns, examples, and types. Recognizing the relationship is useful; making etymology determine every theological use is not.

## Where You’ll Meet It

The NET renders Peter's transition:

> “And this prefigured baptism, which now saves you” (1 Peter 3:21, NET)

Peter immediately distinguishes Baptism from ordinary removal of dirt and connects its saving significance with Christ's resurrection. Noah's eight-person household had been brought safely through water; Peter now addresses his Christian readers about Baptism.

The preceding verses concern Christ suffering for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring us to God. The comparison is enclosed within Christ's saving work. Water does not become a power independent of him.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The sentence's positive assertion about Baptism should be allowed to stand. Peter does not say that Baptism merely depicts salvation while remaining unrelated to its giving. He names its saving benefit and explains its connection to the risen Christ.

The Small Catechism likewise distinguishes Baptism from plain water by God's command and Word. It attributes the gift to that Word, with faith trusting the promise. The sacrament is neither an ordinary bath nor a human achievement that competes with grace.

At the same time, the conscience phrase in 1 Peter 3:21 deserves careful reading. Translations differ between an appeal and a pledge or response. [Eperōtēma](/greek/eperotema/) treats that question directly. The disputed phrase does not authorize deleting the assertion about Baptism or the concluding reference to Christ's resurrection.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Calling the relationship symbolic can be misleading if *symbolic* is taken to mean unreal or ineffective. Biblical correspondence may join real events and real gifts.

Another mistake assigns an imagined meaning to every feature of Noah's story. Peter gives a particular comparison, not permission to make every plank, animal, and measurement into a separate sacramental doctrine.

## So What

Read the paragraph from Christ's suffering through his resurrection and heavenly authority. Let that whole movement explain the Baptism in which his promise comes to you.

You need not choose between Christ's saving work and his baptismal gift. Peter locates the gift within that work and directs the conscience to the risen Lord.
