The English word *charisma* now means personal magnetism — the quality that makes a room turn when someone enters. Ask a modern Christian what a *spiritual gift* is and you will usually get something adjacent: an aptitude, a bent, a capacity discovered by looking inward, perhaps with the help of an inventory.

The Pastorals use the Greek word twice, and both times it arrives from outside the man, through other people's hands, in public.

Timothy did not find his *charisma*. He was given it, at an identifiable moment, by a group of men who put their hands on him while something was said aloud. Paul can refer to the event as a fact — *the spiritual gift you have, given to you and confirmed by prophetic words when the elders laid hands on you*. There is a date. There were witnesses.

That is a considerably stranger idea than personal magnetism, and it is the older one.

## The Word

The Greek word is χάρισμα (*charisma*), pronounced in the Erasmian convention as *KHA-ris-ma*, with the accent on the first syllable. It is a third-declension neuter noun.

The formation is the key to the meaning. It is built on *charis* (χάρις), *grace, favor*, with the suffix *-ma*, which in Greek marks the **result or product** of an action. A *poiēma* is a thing made; a *ktisma* is a thing created; a *charisma* is **a thing graced** — the concrete outcome of favor shown. It cannot mean an earned capacity, because the noun's own morphology says where it came from.

The word family:

*Charis* (χάρις) — *grace, favor*. The root, and the New Testament's central term for God's disposition toward sinners.

*Charizomai* (χαρίζομαι) — *to give freely, to forgive*. Romans 8:32; Colossians 2:13.

*Dōrea*, *dōron*, *doma* — the other New Testament gift-words. Worth distinguishing: these name a gift without specifying its ground. *Charisma* names a gift and says in its own letters that grace is why.

*Epithesis tōn cheirōn* (ἐπίθεσις τῶν χειρῶν) — *the laying on of hands*. Not a cognate, but the phrase attached to *charisma* in both Pastoral occurrences. Also 1 Timothy 5:22 (do not be hasty about it), Acts 6:6; 13:3, and Hebrews 6:2, where it appears in a list of foundational teachings.

*Anazōpyreō* (ἀναζωπυρέω) — *to rekindle, to fan into flame*. 2 Timothy 1:6, a New Testament hapax. The compound is *ana* (again) + *zōos* (living) + *pyr* (fire).

**Distribution.** Seventeen New Testament occurrences. All are Paul's except 1 Peter 4:10. Two are in these letters.

## Range of Meaning

*The gift of salvation itself.* Romans 5:15–16 and, most memorably, Romans 6:23: the wages of sin is death, but the *charisma* of God is eternal life. The word's largest use, and the one that governs the rest — whatever else a *charisma* is, it belongs to the same category as eternal life freely given.

*A deliverance or benefit received.* 2 Corinthians 1:11, of Paul's rescue from deadly peril, for which many will give thanks.

*God's irrevocable gifts to Israel.* Romans 11:29 — the *charismata* and the calling of God are without repentance.

*A capacity given for the church's use.* Romans 12:6; 1 Corinthians 12:4, 9, 28, 30, 31; 1 Peter 4:10. The sense most modern readers assume is the only one.

*A condition of life.* 1 Corinthians 7:7, where Paul says each has his own *charisma* from God, one of one kind and one of another — and the subject under discussion is celibacy and marriage. A state of life is called a gift of grace. This single verse does more to unsettle the modern usage than any argument, because nobody discovers celibacy on an inventory.

*The gift conferred for office.* 1 Timothy 4:14; 2 Timothy 1:6. The Pastoral use.

## Where You’ll Meet It

*1 Timothy 4:14.* "Do not neglect the gift that is in you, which was given you through the prophecy spoken over you at the laying on of the hands of the elders."

Three elements: the gift, prophecy, and the hands of the *presbyterion* — the council of elders acting as a body. And a warning: *do not neglect*. The gift can be neglected without ceasing to have been given.

*2 Timothy 1:6.* "For this reason I remind you to fan into flame the gift of God, which is in you through the laying on of my hands."

The same event, described later and from Paul's side. *Anazōpyrein* — fan it back into flame. The image concedes that a real gift can go cold, and insists that going cold is not the same as not having it. That is a pastorally enormous distinction for anyone who has ever wondered whether a call was genuine because it no longer feels like one.

**A small honest observation about the prepositions.** First Timothy 4:14 says the gift was given *meta* — *with* — the laying on of the elders' hands. Second Timothy 1:6 says it is in Timothy *dia* — *through* — the laying on of Paul's hands. *Meta* accompanies; *dia* instruments.

The difference is worth noting and not worth over-building. It has been used to argue that the elders merely attended while Paul actually conferred, which is more weight than two prepositions in two letters will carry. The plainer reading is that Paul acted together with the elders, and describes the same event from two vantage points — his own hands in the letter where he is remembering, the council's in the letter where he is charging Timothy about the office. But a study should say that the words differ, because they do, and because the reader will not see it in English.

*Romans 6:23.* "For the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." The contrast is between *opsōnia* — a soldier's wages, what is owed — and *charisma*. Earnings on one side, grace-gift on the other. Everything the word does elsewhere is anchored here.

*1 Corinthians 12:4–7.* "There are different gifts, but the same Spirit. … to each one the manifestation of the Spirit is given for the common good." Note the purpose clause: *pros to sympheron* — for what is advantageous, to the whole. A *charisma* in Paul is never described as being for the recipient's fulfilment.

*1 Peter 4:10.* "As good stewards of the manifold grace of God, each of you should use whatever gift he has received to serve one another." *Oikonomoi* — stewards, household managers of what belongs to someone else. The same theme of stewardship explored in [Paratheke](/greek/paratheke/), arriving from a different apostle.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear


*The gift comes from outside, and that is the whole Lutheran instinct.* Confessional Lutheran theology is characteristically suspicious of anything located in the believer's interior as the ground of assurance — feelings, experiences, discovered aptitudes, the sense of having been called. Not because interiors are unreal, but because they fluctuate and cannot be appealed to when they do.

The *charisma* of the Pastorals is *extra nos* in the same way baptism is. It was given at a time, by people, with words, in public. When Timothy doubts, Paul does not tell him to examine whether the gift is really there. He tells him to remember the hands.

This is the same logic the Small Catechism applies to baptism, and it is why the two belong in the same theology. A Christian troubled about his standing is pointed to water and a word; a minister troubled about his call is pointed to hands and a word. Neither is asked to look inward for evidence.

*Ordination, and how far the Confessions will go.* Augsburg Confession XIV requires a rightly ordered call for public teaching and administration, and the Apology addresses the status of ordination directly: if ordination is understood with reference to the ministry of the Word, the Lutherans are willing to call it a sacrament, since the ministry of the Word has God's command and glorious promises.

That is a carefully placed sentence and it does two things at once. It refuses the medieval account, in which ordination confers an indelible character making a man a sacrificing priest — see [*presbyteros*](/greek/presbyteros/). And it declines to reduce ordination to a congregation's administrative arrangement, because the New Testament attaches a *charisma* to those hands.

## So What

*A gift for the church, not for the man.* Every Pauline use points outward — for the common good, to serve one another, for what is advantageous. The Pastorals sharpen it: the *charisma* is given so that Timothy can do a job with the deposit, and the surrounding verses are all about teaching, reading Scripture publicly, and watching his life and his doctrine.

The modern practice of identifying one's gifts in order to find a fulfilling place to serve is not wrong so much as inverted. In these letters the need comes first, the gift is given for it, and the man is told not to neglect it — which implies that neglect is the likelier failure, and that the gift will sometimes feel like an obligation rather than a fit. That is a more durable account of Christian service than one resting on the discovery of aptitude, and it is a kinder one to the many faithful people whose service has never felt like a natural fit at all.
