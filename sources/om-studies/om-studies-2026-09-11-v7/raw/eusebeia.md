Ask an English-speaking Christian what *godliness* means and you will get a description of behavior. Modesty, restraint, prayerfulness, a certain quietness. It is a word about how a person carries himself, and it has acquired a faint whiff of the antique — the sort of thing said about someone's grandmother, admiringly and from a distance.

Then you reach 1 Timothy 3:16, where Paul says that the mystery of *eusebeia* is great, and stops to quote a hymn.

The hymn is not about behavior. It has six lines and every one of them is about Christ: revealed in flesh, vindicated in spirit, seen by angels, proclaimed among nations, believed in the world, taken up in glory. Whatever *eusebeia* is, Paul's definition of its mystery is a short creed about the incarnation and its aftermath.

That is the discovery this word offers. In the Pastorals, godliness has **content before it has conduct**. It is not first a way of living that a Christian adopts; it is first something true about God that a Christian has been told. The conduct follows — the letters are emphatic that it does — but it follows, and the order is not decorative.

Which is also why the same letters can speak of a *form* of godliness with the power denied, and of men who suppose godliness is a way to make money. A word that meant only behavior could not be counterfeited so precisely. A word with content can be.

## The Word

The Greek word is εὐσέβεια (*eusebeia*), pronounced in the Erasmian convention as *yoo-SEB-ay-a*, with the accent on the second syllable. It is a first-declension feminine noun.

The compound is *eu* (εὖ), *well*, plus the root of *sebomai* (σέβομαι), *to revere, to worship, to stand in awe of*. To have *eusebeia* is to revere *well* — rightly, fittingly, in the manner the object of reverence deserves.

This is worth pausing on, because it explains something about how the word behaves. *Eusebeia* was not a Christian coinage or even a distinctively religious one. It was the ordinary Greek and Hellenistic word for **proper reverence** as a civic and domestic virtue: reverence toward the gods, yes, but also toward parents, toward the dead, toward the city. Its Latin equivalent is *pietas*, and Roman *pietas* is exactly the same shape — Aeneas is *pius* not because he is devout but because he carries his father out of Troy. The word names an ordered set of obligations rightly kept.

The New Testament borrows a pagan virtue word and refills it. The container is Hellenistic; the contents are 1 Timothy 3:16.

The word family:

*Eusebēs* (εὐσεβής) — the adjective, *godly, devout*. Used of Cornelius the centurion (Acts 10:2, 7) and at 2 Peter 2:9.

*Eusebeō* (εὐσεβέω) — the verb, *to show reverence*. Two occurrences, and they are instructively different. At Acts 17:23 Paul tells the Athenians that what they *revere* in ignorance he now proclaims — the pagan sense, used without scorn. At 1 Timothy 5:4 children and grandchildren are to *show godliness* toward their own household by caring for aging parents. The domestic obligation is not an illustration of godliness. It is named as godliness.

*Eusebōs* (εὐσεβῶς) — the adverb, *in a godly manner*. 2 Timothy 3:12 and Titus 2:12.

*Theosebeia* (θεοσέβεια) — *reverence toward God*, specifying the object. 1 Timothy 2:10, of women who profess it. The adjective *theosebēs* at John 9:31.

*Sebomai* (σέβομαι) and *sebastos* (σεβαστός) — the root verb, and the adjective meaning *revered*, which was the Greek rendering of the imperial title *Augustus* (Acts 25:21, 25). The vocabulary of reverence was also the vocabulary of empire, which gives the Pastorals' insistence on *one* God and *one* mediator a sharper edge than it has in English.

*Asebeia* (ἀσέβεια) — the negation, *ungodliness*. Comparatively rare in Paul, but it saturates Jude and 2 Peter, which are the New Testament's other home for this word family.

*Deisidaimonia* (δεισιδαιμονία) — the neighbor word, *religiosity* or *superstition*, depending on who is speaking. Acts 25:19. Greek had a word for reverence that had gone wrong, and it is not this one.

**The distribution is the argument.** The noun *eusebeia* occurs fifteen times in the New Testament. Ten are in these three letters. Four are in 2 Peter. One is in Acts. That is the entire spread. Paul never uses the word in Romans, Galatians, Corinthians, Ephesians, Philippians, or Colossians — and then uses it ten times in three short letters to two men. Whatever these letters are doing, this word is central to it.

## Range of Meaning

*Reverence toward God — the general sense.* The base meaning, and the one that governs most occurrences. 1 Timothy 2:2, where Christians pray for rulers so that they may lead a peaceful and quiet life in all godliness and dignity. 1 Timothy 4:7–8, where Timothy is to train himself for godliness, which holds promise for the present life and the one to come.

*The content of the faith itself.* 1 Timothy 3:16, where *eusebeia* has a *mystery* which is then quoted as a hymn about Christ. Titus 1:1, where the knowledge of the truth is *according to godliness* — *kat' eusebeian*. In these uses the word points to the substance of Christian confession rather than to Christian behavior. English translations have no good way to signal the shift, and most readers pass through 1 Timothy 3:16 without noticing that the word has changed weight.

*Godly conduct in ordinary vocation.* 1 Timothy 5:4 — caring for one's own aging household. This is the least glamorous use and one of the most important. Godliness is discharged, in that verse, by an adult child paying a debt to a parent.

*Godliness as a commodity.* 1 Timothy 6:5, of those who suppose that godliness is a *means of gain* — *porismon*, a business term. Paul's reply in 6:6 is one of the great turns in the letters: godliness *is* great gain, with contentment. He does not deny the premise. He redefines the profit.

*Godliness as a shape without a substance.* 2 Timothy 3:5 — *morphōsis eusebeias*, having the outward form of godliness while denying its power. *Morphōsis* is a real form, not a false one; the same noun at Romans 2:20 means the embodiment of knowledge in the law. The problem is not that the form is fake. The problem is that it has been detached from what animates it.

## Where You’ll Meet It

*1 Timothy 3:16.* "By common confession, the mystery of godliness is great: He appeared in the flesh, was vindicated by the Spirit, was seen by angels, was proclaimed among the nations, was believed in throughout the world, was taken up in glory." The hymn is almost certainly pre-Pauline — six lines, rhythmically balanced, each opening with a third-person aorist passive verb. Christ is the subject of every line and the agent of none. Everything in it is done *to* him or *about* him. If the mystery of godliness is this, then godliness begins as something received rather than performed.

> **Text-critical note.** This verse contains the most significant variant in the Pastoral Epistles. Where Nestle’s 1904 text reads *hos* (ὅς, “He appeared in the flesh”), Robinson–Pierpont and the Textus Receptus edition included here read *theos* (θεός, "God was manifest in the flesh"), while a smaller group reads the neuter *ho* (ὅ), agreeing grammatically with "mystery." The variant is easy to account for: in the manuscripts *theos* was routinely abbreviated as a *nomen sacrum* — ΘΣ with a line above — which is one stroke away from ΟΣ. A copyist's eye, or a copyist's theology, could move it in either direction, and both accidental and deliberate change have been proposed.
>
> What matters for the reader is proportion. The doctrine of the incarnation does not rest on this reading. On the critical text, "who" still refers to Christ, and the line still says that he was revealed in the flesh — which is the incarnation, stated plainly, in a verse that nobody disputes. A doctrine that would fall if a variant went the other way was never resting on Scripture; it was resting on one letter. See **Ad Fontes** for the manuscript evidence and the method.

*1 Timothy 4:7–8.* "train yourself for godliness. For physical exercise is of limited value, but godliness is valuable in every way, holding promise for the present life and for the one to come" The verb is *gymnaze* — the gymnasium word, from which English gets *gymnastics*. Godliness is trained into a person the way a body is trained, by repetition over time. Note that this sits comfortably beside everything said above: content received does not exclude discipline practiced. It orders it.

*1 Timothy 6:5–6.* "and constant friction between men of depraved mind who are devoid of the truth. These men regard godliness as a means of gain. Of course, godliness with contentment is great gain." The counterfeit and the correction in consecutive verses. The false teacher's error is not that he wants gain; it is that he has misidentified where the gain is kept.

*2 Timothy 3:5.* "having a form of godliness but denying its power. Turn away from such as these!" The pastoral center of the whole study. This is a description of people who look right. It appears at the end of a long list of vices, which makes the placement pointed: the list does not culminate in scandal but in respectability.

*2 Timothy 3:12.* "Indeed, all who desire to live godly lives in Christ Jesus will be persecuted," *Eusebōs*, the adverb. Placed seven verses after the form-without-power warning, and it functions as the test that distinguishes them. The counterfeit costs nothing.

*Titus 2:11–12.* "For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation to everyone. It instructs us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live sensible, upright, and godly lives in the present age," The clearest statement in the letters of the relationship between the two. Grace appeared; grace trains. The training is real and it is downstream. The verb is *paideuousa* — the schoolmaster word.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear


*The order is not negotiable.* Titus 2:11–12 gives the whole confessional Lutheran doctrine of sanctification in two sentences and in the right sequence: grace appeared, and it trains. Not — grace will appear if we are trained. The Formula of Concord treats good works with a kind of anxious precision that can look excessive until you notice what it is protecting: works are necessary, and they are necessary *as fruit*, and the moment they are made necessary *for* salvation the gospel has been converted into something else. Article IV of the Formula fights over prepositions because prepositions are where this goes wrong.

*Eusebeia* is a useful test case because it is so plainly a behavior word in English. If godliness in the Pastorals had only ever meant conduct, the letters would sit awkwardly beside Romans and Galatians. That 1 Timothy 3:16 anchors the word in a hymn about Christ is not a curiosity. It is the letters telling the reader where to start.

## Where People Get It Wrong

*The counterfeit is respectable.* Second Timothy 3:5 describes people with the outward form and no power, and it should be read as addressed to the church rather than about the world. Lutheran theology has a specific name for the mechanism: the confusion of law and gospel, in which sanctification is preached as though it produced justification, and the result is people who have the shape of Christian life without the thing that makes it alive. The shape can be maintained for decades. That is what makes it dangerous, and it is why the Confessions keep returning to the terrified conscience — because a conscience that has been given the form and denied the power will eventually notice, and what it does then is a pastoral emergency.

The corollary is uncomfortable for a certain kind of confessional zeal: doctrinal precision is itself available as a *morphōsis*. A congregation can hold every article correctly and deny the power, and the letters do not exempt the orthodox from the diagnosis.

## So What

*Godliness is discharged in vocation, not in a religious sphere.* First Timothy 5:4 says that grown children show *eusebeia* by caring for their parents. Not *demonstrate* godliness, not *apply* it — the verb makes the care itself the reverence. This is Luther's doctrine of vocation with the apostolic vocabulary attached: the ordinary offices of life are where God is served, and the milkmaid's work is not a lesser stage on which to display a piety generated somewhere holier.

For the reader who came to the Lutheran Confessions from a tradition that measured godliness by devotional intensity, this is the sentence to sit with. The Pastorals' word for reverence toward God includes paying your mother's bills.
