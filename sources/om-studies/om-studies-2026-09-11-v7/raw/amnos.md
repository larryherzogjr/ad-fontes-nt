John the Baptist points to Jesus and calls him the Lamb of God. It is easy to hear only softness in that title. The passages that use it lead us instead to sin, blood, and a death that brings deliverance.

The Lamb is gentle, but gentleness does not exhaust what John announces.

## The Word

**ἀμνός** (*amnos*) means *lamb*. Its four New Testament occurrences are John 1:29 and 36, Acts 8:32, and 1 Peter 1:19. In each setting the word is applied to Christ, directly or through Scripture explained as speaking of him.

The noun itself names an animal. Its saving significance comes from the biblical account in which the Lamb bears sin, sheds blood, and redeems.

## Range of Meaning

Acts 8 quotes Isaiah's picture of a lamb silent before its shearer. First Peter describes Christ's blood as that of an unblemished and spotless lamb. John's announcement identifies the Lamb by what he does about the world's sin.

Revelation usually uses a different word, **ἀρνίον** (*arnion*), for the Lamb. That lexical distinction does not separate its Savior from John's. Revelation 5 portrays the Lamb who was slain and who has purchased people for God by his blood. Different words participate in the same larger witness to Christ.

## Where You’ll Meet It

John announces:

> “The next day John saw Jesus coming toward him and said, ‘Look, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!’” (John 1:29, BSB)

Acts 8:32 takes us to Isaiah 53, where the servant suffers for the sins of others. Philip begins with that passage and proclaims Jesus. First Peter 1:18–19 speaks of redemption accomplished by Christ's precious blood.

The Passover also belongs to this scriptural background. In Exodus 12, the lamb's blood marks the households spared from judgment. Paul explicitly calls Christ our sacrificed Passover lamb in 1 Corinthians 5:7. These connections come from Scripture's use of the image, not from assuming that every lamb in every passage means the same thing.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Agnus Dei, the Lamb of God sung in the liturgy, confesses who is giving himself in the Supper. The congregation addresses the Savior who took away sin through his sacrifice and who now gives his body and blood according to his promise.

We are not asking him to die again. We receive the fruits of the sacrifice he has accomplished. [Teleō](/greek/teleo/) follows the language of completion at the cross; [Hilaskomai](/greek/hilaskomai/) connects the prayer for mercy with Christ's atoning work.

The song therefore brings together confession and petition. The one named as the Lamb is the one from whom we ask mercy and peace.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Innocence and meekness belong to the biblical picture. The error is making them the whole picture, so that Jesus becomes an example of harmless behavior while his blood and the removal of sin disappear.

Neither the Passover nor Isaiah's servant can be reduced to that sentiment. The Lamb gives his life for others. His gentleness serves a costly and effective rescue.

## So What

When the congregation sings to the Lamb, your need for mercy is already being named. You do not have to supply a gentleness or innocence of your own before approaching his promise.

The Lamb has taken your sin seriously enough to give himself for it. His gifts at the altar direct you to that accomplished mercy.
