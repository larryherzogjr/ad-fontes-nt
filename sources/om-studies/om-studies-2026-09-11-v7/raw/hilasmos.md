When conscience asks whether Christ's death is enough, John answers by pointing to Christ himself. The answer does not begin with the strength of our sorrow or the quality of our faith. It begins with the one who stands before the Father for sinners.

## The Word

**ἱλασμός** (*hilasmos*) is a noun used for atonement or an atoning sacrifice. Its two New Testament occurrences are 1 John 2:2 and 4:10. English translations sometimes use *propitiation* or *expiation*, terms that emphasize the dealing with divine wrath or the removal of sin.

Those English distinctions can help identify questions, but the Greek noun should not be forced to choose between everything Scripture says about reconciliation. John names Jesus as the remedy for our sins within a passage about his advocacy with the Father.

## Range of Meaning

The word belongs to the language of dealing with sin before God. It does not mean that sin was imaginary or that God decided it did not matter. Nor does it describe our attempt to persuade an unwilling God to become loving.

First John 4:10 locates the initiative in God's love: he sent his Son. The Son's saving work is the expression of that love. It is not an intervention that turns the Father from an enemy into someone the Son must reluctantly convince.

## Where You’ll Meet It

John calls Jesus:

> “He Himself is the atoning sacrifice for our sins, and not only for ours but also for the sins of the whole world.” (1 John 2:2, BSB)

The sentence continues beyond the sins of John's immediate readers to the whole world. The passage also urges them not to sin and directs anyone who does sin to Jesus Christ, the righteous advocate. Consolation and the call to abandon sin belong together.

In 1 John 4:7–11, the same saving gift grounds love for one another. God's action precedes ours. Christians love because they have been loved, rather than loving in order to create a reason for God to send his Son.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Christ's atonement is sufficient for every sinner. We do not need to discover a hidden limitation in the invitation before telling someone that Christ died for them. John's language gives troubled people a Savior to whom they may turn.

Augsburg Confession IV locates forgiveness and righteousness in Christ's suffering for us, received through faith rather than earned through our merits or satisfactions. That order protects the comfort of this passage. Faith receives the benefit of Christ's work; it does not improve the sacrifice until God accepts it.

Universal atonement is not universal salvation. John does not erase the distinction between faith and unbelief or excuse a life that rejects Christ's Word. The scope of the sacrifice and the reception of its benefits are related questions, but they are not interchangeable.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Reducing atonement to a moving example leaves the sinner without the very remedy John names. Turning it into permission to persist in sin ignores his opening purpose. Restricting the whole world's atonement to people who already believe also takes away the breadth of the sentence.

## So What

When your sins are specific and your regret is real, do not settle for the vague thought that God is generally kind. Hear what his kindness has done: he has given his Son for sinners, including you.

[Hilastērion](/greek/hilasterion/) explores the related language in Romans. Here John places the comfort in a person: Jesus Christ, your advocate.
