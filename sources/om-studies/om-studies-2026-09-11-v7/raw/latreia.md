Paul urges Christians to offer their bodies to God because of God's mercies. That order matters: mercy comes first, and the life offered in response is real worship. We lose something essential if we reverse the order or deny the response.

## The Word

**λατρεία** (*latreia*) means *service* or *worship*, especially service directed toward God. The related verb **λατρεύω** (*latreuō*) means *to serve* or *to worship*.

The noun does not itself mean *God serving us*. When Lutherans explain the Divine Service by beginning with God's gifts to the congregation, they are making a theological statement about worship's foundation, not translating this Greek word in reverse.

## Range of Meaning

Romans 9:4 includes worship among Israel's gifts. Hebrews 9:1 and 9:6 uses *latreia* in describing the first covenant's worship and the priests' service in the tent.

John 16:2 gives a sobering use: Jesus warns that people who kill his disciples will think they are offering service to God. Calling an action worship does not establish that God commands or accepts it. Sincerity cannot make persecution holy.

Romans 12:1 uses the word as Paul turns to the lives believers offer in response to mercy. The scope includes their bodies and daily conduct, rather than only the moments in which a congregation assembles.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul appeals to believers:

> “Therefore I urge you, brothers, on account of God’s mercy, to offer your bodies as living sacrifices, holy and pleasing to God, which is your spiritual service of worship.” (Romans 12:1, BSB)

He calls this their reasonable service. His next sentence speaks of a renewed mind and discerning God's will. What follows addresses life within the body, the use of gifts, love, patience, hospitality, and conduct toward enemies.

The appeal begins with God's mercies. Paul's account of the Christian response rests on the salvation he has proclaimed, rather than introducing a new method of earning God's favor through dedication.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Apology XXIV.18–25 distinguishes God's promised gift in a sacrament from a sacrifice offered to honor him. Christ's atoning sacrifice reconciles sinners to God; the sacrifices of thanksgiving come from people already reconciled. Praise, prayer, confession, and good works have a proper place as this response.

That distinction explains why Lutheran worship gives priority to receiving the Word and sacraments. God addresses sinners with forgiveness for Christ's sake. The congregation then truly prays, praises, and gives thanks. Receiving his gifts does not make its response unreal or unnecessary.

The same order reaches ordinary vocation. Work done faithfully, a neighbor helped, and forgiveness extended can belong to the life offered to God. They do not repeat Christ's sacrifice or supply something missing from his reconciliation.

## Where People Get It Wrong

One mistake makes worship an achievement that persuades God to be gracious. Beautiful music, heartfelt prayer, and disciplined service can all be good, but none purchases his mercy.

Another mistake hears the priority of God's giving as though Christians had nothing to offer in response. Paul expressly calls for an offered life. The distinction concerns what that offering does: it honors the God who has shown mercy rather than causing him to become merciful.

## So What

Come to worship ready to receive what Christ promises and to answer with prayer and praise. You do not need to manufacture the feeling that makes his Word effective.

Then carry that response into the work and relationships before you. The body you present to God is the body with which you care for your neighbor. His mercy gives both the reason and the freedom to serve.
