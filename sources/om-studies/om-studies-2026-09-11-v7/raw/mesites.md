Job wanted one. In the middle of his argument with God he says the thing that the whole book is straining toward: there is no umpire between us, no one to lay his hand on us both. Two parties, an unbridgeable difference in kind, and nobody standing between them who belongs to both sides.

That is the position a *mesitēs* occupies. The word is not poetic. In ordinary Hellenistic Greek it named a real office — the arbitrator in a dispute, the guarantor who stood surety for a contract, the neutral third party who held the deposit while two others transacted. A *mesitēs* was somebody you hired, and the job had a job description: he must be trusted by both parties, and he must be able to bind both.

First Timothy 2:5 takes that commercial and legal word and says there is one of them.

And then it says something stranger. Having established that there is one God, and one *mesitēs* between God and human beings, Paul names him — and the name comes with a noun attached. *Anthrōpos Christos Iēsous*. The mediator is a **human being**.

Not, notably, *anēr* — the word for a male as distinct from a female — but *anthrōpos*, the word for a member of the human race. The same word Paul has just used for the party on the far side of the gap. Whatever mediation required, it required somebody who was one of us.

## The Word

The Greek word is μεσίτης (*mesitēs*), pronounced in the Erasmian convention as *mes-EE-tays*, with the accent on the second syllable. It is a first-declension masculine noun.

It derives from *mesos* (μέσος), *middle*, and the agent suffix *-tēs* — literally *a middle-man*, in the neutral sense the English compound once had before commerce ruined it. The related verb *mesiteuō* (μεσιτεύω) appears once, at Hebrews 6:17, where God *guaranteed* his promise with an oath.

Six occurrences in the New Testament: Galatians 3:19 and 3:20; 1 Timothy 2:5; Hebrews 8:6, 9:15, and 12:24.

The word family and its neighbors:

*Mesos* (μέσος) — *middle, in the midst*. Common.

*Mesiteuō* (μεσιτεύω) — *to act as guarantor, to interpose*. Hebrews 6:17.

*Paraklētos* (παράκλητος) — *advocate, one called alongside*. Not a cognate and not a synonym, but the closest functional relative. 1 John 2:1 says that if anyone sins, we have a *paraklētos* with the Father — Jesus Christ the righteous. John's Gospel uses the same word of the Spirit. The two words divide the work: the *mesitēs* stands between the parties; the *paraklētos* stands beside one of them and speaks.

*Engyos* (ἔγγυος) — *guarantor, surety*. Hebrews 7:22, of Jesus as guarantor of a better covenant. The commercial register again.

**Background.** In the papyri and in Hellenistic legal usage, *mesitēs* is unremarkable and technical: an arbitrator, a mediator between disputing parties, sometimes a broker or middleman in a sale, sometimes the trustee holding property during a transaction. The Septuagint does not use the noun for any figure of Israel's history, though the Greek tradition supplies it at Job 9:33 for the arbiter Job wishes existed. Judaism of the period did speak of Moses in mediatorial terms, and Galatians 3:19 assumes the reader will make that connection without being told.

## Range of Meaning

*Arbitrator or guarantor between parties — the ordinary sense.* Never used this way in the New Testament of an ordinary transaction, but it is the sense the readers brought to the word and the reason it works.

*Moses, mediator of the law.* Galatians 3:19–20. The law was ordained through angels *by an intermediary* — and then Paul makes an argument out of the noun itself: a mediator implies more than one party, but God is one. The logic is compressed and much disputed. Whatever else Paul means, he is exploiting the fact that a *mesitēs* is structurally a third party, which is precisely what makes the mediation of the law different from the promise given directly to Abraham.

*Christ, mediator of the new covenant.* Hebrews 8:6, 9:15, 12:24. In Hebrews the word is always paired with *diathēkē* — covenant — and always comparative: a better covenant, a new covenant. Hebrews is interested in Christ's mediation as the successor to a prior arrangement.

*Christ, mediator between God and humanity.* 1 Timothy 2:5. Distinct from Hebrews in its framing: not between two covenants but between two *parties*. This is the only place in the New Testament where the mediation is described in terms of the gap itself.

## Where You’ll Meet It

*1 Timothy 2:5–6.* "For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who gave Himself as a ransom for all—the testimony that was given at just the right time."

The Greek is spare to the point of severity: *heis gar theos, heis kai mesitēs theou kai anthrōpōn, anthrōpos Christos Iēsous.* No verbs. Two occurrences of *heis* — one — placed at the head of each clause. It reads like something quoted rather than composed, and it may well be.

Three observations.

First, the two occurrences of *heis* are doing different work. *One God* is the confession of Israel — the Shema, in its Greek form, would be immediately audible to any reader of the Septuagint. *One mediator* is the Christian addition, and it is placed in exact parallel so that the second inherits the exclusivity of the first.

Second, *anthrōpos* rather than *anēr*, twice: the parties on one side are *anthrōpōn*, and the mediator is *anthrōpos*. The mediator is a member of the class he mediates for. This is not incidental to the office — a *mesitēs* who belonged to only one party was useless in Hellenistic law, and the same logic operates here. (For the noun itself see [*anthrōpos*](/greek/anthropos/), where 1 Timothy 2:5 is treated at length.)

Third, verse 6 supplies what the mediator *did*: he gave himself as an [*antilytron*](/greek/antilytron/) for all. The mediation is not negotiation. It is a payment made by the go-between out of his own resources — which is precisely what no ordinary arbitrator does.

*1 Timothy 2:3–4.* The immediate context, and it cannot be separated. God our Savior "wants everyone to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth." The universal will stands two verses before the exclusive mediator, in the same argument, offered as its ground — *for* there is one God. Any reading that has to weaken one of the two has misread the paragraph, and the study should say so plainly in both directions.

*Galatians 3:19–20.* "Why then was the law given? It was added because of transgressions, until the arrival of the seed to whom the promise referred. It was administered through angels by a mediator. A mediator is unnecessary, however, for only one party; but God is one." The most difficult use of the word in the New Testament and the one commentators disagree about most. Include it for contrast rather than trying to settle it.

*Hebrews 9:15.* "Therefore Christ is the mediator of a new covenant, so that those who are called may receive the promised eternal inheritance, now that He has died to redeem them from the transgressions committed under the first covenant." The *inheritance* language links Hebrews to Titus 3:7, also discussed in [Soter](/greek/soter/).

*Job 9:33.* "Nor is there a mediator between us, to lay his hand upon us both." The background wish. Worth quoting because the New Testament's answer is so exactly shaped to the question.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear


*The exclusive particle.* The Reformation's *solus Christus* has no clearer single text. The Apology of the Augsburg Confession, in its long article on the invocation of saints, turns directly on this verse: Christ alone is the mediator, propitiator, high priest, and intercessor, and Scripture nowhere directs prayer to any other.

The confessional argument deserves to be stated accurately, including the part where it is careful. The Confessions do not deny that Christians on earth pray for one another, nor do they attack the honoring of saints as examples and as evidence of God's mercy — the Augsburg Confession explicitly permits remembering the saints in order to strengthen faith and imitate their callings. What is refused is the invocation of any figure as mediator, on the ground that the office has one occupant.

Rome's reply is available and should be given fairly: the Roman position distinguishes Christ's unique mediation of redemption from the subordinate intercession of the saints, which is held to depend on his mediation rather than compete with it. The Lutheran counter is not that Roman theology is unaware of the distinction, but that the distinction does not survive contact with popular devotion, and that the New Testament nowhere models the practice. That is an argument about consequences and about silence, and it should be presented as such rather than as an exegetical knockout.

*Mediation required both natures, and this is why the Christology matters.* The Formula of Concord's article on the person of Christ is one of the most technical stretches of the Lutheran Confessions, and readers sometimes wonder what is at stake. First Timothy 2:5 is what is at stake. If the mediator is not truly God, he has nothing to give that would satisfy God. If he is not truly *anthrōpos*, he does not belong to the party he represents and his obedience is not reckoned to us. The two natures are not a metaphysical curiosity attached to a simpler gospel. They are the structural requirements of the office named in this verse.

## So What

*The mediator is the one who wants the mediation.* A go-between is usually hired by a party who wants a settlement. Verse 4 says who wants it: God our Savior, who desires all to be saved. The mediator does not stand between an angry God and a merciful Son, extracting concessions. He is *sent*, by the God whose will verse 4 describes, and he is himself God.

This is worth pressing for readers formed by a certain kind of atonement preaching, in which the Father is the obstacle and the Son the solution. The Pastorals will not permit it. The same paragraph that names the mediator names God as *Savior* — a title these letters give to the Father and the Son with almost equal frequency, six times for the Father and four for the Son across the three letters. The mediation is not a rescue *from* God. It is a rescue *by* him.
