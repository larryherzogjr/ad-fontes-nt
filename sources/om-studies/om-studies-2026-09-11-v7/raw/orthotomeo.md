The traditional phrase *rightly dividing* has shaped how many readers hear 2 Timothy 2:15. The BSB instead speaks of “the word of truth.” That wording brings the question into focus: what kind of work does *orthotomeō* ask the teacher to do?
<!-- /APPROVED ADAPTATION -->

Almost every English-speaking Christian over a certain age knows the phrase, and almost everyone who knows it has a picture attached. The picture is of the Bible as something to be *divided* — sorted into parts, separated into categories, its contents distributed into their proper compartments by a reader skilled enough to know which piece goes where. Whole systems of interpretation have been built on that picture, and it has been printed on study Bible covers and taught in Sunday schools for a century and a half.

The verb does not mean divide.

It means to cut a straight line. It is a road-builder's word, or a ploughman's, or a stonemason's — the image is of doing a job accurately, cutting true, not wandering off the line. The object being cut is not being cut *into pieces*. It is being cut *straight*.

That is a small correction with large consequences, and this study is going to make it. It is also going to make a second correction in the same breath, and the second one is aimed closer to home.

## The Word

The Greek word is ὀρθοτομέω (*orthotomeō*), pronounced in the Erasmian convention as *or-tho-to-MEH-o*, with the accent on the fourth syllable. It is a compound verb, and at 2 Timothy 2:15 it appears as a present active participle, *orthotomounta*.

Two elements. *Orthos* (ὀρθός) means *straight, upright, correct* — English gets *orthodox*, *orthopedic*, *orthography* from it. *Temnō* (τέμνω) means *to cut*, and gives English the *-tomy* of *anatomy* and *appendectomy*.

*Orthotomeō* is a New Testament hapax legomenon: 2 Timothy 2:15, once, and nowhere else.

The word family:

*Orthos* (ὀρθός) — the adjective. Acts 14:10, where the lame man is told to stand *upright*; Hebrews 12:13, make *straight* paths for your feet.

*Orthopodeō* (ὀρθοποδέω) — *to walk straight*. Galatians 2:14, where Peter and the others are not walking straight with respect to the truth of the gospel. The construction is instructive: Paul uses an *orthos*- compound to describe conduct that fails to track the gospel accurately. Straightness in this family is about *correspondence*, not about division.

*Orthotomia* — the noun form, not in the New Testament.

**The Septuagint background is decisive, and it is not obscure.** The verb appears twice in the Greek Proverbs, both times of *paths*. Proverbs 3:6: acknowledge God in all your ways, and he will *make your paths straight*. Proverbs 11:5: righteousness *cuts straight* the ways of the blameless.

That is the sense a Greek-reading Jew would have brought to the word. It is about laying out a road correctly — cutting a way through terrain so that it runs true rather than meandering. Nothing in it suggests partition.

**The versions confirm it.** Jerome's Latin renders the phrase *recte tractantem verbum veritatis* — *rightly handling* the word of truth. The English "rightly dividing" enters through Tyndale and reaches its permanent form in the King James, where *divide* was a live rendering of *tractare* in a sense English has since lost. The translators were not careless; the word has moved under them. But the picture the modern reader forms from "dividing" is not the picture the Greek forms, and after four centuries the phrase has done work that its Greek cannot support.

## Range of Meaning

*To cut a straight path.* The Septuagint sense, and almost certainly the controlling one.

*To handle correctly, to deal rightly with.* The extension that most modern translations adopt: *accurately handling* (NASB), *correctly handling* (NIV), *teaching the message of truth accurately* (BSB). All of these are attempts to render a metaphor of accurate workmanship without importing a specific trade.

*To divide into parts.* Not attested for this verb, and not what it means.

The metaphor's exact trade — road-builder, ploughman, stonemason, tentmaker cutting cloth — is undeterminable and the study should not pretend otherwise. Commentators have proposed all four, sometimes with elaborate application, and the elaborations tend to say more about the commentator than the verb. What is determinable is the *shape* of the metaphor: a workman, a material, a line, and the possibility of getting it wrong.

## Where You’ll Meet It

*2 Timothy 2:15.* "Make every effort to present yourself approved to God, an unashamed workman who accurately handles the word of truth."

The context is the whole argument. This verse does not appear in a discussion of hermeneutics. It appears in the middle of a passage about *how people talk*.

Verse 14: warn them before God not to *fight about words* — *logomachein* — which does no good and ruins the hearers. Verse 16: avoid *empty chatter*, which leads people into more ungodliness. Verse 17: their message will spread like *gangrene*. Verse 18: Hymenaeus and Philetus, who have said the resurrection has already happened and are overturning the faith of some.

Cutting straight is what the workman does *instead of* word-fighting and empty noise. The contrast is not between careful exegesis and sloppy exegesis. It is between a man doing an honest job with the message and a man using it for something else. The word Paul reaches for is *ergatēs* — a labourer, a workman, a man who is paid for output — and the standard is that he not be *ashamed* of the work when it is inspected.

*Proverbs 3:6 (Septuagint).* “in all your ways acknowledge Him, and He will make your paths straight.” The background text, and the verb's other life.

*Galatians 2:14.* "…they were not walking in line with the truth of the gospel, I said to Cephas in front of them all, ‘If you, who are a Jew, live like a Gentile and not like a Jew, how can you compel the Gentiles to live like Jews?’" The *orthos*-compound applied to conduct. Straightness is the standard against which a line is measured, and the line in both places is *the truth of the gospel*.

## Where People Get It Wrong

*The correction to the dispensational reading.* "Rightly dividing" has been the textual anchor for a hermeneutic that divides Scripture into distinct administrations and assigns different portions to different peoples and eras. Whatever is to be said for or against that system on other grounds — and this site treats the question at length elsewhere — it cannot be founded on this verb. The verb is not about dividing Scripture; it is about doing accurate work with it. A system may be defensible without this text. It is not defensible *from* this text.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

*And the same correction runs the other way.* Lutherans have used this verse too. The proper distinction between law and gospel is the central hermeneutical commitment of confessional Lutheran theology, and 2 Timothy 2:15 has frequently been attached to it — in sermons, in classroom shorthand, in the English framing of the tradition's best-known treatment of the subject.

The distinction is true. It is not from this verse.

Saying so costs a convenient proof text and buys something worth more. The distinction of law and gospel rests where the Formula of Concord actually rests it: on the whole witness of Scripture to what the law does to a sinner and what the gospel does, on Romans and Galatians, on the plain evidence that God's Word addresses a person in two irreducibly different ways. Article V of the Formula makes its case from the function of the two words, not from a hapax legomenon in a passage about people who argue too much.

A study that corrected one tradition's misuse of a verse while quietly protecting its own would not be doing word study. It would be doing team sport, and readers can tell. The method of honest word study — flag the translation choice honestly rather than smoothing it over — is worth exactly as much as its willingness to apply the flag where it stings.

## So What

*The standard is a workman's, not a scholar's.* The pastoral payoff is in *ergatēs*. Paul's image for the man who handles the word of truth is not a sage or an interpreter but a labourer presenting finished work for inspection. The virtue named is not brilliance. It is *not being ashamed of the job* — a straight line, cut where it was supposed to go, by someone who did not take shortcuts on the parts nobody was going to check.

That is a description of an ordinary faithful pastor, and it is a rebuke to the two failures the surrounding verses name: the man who uses the text to win arguments, and the man who fills the time with noise. Neither is cutting straight. Both are working, and both will have their work looked at.
