Christ's two natures can sound like a remote question about theological vocabulary. It becomes very near when we say that the Son of God suffered for us. Who died? Was the suffering real? Did the divine nature become mortal? The distinction between nature and person helps us answer without losing the Savior Scripture gives us.

## The Word

**φύσις** (*physis*) means *nature*. Biblical uses concern what something is by nature, its characteristic condition, or the natural order. Later Christian teaching employs the word more precisely when confessing Christ's divine and human natures.

Those later definitions help articulate the faith, but they should not be substituted mechanically into every biblical verse containing the word.

## Range of Meaning

Galatians 4:8 describes false gods as beings that are not gods by nature. Romans 2:14 speaks of Gentiles doing by nature things required by the law, while Romans 1:26 concerns conduct contrary to nature. Ephesians 2:3 describes humanity's condition under wrath.

Second Peter 1:4 speaks of sharing in the divine nature through God's promises. The context contrasts this gift with the world's corruption. It does not promise that creatures will become the divine essence or acquire the personal union unique to Christ.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul recalls the Galatians' former bondage:

> “Formerly, when you did not know God, you were slaves to those who by nature are not gods.” (Galatians 4:8, BSB)

Here *nature* helps distinguish the true God from those falsely regarded as divine. The sentence is not discussing the two natures of Christ, but it shows why attention to what someone actually is matters.

When the church later speaks of Christ's natures, it confesses his full deity and full humanity. The words answer a particular question about the identity of the one Savior.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Council of Chalcedon confessed the one Christ in two natures, without confusion, change, division, or separation. The Formula of Concord likewise insists that both natures remain distinct, preserving their essential properties, while united in one person (Solid Declaration VIII.6–8).

Because the person is one, we can truly say that God suffered and died for us. The Son suffered according to his human nature; the divine nature did not become mortal. The Formula explicitly defends this language as more than a verbal fiction (VIII.44–45). It does not mean that the Father died or that a separate human person died beside the Son.

The union also matters for Christ's gifts. His humanity truly shares in the majesty communicated through that union, without becoming a different divine nature. The Formula connects this with his promised presence in the Supper (VIII.26–29). We receive his body and blood because this Christ has promised to give them.

[Prosōpon](/greek/prosopon/) considers the related language of person. The distinction keeps us from either dividing Christ or mixing his natures into something that is neither truly God nor truly human.

## So What

Your Savior's humanity is real enough to suffer your death, and the person who gives himself is the eternal Son. The cross is neither a divine illusion nor the death of a stranger whom God merely honors afterward.

The same Christ gives himself according to his promise. Careful confession protects that comfort: the one who died for you is the one who now comes to you with forgiveness.
