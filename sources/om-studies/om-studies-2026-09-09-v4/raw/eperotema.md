Peter says that Baptism now saves, and immediately distinguishes it from washing dirt off the body. The distinction explains the kind of washing he is discussing. It does not withdraw the saving claim with which he began.

## The Word

**ἐπερώτημα** (*eperōtēma*) is a rare New Testament word, appearing here in 1 Peter 3:21. Translations render it as *appeal*, *pledge*, or *response*. Its relation to the good conscience named in the verse is part of the interpretive question.

The BSB uses *pledge*. Other translations understand an appeal to God for a good conscience. We should acknowledge the difference without pretending that a preferred English word settles every question about Baptism.

## Range of Meaning

An appeal emphasizes asking God for what is needed. A pledge or response emphasizes an answer directed toward him. These readings give a different shape to the phrase, but neither permits the resurrection of Jesus to be removed from Peter's sentence.

The conscience is central. Peter is discussing a saving relationship to God, not the ordinary cleansing of skin. A bath removes dirt; Baptism's significance cannot be exhausted by that physical description.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Peter speaks of:

> “And this water symbolizes the baptism that now saves you also—not the removal of dirt from the body, but the pledge of a clear conscience toward God—through the resurrection of Jesus Christ,” (1 Peter 3:21, BSB)

The verse ends by identifying the resurrection of Jesus Christ as the means through which this saving takes place. The intervening phrase contains the disputed *eperōtēma*, which the BSB renders as “the pledge of a clear conscience toward God.”

The surrounding passage begins with Christ suffering once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring us to God. It then refers to Noah's deliverance through water before speaking of Baptism. The risen Christ's heavenly authority concludes the argument.

That sequence keeps the reader's attention on Christ's saving achievement. Baptism belongs within what he has accomplished and gives; it is not an independent remedy alongside him.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Lutheran confession receives Peter's positive statement about Baptism without making ordinary water a power apart from God's Word. God gives the washing its promise, and faith receives what he promises through it.

Nor does faith become a replacement saving work. If the phrase is read as an appeal, the believer asks God for the good that only God can give. If it is read as a pledge or response, that response cannot become the achievement that takes the place of Christ's resurrection.

The comfort rests in God's action for sinners. Baptism directs a troubled conscience toward the Savior who died and rose, rather than toward the quality of a commitment the baptized person once made.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Peter's denial that Baptism is bodily dirt removal is sometimes treated as a denial that the baptismal washing gives anything. That conclusion does not follow from the contrast. He distinguishes Baptism from a bath while affirming that it saves through Christ's resurrection.

Conversely, the translation *pledge* need not imply that salvation is earned by a vow. The lexical question is real; making our commitment the saving cause is a further theological conclusion, not something the English word proves.

## So What

When your conscience accuses you, you need more than a memory of how sincerely you once promised to follow Jesus. You need the living Savior and his trustworthy promise.

Baptism gives you a definite place to hear that promise addressed to you. Return to Christ in repentance and faith, receiving the gift rather than trying to improve the past into a sufficient foundation for assurance.
