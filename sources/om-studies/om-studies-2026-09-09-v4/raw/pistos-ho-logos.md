Somewhere in the first decades of the church, before there were creeds and before most Christians could have owned a book, congregations began saying certain things in fixed words.

We know this because Paul quotes them.

Five times in these three letters — and nowhere else in the New Testament — a formula appears: *pistos ho logos*, the saying is trustworthy. It functions the way a citation functions. It signals that what follows or precedes is not the writer's own composition but something already in circulation, already agreed, already portable enough to be invoked with a tag.

This is the earliest visible catechesis in the New Testament. Not a system, not a document, but a handful of sentences short enough to memorize and weighty enough to be worth memorizing. The Christian in Ephesus or Crete did not have to generate his confession under pressure. He had been given words.

This makes the entrusted deposit explored in [Paratheke](/greek/paratheke/) concrete. The deposit is not an abstraction. It has actual sentences in it.

## The Phrase

The Greek is πιστὸς ὁ λόγος (*pistos ho logos*), pronounced in the Erasmian convention as *pis-TOS ho LOG-os*. It is a verbless clause — Greek supplies no *is*, and none is needed: *trustworthy, the saying*.

*Pistos* (πιστός) is the adjective from *pistis* (πίστις), faith. It means *faithful* or *trustworthy*, and it runs in both directions: a *pistos* person can be trusted, and a *pistos* person trusts. Applied to a *logos*, it means *this one will hold*.

*Logos* (λόγος) here does not mean *word* in the sense of a lexical item, and certainly not the Johannine Word. It means a saying, a statement, an account — the thing said. English "saying" is close.

Twice the formula is extended: *pistos ho logos kai pasēs apodochēs axios* — trustworthy and worthy of full acceptance (1 Timothy 1:15 and 4:9). *Apodochē* is a word of reception and welcome; it is used in Hellenistic Greek of receiving a guest or accepting a claim as established.

Related words in the same letters: *homologia* (ὁμολογία), *confession* — the good confession Timothy made before many witnesses, and which Christ made before Pilate (1 Timothy 6:12–13). *Homologoumenōs* (ὁμολογουμένως), *confessedly, by common admission* — 1 Timothy 3:16, introducing the hymn. These are all vocabulary for **things said in common**.

## The Five Sayings

**1. 1 Timothy 1:15.** "This is a trustworthy saying, worthy of full acceptance: Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am the worst."

The clearest of the five and probably the best known. Eight words in Greek for the saying itself. Note that Paul's addition — *of whom I am foremost* — is in the **present tense**. Not *was*. Whatever else has changed since the Damascus road, that has not.

**2. 1 Timothy 3:1.** "This is a trustworthy saying: If anyone aspires to be an overseer, he desires a noble task."

**3. 1 Timothy 4:9.** "This is a trustworthy saying, worthy of full acceptance." The surrounding material concerns training for godliness and hope set on the living God, who is the Savior of all people, especially of believers.

**4. 2 Timothy 2:11–13.** "This is a trustworthy saying: If we died with Him, we will also live with Him; if we endure, we will also reign with Him; if we deny Him, He will also deny us; if we are faithless, He remains faithful, for He cannot deny Himself."

**5. Titus 3:8.** “This saying is trustworthy. And I want you to emphasize these things, so that those who have believed God will take care to devote themselves to good deeds. These things are excellent and profitable for the people.” The saying pointed to is almost certainly the baptismal paragraph of Titus 3:4–7 — see [*palingenesia*](/greek/paliggenesia/).

**The honest flag: which way does the formula point?** For at least three of the five, it is genuinely unclear whether *pistos ho logos* introduces what follows or endorses what precedes. First Timothy 1:15 clearly points forward. Titus 3:8 clearly points back. The other three are contested.

First Timothy 3:1 is the sharpest case. Does the formula commend the saying about aspiring to the office of overseer, which comes after it — or does it close the difficult paragraph about women and childbearing that comes before it? Both have been argued seriously. First Timothy 4:9 is similar: the saying may be the bodily-training proverb of verse 8 or the statement about the living God in verse 10.

Readers should know that **the paragraph breaks in their English Bible are an editorial decision, not part of the text**. The earliest manuscripts have no such divisions. Where your Bible puts the white space is a translator's judgment about which way the formula points, and a different judgment would produce a different reading of the passage.

## The Fourth Saying, Which Breaks Its Own Pattern

Second Timothy 2:11–13 deserves separate treatment. It is plainly rhythmic — four conditional lines, balanced, almost certainly a hymn fragment — and it is built to set up a break.

The first three lines run in strict parallel, and each is a mirror:

*If we died with him, we will also live with him.*
*If we endure, we will also reign with him.*
*If we deny him, he will also deny us.*

Three protases, three apodoses, and the logic is symmetrical. What we do, he answers in kind. The third line is genuinely severe and should not be softened; it echoes a saying of Jesus.

Then the fourth line arrives, and the symmetry collapses:

*If we are unfaithful, he remains faithful, since he cannot deny himself.*

The pattern would require *if we are unfaithful, he will also be unfaithful*. It does not say that. The mirror breaks, and it breaks in one direction only.

The Greek makes the break audible in a way English cannot quite manage: *ei apistoumen, ekeinos pistos menei* — if we are *a-pistoi*, he remains *pistos*. It is the same root as the formula that introduced the whole saying. The hymn ends by predicating of Christ the exact quality it claimed for itself.

And the reason given is not that God is lenient. It is that he *cannot deny himself*. His faithfulness is not a policy he might revise; it is what he is.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear


*The faith was received in words, and this is why the church catechizes.* The existence of these five sayings is an argument in itself. Before there was a New Testament canon, before there was an Apostles' Creed, congregations had fixed formulations they could say together. The creeds are this instinct three centuries on, doing the same work with more precision because more had been disputed.

The confessional Lutheran commitment to catechesis follows directly. Luther wrote a Small Catechism in short answers to be memorized because that is what a deposit in transmissible form looks like. The objection that memorized words are dead words has an answer in these letters: the memorized words in question are *Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners*, and a person who has those eight words available at three in the morning has something a person who must generate his own theology at three in the morning does not.

*Justification stated in the first person, in the present tense.* First Timothy 1:15 is one of the great texts of the Lutheran tradition, and the reason is the last clause. Paul does not say he *was* the foremost of sinners before his conversion. He says he *is*.

*Simul iustus et peccator* — at the same time righteous and a sinner — is not a slogan derived from a doctrine. It is a description of what an apostle said about himself in a sentence the church was already reciting. The Christian is not a former sinner who has been upgraded; he is a sinner to whom Christ came, and continues to be both at once. The Apology's treatment of justification takes this as its shape, and its pastoral force is enormous: a person who discovers that he is still the foremost of sinners has not discovered that his conversion failed. He has discovered what the saying already said.

## So What

*The asymmetry in the fourth saying is the gospel's structure.* Second Timothy 2:13 is where Lutheran theology finds the load-bearing sentence of the whole set. Three lines of symmetry establish that the terms are real — denial has consequences, and the letters do not blunt them. And then the fourth line refuses to complete the pattern.

This is exactly the asymmetry the Confessions insist on when they distinguish law and gospel. The law works by mirroring: do this and live, do that and die, and the correspondence holds. The gospel does not mirror. It answers our faithlessness with his faithfulness, and it gives as its reason something about God's own nature rather than something about our performance.

Which is also why the third and fourth lines can stand together without one cancelling the other. The saying does not resolve them, and neither should a study. It sets a real warning beside a promise that does not depend on us, and leaves the Christian holding both — which is, in the end, what it means to live on a deposit somebody else is keeping.
