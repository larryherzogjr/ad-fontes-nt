The word *sin* gets used a lot of ways in English that it does not carry in Greek. We talk about "sinful" chocolate cake and "sinful" indulgences and the "sin" of skipping the gym. The word has gone soft on us. By the time it reaches the Sunday liturgy where we confess that we are "by nature sinful and unclean," a fair share of the congregation is quietly wondering whether the wording is not a touch overstated.

It is not overstated. It is not even strong enough.

## The Word

**ἁμαρτία** (hamartia), pronounced *hah-mar-TEE-ah*. A first-declension feminine noun. The verb form is *hamartanō* — to sin, to do wrong.

You will sometimes be told that *hamartia* comes from a root meaning "to miss the mark" — an archery term, picturing an arrow that has gone wide of its target. That etymology is real. It is also where most lay treatments of *hamartia* stop, and where most of the trouble starts.

Words do not mean what their etymology says they mean. Even if *hamartia* started life as an archery word, by the time Paul reaches for it in Romans it is doing far more theological work than "I aimed and missed" can carry. The archery picture is in the family tree of the word. It is not the word.

## Range of Meaning

In its New Testament usage, *hamartia* covers a wider field than English "sin" usually does. The range includes at least:

- A specific wrong act. One sin, one occurrence. “Jesus replied, ‘Truly, truly, I tell you, everyone who sins is a slave to sin.’” (John 8:34, BSB).
- The condition or state of sinfulness. Not just acts but the orientation behind them.
- Sin as a power. This is the one English readers most often miss. In Romans especially, Paul personifies *hamartia* as an active agent. It “sin reigned in death” (Romans 5:21, BSB), it "dwells in" people (Rom 7:17), it “produced death in me” (Romans 7:13, BSB). Paul writes about sin the way a doctor writes about a disease that is not just present but actively at work.
- The guilt and burden of sin — the weight that has to be taken away. “the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world” (John 1:29, BSB).
- In the background, from the Septuagint, the Hebrew *chatat* — which can mean either a sin or the sin-offering made to cover it.

The English word "sin" can carry several of these, but rarely all at once, and rarely as forcefully. When Paul uses *hamartia*, the context will usually point toward one of these senses — but the others are present in the family of meanings the word brings with it.

Here is something your English Bible cannot easily show you. When Paul writes about sin as a power, he very often puts the definite article in front of it — *hē hamartia*, “the Sin,” the way you would name a person or a country. English has no graceful way to carry that. “The sin came into the world” sounds like a typo, so translators quietly drop the article and print “sin entered the world” (Romans 5:12, BSB). But the article is doing real work. Paul is not talking about your sins, plural, the items on the list. He is talking about Sin, singular — a power that got loose in the world through one man and has been running the place ever since. Read Romans 5 through 7 watching for that little word *hē*, and a character walks onto the stage: Sin enters, reigns, seizes territory, deceives, pays wages, kills. It is the difference between reading a police blotter and reading about an occupying army.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Some of the most important New Testament occurrences:

> “for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God,” (Romans 3:23, BSB)

The verb form (*hēmarton*) here, not the noun, but the family is the same. The all-have-sinned verse is the diagnostic floor: there are no exceptions.

> “For the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord.” (Romans 6:23, BSB)

*Hamartia* as the employer who pays in death. The personification is already at work.

> “For sin shall not be your master, because you are not under law, but under grace.” (Romans 6:14, BSB)

Read this next to verse 23 and the picture sharpens. Sin is not only an employer who pays in death; it is a would-be lord. The verb Paul uses for “have dominion” is *kyrieuō* — the verb built on *kyrios*, “lord” ([Kyrios](/greek/kyrios/)). Paul’s promise here is not mainly that you will behave better. It is that you have been moved out from under one lordship and set under another. The Christian life is not sin’s dominion carefully managed; it is sin’s dominion broken, and a new Lord in the house.

> “In that case, it is no longer I who do it, but it is sin living in me that does it.” (Romans 7:17, BSB)

This is the verse where Paul shows you that *hamartia* is not only things you do. It is something that *lives in you* and acts. You cannot read Romans 7 honestly and come away thinking sin is only a list of bad behaviors.

> “God made Him who knew no sin to be sin on our behalf, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God.” (2 Corinthians 5:21, BSB)

The great exchange. The one who knew no *hamartia* is made *hamartia* for us. Lutherans read this as the most concentrated statement of substitutionary atonement in the New Testament. (We return to this verse in [Hyper](/greek/hyper/).)

> “If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us.” (1 John 1:8, BSB)

John writes to baptized Christians. Notice what he says — not "if you sinned once, repent and you will have no more sin." He says *if we claim* to have no sin, we are lying. The baptized Christian still has *hamartia*. This is the verse Lutherans point to when they speak of the *simul*.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

In *hamartia* the confessional Lutheran ear catches two notes at once.

First, *hamartia* is more than acts. It is a condition we are born into. This is what the Confessions call original sin. The Augsburg Confession, Article II, puts it bluntly: all human beings since Adam are born “with sin, that is, without fear of God, without trust in God, and with concupiscence.” That is not a list of bad behaviors. It is a description of what we *are* before we do anything.

Roman Catholic theology, since the Council of Trent, has tended to treat the disordered desire that lingers in the baptized Christian — concupiscence — as not-quite-sin: the tinder for sin, perhaps, but not sin itself. Lutherans say concupiscence *is* sin, and the Apology of the Augsburg Confession spends the whole of its second article making the case. It is worth knowing why the Reformers dug in here. If concupiscence is only tinder and not sin, then something in me is still fundamentally sound, and the gospel has only to top up what I cannot quite finish on my own. If concupiscence is sin — if the disorder goes all the way down — then I need a Savior for my nature and not merely for my record. The diagnosis is more severe because the disease is, and the severity is not cruelty. It is the precondition for a gospel large enough to actually save.

Second, *hamartia* remains in the baptized Christian. *Simul iustus et peccator* — at the same time righteous and a sinner. The Reformed tradition often speaks of progressive sanctification in a way that suggests sin is gradually being uprooted in the believer, the sinner shrinking year by year until little is left. Lutherans are more sober, and — strangely — more comforted. The *simul* is not a measurement. It is not that I am forty percent saint and sixty percent sinner, with the ratio slowly improving. It is that I am, at one and the same time, *wholly* righteous and *wholly* a sinner. Wholly righteous, because the righteousness that saves me is Christ’s, reckoned to me, entire and outside myself — it is not a quantity in me that could rise or fall. Wholly a sinner, because the old Adam is still in the house, and he does not need to be invited. He stays.

This is why the comfort holds. If my standing before God rose and fell with how much of the sinner had been burned off, I could never have a quiet conscience; I would spend my life taking my own spiritual temperature. But because the righteousness is Christ’s and complete, the sin that remains — real as it is — cannot unseat me. So we live in daily repentance, daily return to baptism, daily reception of the absolution — not because we are clawing our way toward a sinlessness we might someday reach, but because daily we are sinners, and daily the word of forgiveness is spoken over sinners, and that word is enough.

Both of these notes rest on the word’s deepest register — the one the entry’s title reaches for: *hamartia* is a power, and not only a debit. If sin were merely a record of acts, salvation could be merely the erasing of the record — pardon, and nothing more. And pardon is real; the forgiveness of sins is the beating heart of the gospel. But because *hamartia* is also a reigning power, salvation is also a rescue. Christ does not only cancel what sin charged against us; He goes down into sin’s own territory, dies under its full weight, and rises having broken its dominion — and in baptism He carries us across the border, out of the kingdom where Sin is lord and into the kingdom where He is (Romans 6:1–11). This is why Lutherans refuse to choose between the courtroom and the battlefield. Sin is guilt to be forgiven *and* a tyrant to be defeated; the cross is both an acquittal and a liberation. Hold only the first and the Christian life shrinks to bookkeeping. Hold only the second and you lose the conscience-settling word of pardon. The word *hamartia*, heard in full, will not let you set down either one.

The pastoral payoff: when you confess together with your congregation that you are "by nature sinful and unclean," you are not exaggerating. You are not being theatrical. You are telling the truth about yourself in the only words that the gospel actually answers.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Three common ways.

The first is the root fallacy version: “*hamartia* just means ‘missing the mark.’” This sounds humble and almost cheerful — it conjures an archer whose aim was off, who can try again next round. That is not what Paul means. Paul means a power that has taken up residence and is killing you. The archery picture, taken alone, lets the reader feel sorry for sin instead of afraid of it.

The second is the therapeutic reduction: we should not really call it *sin* anymore, the argument goes — we should call it *brokenness*, or *mistakes*, or *coping mechanisms*. This is sometimes said with pastoral good intentions, but it costs us the word the gospel needs. If your problem is brokenness, the gospel becomes a repair manual. If your problem is mistakes, the gospel becomes self-help. Only if your problem is *hamartia* does the cross make any sense at all.

The third is treating sin only as individual. The biblical witness is that *hamartia* is a power that has reigned over the whole human family since Adam. It is not just a column of behavior reports next to your name. It is what is wrong with the world.

## So What

The New Testament’s vocabulary of salvation depends on understanding *hamartia*. Without sin, grace and justification lose their meaning. Grace becomes a free gift to people who were already mostly fine. Justification becomes good news for the rare bad apple instead of the only news for any of us. The cross becomes a tragedy with no purchase on your Tuesday morning.

When you teach a class or lead a study or sit at the coffee table with a friend who is wondering why we still talk about sin in 2026, this is the word you reach for. Not as a club to beat anyone with — never that — but as the honest naming of what is the matter with us. The diagnosis. The thing the doctor finally says out loud.

And then, only then, does the gospel become news.
