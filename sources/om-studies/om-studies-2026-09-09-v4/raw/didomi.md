The words of the Supper identify both the gift and its recipients. Christ gives his body for you. That small verb keeps the sacrament's direction clear: the Lord gives, and his people receive.

## The Word

**δίδωμι** (*didōmi*) means *to give*. It is a common verb with a broad range of uses. Giving can involve food, money, authority, an answer, or one's own life. The word does not always mean a saving gift, and it does not by itself specify whether the recipient deserves what is given.

In Luke 22:19, the form *didomenon* describes Christ's body as given for his disciples. The saving force comes from the giver, the gift, and his words for them, not from an unusual meaning hidden inside an ordinary verb.

## Range of Meaning

John 3:16 speaks of God giving his only Son. Luke 12:32 speaks of the Father's pleasure in giving the kingdom. Matthew 6:11 uses the verb when Christians ask their Father for daily bread.

Those gifts are not identical, but their differences do not obscure the Father's generosity. Bodily provision, the sending of the Son, and the kingdom are all received from him. The context tells us which gift is being named.

## Where You’ll Meet It

At the Supper Jesus says:

> “And He took the bread, gave thanks and broke it, and gave it to them, saying, ‘This is My body, given for you; do this in remembrance of Me.’” (Luke 22:19, BSB)

Luke's account first describes Jesus taking bread, giving thanks, breaking it, and giving it to the disciples. The statement about his body then identifies what is given for them. The passage is an institution with a promise, not merely an illustration of generous behavior.

The words also point toward his approaching death. The body given in the sacrament belongs to the Lord who goes to the cross for sinners. His giving cannot be separated from the work he accomplishes there.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Augsburg Confession X confesses that Christ's true body and blood are present, distributed, and received in the Supper. The Small Catechism's explanation places its benefit in Christ's words concerning the forgiveness of sins. His testament tells the communicant what the gift is for.

Faith does not manufacture that gift. It trusts the Lord who gives it. Nor does the pastor's personal holiness turn bread into a sacrament. Christ's institution and words are the foundation of what the Church distributes.

Our thanksgiving is fitting and necessary in its place, but it answers the gift. It does not replace the body and blood with the congregation's sincerity or offer God an equivalent payment in return.

## Where People Get It Wrong

An ordinary word need not describe an ordinary transaction. Saying that *didōmi* simply means to give does not decide what Jesus gives. His accompanying words answer that question.

Another mistake makes the repeated reception of his gift evidence that the cross is unfinished. The once-for-all work and the repeated distribution of its benefits belong together; one does not compete with the other.

## So What

At the altar, listen for the recipient as well as the gift. Christ does not only announce that forgiveness exists somewhere. His words address the people receiving his testament.
