Permission is not the only question Christian freedom asks. Paul also asks whether an action benefits another person, builds up the church, or takes mastery over the one who claims to be free. Those questions give freedom a neighbor to serve.

## The Word

**ἔξεστιν** (*exestin*) means *it is permitted* or *it is lawful*. It is an impersonal verb: the construction states that an action is allowed rather than naming a person who permits it.

The word does not by itself identify which authority, rule, or claim of permission is in view. The surrounding conversation supplies that information.

## Range of Meaning

Matthew 12 uses the verb in disputes about the Sabbath, including the question whether healing is lawful. Jesus answers by defending doing good and exposing the failure to recognize mercy.

Acts 16:21 places the verb in an accusation against Paul and Silas: their opponents claim that the missionaries promote customs Romans may not accept. The presence of the word in that speech does not make the accusation God's verdict.

First Corinthians 6:12 and 10:23 repeat a claim that everything is lawful, then immediately qualifies it. Paul is not giving an unrestricted permission slip for anything a Christian desires.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul writes:

> “‘Everything is permissible,’ but not everything is beneficial. ‘Everything is permissible,’ but not everything is edifying.” (1 Corinthians 10:23, BSB)

The sentence follows a claim of permission that the BSB places within quotation marks. Its notes understand Paul to be repeating Corinthian slogans and correcting their misuse. This is a contextual interpretation, not something proved by the verb or by quotation marks supplied in an English edition.

The next verse directs readers to seek another person's good. The discussion then considers food, conscience, and conduct toward believers and unbelievers.

In 1 Corinthians 6:12, Paul also refuses to be controlled by anything. The surrounding argument explicitly rejects sexual immorality. A claim of freedom cannot cancel God's command or the body's belonging to Christ.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration X.8–10, describes the church's freedom concerning genuinely indifferent ceremonies. Such practices may be changed in an orderly way for the church's benefit, with care for the weak. Their usefulness does not turn them into requirements for earning God's favor.

The same article refuses concessions that would deny the gospel when confession is required. Something normally free cannot be treated casually when its imposition demands surrender of the truth.

Christian freedom therefore calls for judgment shaped by God's Word and love. It neither invents divine commands for every preference nor uses the absence of a command to dismiss the neighbor's needs.

## Where People Get It Wrong

Reading every clause Paul writes as his own unqualified thesis misses his interaction with claims he is answering. The slogan reading explains the repeated pattern of permission followed by correction, but the correction matters even where an interpreter describes the opening claim differently.

Another mistake equates *permitted* with *wise in every circumstance*. Paul's concern for edification expressly denies that conclusion. Freedom includes the willingness to refrain for another person's good.

## So What

Before insisting on a right, consider what exercising it will do for the people around you. Their benefit is part of the question, not an obstacle to your freedom.

At the same time, do not surrender the gospel to a demand presented as a harmless custom. Christ frees consciences to trust his promise and frees hands to serve the neighbor.
