In Gethsemane, Jesus addresses his Father while facing the cup of suffering. Paul uses the same address when describing the prayer of God's adopted children. The connection gives Christian prayer a confidence that does not depend on feeling confident.

## The Word

**ἀββά** (*abba*) is an Aramaic word for *father*, preserved in Greek letters within the New Testament. It occurs in Mark 14:36, Romans 8:15, and Galatians 4:6. Each occurrence is accompanied by the Greek word for father.

The word is therefore left in its Aramaic form and explained beside it. We do not have to invent a reason why the writers retained it in order to hear the connection among these passages.

## Range of Meaning

The three settings give this address its force. Jesus prays as the Son; believers cry out as those who have received adoption; the Spirit of God's Son cries in their hearts.

The shared address does not erase the distinction. Jesus is the eternal Son. We become God's children by grace, through him. Our access to the Father is a gift, not a declaration that everyone already possesses the Son's relationship by nature.

Nor does the word turn prayer into casual familiarity. Jesus' prayer in the garden is intimate, anguished, and obedient. Trust and reverence are present together.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Mark records Jesus praying:

> “‘Abba, Father,’ He said, ‘all things are possible for You. Take this cup from Me. Yet not what I will, but what You will.’” (Mark 14:36, BSB)

He names what he dreads and entrusts himself to his Father. His obedience is not the absence of suffering. It carries him through the suffering by which he saves us.

Romans 8:15 contrasts slavery leading back into fear with the Spirit of adoption, by whom believers cry to the Father. Galatians 4:4–7 first speaks of God sending his Son to redeem those under the law, then of sending the Spirit of his Son into their hearts. The cry follows God's saving action.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Small Catechism's explanation of the Lord's Prayer introduction teaches us to believe that God is truly our Father and we are truly his children. He invites us to ask with the confidence of loving children approaching their loving father.

This confidence is not a spiritual achievement added to Christ's work. The Spirit brings us to trust the Father through the Son. A hesitant prayer does not need to be made emotionally impressive before God can hear it.

The catechism also supplies words when our own words fail. Praying the Lord's Prayer need not compete with heartfelt prayer. Its petitions teach the heart what to ask and place the struggling Christian among others who pray to the same Father.

## So What

You can tell your Father what frightens you. Gethsemane gives no reason to pretend that obedience makes anguish disappear.

You can also pray when the warmth you associate with being God's child is absent. Christ's redemption and the Spirit's promise are firmer than that feeling. Begin with the address your Lord has given you. You are asking for help from the Father who has already sent his Son for you.
