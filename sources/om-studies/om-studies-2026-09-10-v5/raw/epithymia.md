Sin reaches deeper than the deed other people can see. The commandment against coveting addresses what we want, not only what we take. That makes an honest account of our desires necessary, while Scripture also keeps us from calling every desire sinful.

## The Word

**ἐπιθυμία** (*epithymia*) means *desire*, *longing*, or *craving*. The word itself does not establish whether the desire is good. Its object and context matter.

Luke 22:15 uses the noun in Jesus' earnest desire to eat the Passover with his disciples. Philippians 1:23 uses it for Paul's desire to depart and be with Christ. Neither is a sinful craving.

## Range of Meaning

In Romans 7:7–8, however, the commandment against coveting exposes wrong desire. Sin takes the opportunity presented by the commandment and produces desires opposed to God's will. The law is not itself sinful; it reveals the sin already at work.

Galatians 5 describes the conflict between flesh and Spirit, then names the desires crucified by those who belong to Christ. First John 2:16 places the desires of the flesh and eyes within the world's opposition to the Father.

James 1:14–15 traces desire's movement toward sinful action and death. His picture describes sin's development; it does not make every inward craving morally innocent until it produces a visible act.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul writes:

> “But sin, seizing its opportunity through the commandment, produced in me every kind of covetous desire. For apart from the law, sin is dead.” (Romans 7:8, BSB)

The commandment does not merely forbid successful theft. It addresses coveting itself. A person who refrains from taking a neighbor's property may still need forgiveness for wanting to acquire it wrongfully.

That does not erase the difference between temptation and consent. Being confronted with evil is not the same as embracing it, and an unwanted thought is not proof that someone welcomes what it suggests. The point is that disordered desire itself also needs God's mercy.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Apology II.35–38 teaches that concupiscence remains after Baptism, while Baptism removes the guilt of original sin. The Spirit begins putting sinful desire to death and creating new impulses. Remaining corruption is real sin, and forgiveness is also real.

This helps explain the Lutheran confession that the Christian is at once righteous and a sinner. Righteousness before God rests in Christ and his forgiveness; it does not require pretending that every inward movement has already become pure.

Nor does that confession give permission to nurture destructive desires. The Spirit's work includes resistance, repentance, and new obedience. Forgiveness frees us to name sin truthfully and struggle against it without making our progress the ground of God's acceptance.

## Where People Get It Wrong

One error treats desire as morally neutral whenever no outward act follows. Another treats every strong longing, temptation, or distressing thought as equally culpable. Scripture's varied uses of *epithymia* require more careful judgment.

The commandment exposes coveting. It does not condemn Jesus' desire for the Passover or Paul's longing to be with him.

## So What

Bring your desires as well as your deeds into confession. You need not excuse what is wrong, and you need not invent guilt merely because a temptation troubles you.

Christ's forgiveness is for the sinner you actually are. Receive it, resist what harms your neighbor, and ask the Spirit to cultivate desires shaped by God's good gifts.
