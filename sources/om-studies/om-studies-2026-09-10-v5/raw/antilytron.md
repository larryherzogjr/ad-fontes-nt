Doctrines are usually built out of sentences. Occasionally one is built into a word.

Jesus said that the Son of Man came to give his life a ransom *for* many — *lytron anti pollōn*. Two elements: a noun for the price paid to free a captive, and a preposition meaning *in place of*. The preposition sits loose in the sentence, and readers who dislike the idea of substitution have always had somewhere to go: *anti* has a range, contexts vary, prepositions are slippery.

First Timothy 2:6 takes the same two elements and welds them together.

*Antilytron.* One word. The preposition is no longer a separate item that a reader can renegotiate; it has become part of the noun. Christ gave himself as an *instead-of-price*. Whatever else is debated about the atonement, the substitution is not being inferred from context here. It is inside the vocabulary.

The word occurs once in the New Testament, and as far as the surviving evidence goes it was very nearly a new word when Paul used it.

## The Word

The Greek word is ἀντίλυτρον (*antilytron*), pronounced in the Erasmian convention as *an-TEE-loo-tron*, with the accent on the second syllable. It is a second-declension neuter noun.

Two elements. *Anti* (ἀντί) is the preposition of exchange and substitution — *in place of, instead of, in return for*. English readers already know it from Matthew 5:38, an eye *anti* an eye. *Lytron* (λύτρον) is the price paid to release someone, formed from *lyō* (λύω), *to loose*, with the *-tron* suffix that marks an instrument or means. A *lytron* is literally *the thing by which someone is loosed*.

The family is large and worth laying out, because English translations scatter it across several unrelated words:

*Lytron* (λύτρον) — the ransom price itself. Twice: Matthew 20:28 and Mark 10:45, in the same saying.

*Lytroō* (λυτρόω) — *to ransom, to redeem*. Luke 24:21, of the hope that Jesus would redeem Israel. Titus 2:14, that he gave himself to redeem us from all lawlessness. 1 Peter 1:18, redeemed not with perishable things like silver or gold.

*Lytrōsis* (λύτρωσις) — the act of redeeming. Luke 1:68; 2:38; Hebrews 9:12.

*Lytrōtēs* (λυτρωτής) — *redeemer*. Acts 7:35, of Moses.

*Apolytrōsis* (ἀπολύτρωσις) — the fullest form, *redemption*. Romans 3:24; 8:23; Ephesians 1:7, 14; Colossians 1:14; Hebrews 9:15; and elsewhere. This is the word most English Bibles render *redemption*, and readers rarely notice that it is built on the same root as *ransom*.

**Background, and its limits.** The *lytron* word group belonged to the ordinary world of captivity and slavery. A prisoner of war was ransomed; a slave could be freed by purchase. The inscriptions at Delphi record a formal procedure of sacral manumission in which a slave was nominally sold to Apollo, the price was paid, and the slave went free — a legal fiction with a religious frame that has often been cited as background to the New Testament's language.

The parallel is illuminating and it has a hard limit, which an honest study should name. In the manumission procedure there is a recipient of the price. In the New Testament there is not. The question *to whom was the ransom paid* has been asked since the early centuries and has produced some of the worst theology in the tradition — including the medieval notion of a price paid to the devil, which the Reformers had no patience for. The metaphor names a **cost** and a **substitution**. It does not name a payee, and pressing it for one is asking a picture to answer a question it was not drawn to answer.

The Septuagint background points the same way. There the *lytron* group renders Hebrew terms for redemption of the firstborn, of land, of a kinsman — and the redeemer is God himself. The one who pays is the one who gains nothing.

## Range of Meaning

With one occurrence there is no range. What there is instead is a compound whose two halves each carry weight.

*The lytron half — a price that actually costs.* Redemption in this vocabulary is not release by decree. Something is handed over. Titus 2:14, in the same letter group, spells out what: *he gave himself*.

*The anti half — in place of, not merely on behalf of.* This is the load-bearing element. Greek has another preposition, *hyper* (ὑπέρ), which means *on behalf of, for the sake of* — broader, and compatible with substitution without requiring it. First Timothy 2:6 has both: Christ gave himself an *antilytron* **hyper pantōn**, a substitute-price *on behalf of* all. The compound supplies the substitution; the preposition that follows supplies the scope.

*hyper pantōn — for all.* And here the same universal that governs verse 4 governs verse 6. The ransom is not described as given for some. Whatever account of the atonement a reader holds, this phrase is part of the data.

## Where You'll Meet It

*1 Timothy 2:6.* "who gave Himself as a ransom for all—the testimony that was given at just the right time." Set inside the paragraph running from verse 3, and the paragraph's logic is worth tracing: God desires all to be saved (v. 4) → there is one God and one mediator (v. 5) → who gave himself a ransom for all (v. 6). The universal will, the exclusive mediator, and the universal ransom are three links of one chain. Cutting any of them breaks the argument.

*Mark 10:45 / Matthew 20:28.* "For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many." The uncompounded original: *lytron anti pollōn*. The verbal echo between this saying and 1 Timothy 2:6 is close enough that many take the Timothy line to be a deliberate restatement of a saying of Jesus already in circulation — which would make it, like the faithful sayings discussed in [Pistos ho logos](/greek/pistos-ho-logos/), a piece of received tradition rather than a fresh composition.

The *many* / *all* difference between the two texts is old ground and should be handled without heat. Semitic idiom permits *many* to mean *the many*, i.e. the multitude, without implying *not all*; and 1 Timothy 2:6 gives *pantōn* where Mark gives *pollōn*, which at minimum shows how the phrase was heard by the writer closest to it.

*Titus 2:14.* "He gave Himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for Himself a people for His own possession, zealous for good deeds." The verb form, in the same three letters, with the purpose attached. Redemption is *from* lawlessness and *for* a people — deliverance and possession, both.

*1 Peter 1:18–19.* "For you know that it was not with perishable things such as silver or gold that you were redeemed from the empty way of life you inherited from your forefathers, but with the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish or spot." The explicit denial that the currency was money is itself evidence that the metaphor was being heard as a commercial one and needed the correction.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear


*Vicarious satisfaction, stated in a noun.* Article III of the Augsburg Confession says that Christ truly suffered, was crucified, died, and was buried in order to reconcile the Father to us and to be a sacrifice not only for original guilt but for all actual sins. Article IV says that we cannot be justified before God by our own powers, but are justified freely on account of Christ through faith, when we believe that Christ suffered for us and that for his sake our sin is forgiven.

*Antilytron* is that doctrine compressed into a word. Not Christ as example, not Christ as demonstration of divine love only, not Christ as the one who makes salvation available on terms we then meet — Christ as the price given in our place. Confessional Lutheran theology has never treated substitution as one atonement theory among several to be weighed for elegance. It treats it as the thing the vocabulary says.

*The scope is universal, and the Lutheran tradition does not soften it.* This is one of the clearer places where confessional Lutheran teaching parts from the Reformed. *Hyper pantōn*, following God's stated desire that *all* be saved in verse 4, is read as meaning what it says: the ransom was given for all people without exception. The Formula of Concord's article on election refuses to resolve the resulting tension by limiting the atonement or by positing a hidden divine will at odds with the revealed one.

The tension is real and the Confessions leave it standing rather than tidying it. If Christ died for all, and not all are saved, something is unresolved. The Lutheran instinct is to locate the unresolved part in our knowledge rather than in God's Word, and to refuse the two available tidy solutions — universalism on one side, a limited atonement on the other — because both purchase coherence by trimming a text. A study should say that this is an unresolved tension held deliberately, not a problem the tradition failed to notice.

*The ransom is not a transaction God needed.* Because *antilytron* is a commercial word, it invites a commercial imagination: a debt, a creditor, an account settled. The imagery is biblical and useful, and it is a picture. God is not a creditor who could not afford to forgive. Verse 4 has already said that God *desires* all to be saved — the ransom is the expression of that desire, not the obstacle to it.

## So What

Where this lands pastorally: a Christian troubled about whether the payment covered his particular sins has, in this verse, the answer that the payment was not itemized. It was *himself*, given for all. There is no ledger to check.
