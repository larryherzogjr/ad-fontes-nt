Every other word in this study can be checked against itself. *Parathēkē* appears three times, *eusebeia* ten, *epiphaneia* five. When a word appears more than once, the reader has leverage: the passages interrogate each other, and a reading that works in one place but not another is exposed.

*Authenteō* appears once.

One occurrence, in one clause, in one verse — 1 Timothy 2:12 — and that clause has probably generated more argument per syllable than any other in the Pauline corpus. There is no second passage to check it against. There is no earlier letter where Paul used it differently. There is a small body of occurrences in other Greek authors, most of them from centuries on either side of Paul, and scholars who have read all of them in the original disagree about what they show.

This entry spends considerable space on what the evidence does *not* establish. That is not throat-clearing. A study that promises to flag translation choices honestly rather than smoothing them over has to be willing to do it at the place where smoothing would be most convenient — and where the convenience runs in the direction of the author's own convictions. So: the confessional Lutheran reading of this passage is stated below, plainly and without hedging, along with the grounds it actually rests on. Those grounds are not a lexical knockout, because there isn't one, and claiming otherwise would be doing with *authenteō* precisely what the [Orthotomeo](/greek/orthotomeo/) entry faults others for doing.

## The Word

The Greek word is αὐθεντέω (*authenteō*), pronounced in the Erasmian convention as *ow-then-TEH-o*, with the accent on the third syllable. It is a contract verb, and in 1 Timothy 2:12 it stands as a present active infinitive, *authentein*, governing a genitive object, *andros* — "a man."

The noun behind it is *authentēs* (αὐθέντης), and its history is the reason this is difficult.

In classical Attic — Aeschylus, Euripides, Herodotus — *authentēs* most often means a **murderer**, and specifically one who kills with his own hand, or one who kills a member of his own family. This is genuinely the dominant classical sense and it is startling to encounter. It is also, and this must be said clearly, almost certainly irrelevant to Paul. Words move. By the Hellenistic period the noun had drifted toward *one who acts on his own authority*, then toward *master* or *doer*, and it is that later semantic field, not the tragedians', that stands behind the verb in the New Testament era. Popular arguments that import "murder" into 1 Timothy 2:12 are working from a dictionary entry across a gap of centuries and should be set aside.

The word family:

*Autos* (αὐτός) — *self*. The first element of the compound, and the persistent core of the word's meaning across all its shifts: whatever *authentēs* is doing, he is doing it *himself*, on his own initiative, from his own resources of authority.

*Authentēs* (αὐθέντης) — the noun. Not in the New Testament.

*Authentikos* (αὐθεντικός) — *original, authoritative, firsthand*. Not in the New Testament, but it gives English **authentic**, by way of Latin. The path is instructive: what is authentic is what comes from the source itself.

*Exousiazō* (ἐξουσιάζω) and *katakyrieuō* (κατακυριεύω) — not cognates, but the comparison matters. These are Greek's ordinary words for exercising authority and for lording it over someone, and Paul uses both elsewhere (1 Cor 7:4; 2 Cor 1:24 uses the related noun; *katakyrieuō* at Mark 10:42 of rulers who lord it over their subjects). One live question is why, if he simply meant "have authority," Paul reached for a rare verb instead of the common one. Both sides of the debate have an answer, and neither answer is decisive.

**The evidence base.** Scholars have assembled the surviving occurrences of the verb and its cognates from roughly the fourth century BC to the Byzantine period — the fullest catalogue, compiled by H. Scott Baldwin, runs to a few dozen instances of the verb and around a hundred of the whole family. That sounds like a lot. Spread across a millennium, in texts of wildly different genre, with most instances falling well after the New Testament, it is thin. It is enough to establish a range. It is not enough to fix a meaning in a first-century letter by frequency count, and the honest summary of the situation is that both readings below survive contact with the data.

## Range of Meaning

Two readings are live in current scholarship. A third was once prominent and has been largely abandoned; it is included because readers will still encounter it.

*To exercise authority over — neutral.* On this reading *authentein* describes the ordinary exercise of authority, carrying no built-in judgment about whether the authority is legitimately held or well used. The clause then prohibits a woman from teaching and from exercising authority over a man, full stop, without any implied "improperly." This is the reading of most modern English translations (NASB, CSB, BSB, and — in a deliberately ambiguous rendering — the NIV's "assume authority"). Its main support: several of the surviving occurrences are plainly neutral or even positive, and a negative sense has to be read in from context rather than found in the word.

*To domineer, to usurp, to seize authority — negative.* On this reading the verb carries its own pejorative freight: to act high-handedly, to arrogate authority to oneself. The clause then prohibits something narrower — not a woman teaching or holding authority, but a woman *domineering* over a man. The KJV's "usurp authority over" reflects this reading, as do the Vulgate's *dominari* and several early versions, which is not nothing: those translators were closer to the living language than we are. Its main support: the *autos* core of the word suggests self-derived authority; the surrounding verses are about disruption; and Paul's avoidance of the ordinary verb *exousiazō* wants explaining.

*"To engage in fertility rites" or similar cultic senses.* Proposed in the late twentieth century, chiefly by Catherine Kroeger, on the basis of the noun's association with certain religious contexts. It attracted attention and it has not held up; the lexical evidence for it is now regarded as weak by scholars across the theological spectrum, including many who hold the broader position it was offered to support. It should be named and set aside rather than quietly ignored.

**The syntactic question**, which may matter more than the lexical one. The clause reads *didaskein de gynaiki ouk epitrepō oude authentein andros* — "I do not permit a woman to teach, *oude* to *authentein* a man." Everything turns on how *oude* joins the two infinitives.

Andreas Köstenberger's study of the construction argues that *oude* joins two activities that are viewed alike — both positively or both negatively, never one of each. Since *didaskein*, "to teach," is plainly a good and ordinary activity in the Pastorals (it is what Timothy is commanded to do, and what qualified elders must be able to do), *authentein* joined to it by *oude* should also be neutral or positive. On that argument the neutral reading wins on syntax even if the lexicon is ambiguous.

Philip Payne argues the opposite: that the two infinitives form a hendiadys — a single idea expressed with two words — so that the phrase means something like *to teach in a domineering manner*. On that reading the prohibition targets a manner of teaching, not two separate activities.

Linda Belleville and Al Wolters have argued the lexical question from opposite directions, both with real philological work behind them.

Neither construction argument has produced a consensus, and readers should be suspicious of any summary — including summaries written by people they agree with — that presents this as settled. It is not settled. It is, however, the strongest ground either side has, which is worth knowing: the argument that actually carries weight here is grammatical, not lexical.

## Where You'll Meet It

*1 Timothy 2:12.* "I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man; she is to remain quiet." The single occurrence. Three details in the Greek deserve flagging.

First, *epitrepō* is present tense and first person: *I do not permit*. Some read the present as marking an ongoing standing rule; others read it as marking Paul's practice in a particular situation. Greek present tense does not settle this by itself. Both readings are grammatically available, and this is a genuine ambiguity rather than a manufactured one.

Second, *gynaiki* and *andros* can mean *woman* and *man*, or *wife* and *husband*. Greek does not distinguish. Context decides, and the surrounding verses reach back to Adam and Eve, who were both.

Third, *hēsychia* — rendered "quiet" — is not the word for silence used at 1 Corinthians 14:34. It means quietness, settledness, an absence of disturbance, and it is the same word used positively at 2 Thessalonians 3:12 of working quietly and at 1 Timothy 2:2 of the quiet life all Christians are to pray for. Whatever verse 12 requires, it is not muteness.

*1 Timothy 2:13–14.* "For Adam was formed first, and then Eve. And it was not Adam who was deceived, but the woman who was deceived and fell into transgression." This is the load-bearing context, and it is the strongest argument against reading verse 12 as a purely local ruling. Paul grounds the instruction not in anything about Ephesus but in Genesis — in the order of creation and in the account of the fall. Instructions grounded in creation are ordinarily harder to confine to one city than instructions grounded in circumstance.

The serious counter-argument deserves its full strength here rather than a caricature. Ephesus was the home of the temple of Artemis, and the letter is visibly preoccupied with false teaching (1 Tim 1:3–7; 4:1–3), with myths and genealogies, and with particular women who had been drawn into it (1 Tim 5:13–15; compare 2 Tim 3:6–7). On this reading, Paul's appeal to Genesis is corrective of a specific error about origins that was circulating among those women, not a general ordinance — and the appeal to Adam and Eve is doing the same work it does at 2 Corinthians 11:3, where Paul invokes Eve's deception to warn an entire congregation without deriving any gender-specific rule from it. That is a real argument made by careful readers, and a study that does not state it at full strength has not earned the right to disagree with it.

*1 Timothy 2:15.* "Women, however, will be saved through childbearing, if they continue in faith, love, and holiness, with self-control." One of the genuinely difficult verses in the New Testament, and it cannot be quietly skipped once verses 11–14 are on the table. The main readings: that women are preserved *through* the experience of childbearing rather than *by* it; that *tēs teknogonias* with the article points to *the* childbearing — the birth of Christ; that the verse addresses spiritual well-being in the ordinary vocation of motherhood; that it counters an ascetic teaching forbidding marriage, which 1 Timothy 4:3 shows was in circulation. None of these is free of difficulty. A study should say so and resist tidying it.

*Titus 2:3–5.* Included here because it bounds the question. Older women are to be *kalodidaskalous* — teachers of what is good — and to train the younger women. Whatever 1 Timothy 2:12 prohibits, it does not prohibit women teaching, since the same body of letters commands it in the next book. Precision about scope is not a concession; it is the difference between reading a text and using one.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear


*The question is the office, and the office is not a rank.* Augsburg Confession V says that to obtain faith God instituted the office of preaching, giving the gospel and the sacraments. Article XIV says that no one should publicly teach or administer the sacraments without a rightly ordered call. The confessional Lutheran reading of 1 Timothy 2:12 lives inside that framework: the passage is understood to speak to the public office of preaching and its authority, not to women's competence, spiritual standing, or capacity to teach.

That framing matters more than it may appear to. If the office were a rank, a restriction on it would be a statement about the worth of those excluded. The Lutheran doctrine of vocation refuses the premise. The pastoral office is one calling among many, held by a man who did not choose it for himself and does not own what he administers — the trusteeship explored in [Paratheke](/greek/paratheke/). Whatever is being distributed in 1 Timothy 2–3, it is not status, because there is no status in a trusteeship.

It follows that Galatians 3:28 is affirmed rather than managed. There is neither male nor female in Christ; baptism makes no distinctions and confers no grades of membership. Any reading of 1 Timothy 2:12 that requires Galatians 3:28 to be softened has already gone wrong, and confessional Lutherans should say so before anyone else does.

## Where People Get It Wrong

*The reading rests on the passage, not on the verb.* This is the point most worth being scrupulous about. The confessional position does not depend on winning the lexical argument over *authenteō*, and it would be in poor shape if it did — a hapax with a contested range cannot bear the weight of a church practice. What the position actually rests on is cumulative: the grounding of the instruction in creation rather than circumstance (vv. 13–14); the immediate transition in chapter 3 to the qualifications for the office, which presume a man; the parallel restriction at 1 Corinthians 14:34–35, which is not vulnerable to the same lexical uncertainty; and the near-uniform practice of the church across its history until recently.

That is a real argument and it should be made as one. What it is not is a proof text, and a study that presents *authenteō* as though the Greek settles the matter is doing exactly what the [Orthotomeo](/greek/orthotomeo/) entry criticizes in popular word studies — resting true doctrine on a word that cannot carry it. The doctrine is better served by the honest version.

*Lutherans have not all landed in the same place, and the study should say so.* Confessional Lutheran bodies — the LCMS, WELS, the ELS, and the AFLC among them — hold to a male pastorate on the grounds sketched above. The ELCA and a number of Lutheran churches worldwide ordain women, and they do so with argument rather than by drift. Presenting "the Lutheran view" as though the question had never been contested among Lutherans would be a small dishonesty of exactly the kind this study exists to avoid, and readers who know the landscape will notice.

So: this is where the confessional tradition has stood and these are its grounds. It is also a place where serious Christians who read the same Greek have concluded otherwise, and the disagreement is not usually a disagreement about whether Scripture is authoritative. Saying that costs nothing that is worth keeping.

## So What

<!-- COPY APPROVED: Larry, 2026-09-06; editorial punch-list repair approved for production. -->
A difficult word calls for patient teaching. If you lead a Bible study, give people room to ask what the passage says, what the Greek can establish, and why the church teaches as it does. An honest question is an opportunity to teach, and a contested verb is no reason to speak dismissively of the person asking.

The office described here exists to deliver Christ's gifts. The pastor stands among people who need the same forgiveness he needs; his calling gives him work to do for them, never permission to belittle them. Men and women alike receive the whole Christ in the gospel and the sacraments. The discussion of the office should leave that promise clear enough for every hearer to take home.
