Paul speaks of longing for the Philippians with Christ's affection. John asks how God's love can remain in someone who sees a fellow Christian in need and closes off compassion. The language of affection reaches the body and then reaches the neighbor.

## The Word

**σπλάγχνα** (*splanchna*) is the plural of **σπλάγχνον** (*splanchnon*), referring to inward parts or organs. In figurative use, it expresses deep affection, compassion, or the inward self.

An English translation may therefore use *heart* or *affection* where the Greek noun literally names inward parts. Translating the physical image mechanically in every sentence would obscure the meaning rather than clarify it.

## Range of Meaning

Philippians 1:8 uses *splanchna* for Paul's affection toward the congregation. Philippians 2:1 joins it with mercy in an appeal for unity, humility, and attention to others' interests.

In Philemon, the noun appears in references to refreshed hearts and in Paul's description of Onesimus as his own heart. These are personal expressions of attachment, not abstract definitions of a moral duty.

The related verb **σπλαγχνίζομαι** (*splanchnizomai*) describes compassion in Gospel narratives. Luke 7:13 uses it when Jesus sees the bereaved widow. The verb is related to the noun, but the forms should be distinguished.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul says:

> “God is my witness how I long for all of you with the affection of Christ Jesus.” (Philippians 1:8, BSB)

He is not merely reporting a pleasant mood. His attachment to the congregation belongs to his life in Christ and his concern for their good.

First John 3:17 places the corresponding language in a practical test: someone has possessions, sees a fellow believer's need, and shuts off compassion. The next verse calls for love in deed and truth. The inward response and outward help belong together.

Colossians 3:12–14 similarly joins compassion with kindness, patience, forgiveness, and love. Its starting point is the identity of people chosen, holy, and dearly loved by God.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

God's mercy creates a life directed toward the neighbor. Christ's compassion is not an impersonal transaction, and Christian love need not be embarrassed by affection or tenderness.

But the physical image does not make emotion superior to principle or faithful action. A person may feel little warmth and still serve patiently; another may feel intensely and offer no help. John's instruction joins concern to actual care.

The foundation is Christ's own self-giving, named in 1 John 3:16. Believers do not generate his love by producing a sufficiently strong feeling. They receive his mercy and learn to serve from it.

## So What

Let another person's need interrupt your plans. Listen, offer what help you can, and avoid substituting a compassionate phrase for the care the situation requires.

When affection feels thin, continue the faithful work of love rather than waiting for a dramatic inward movement. When affection is strong, give it a useful form. Christ's mercy concerns the person before you, not the impressiveness of your response.
