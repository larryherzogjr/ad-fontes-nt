Christian hope addresses more than the question of where guilt goes. It also addresses the body that grows weak, the grave, and a creation marked by loss. Forgiveness is not a promise that decay will be allowed to continue forever without a final answer.

Paul names that condition and then speaks of the freedom God will give.

## The Word

**φθορά** (*phthora*) means *corruption*, *decay*, or *ruin*. It can describe physical perishability or moral corruption. The surrounding passage tells us which aspect is in view.

The related word *aphtharsia* means incorruption or imperishability. The adjective [Aphthartos](/greek/aphthartos/) describes what is imperishable. Paul uses this family to distinguish the body's present condition from its promised resurrection life.

## Range of Meaning

The physical and moral uses should not be confused. Illness is not proof of a particular secret sin, and moral corruption is not simply an unfortunate physical process for which nobody is responsible.

Romans 8 speaks about creation's bondage to decay. Galatians 6:8 warns that sowing to the flesh yields corruption. Second Peter 1:4 speaks of worldly corruption produced by evil desire, while 2:19 exposes teachers who promise freedom while themselves enslaved to corruption. The same word family can describe what afflicts us and what our sinful desires pursue.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Paul says creation will be freed from its bondage to decay in Romans 8:21. The paragraph includes our groaning and our waiting for the redemption of our bodies. Creation is not treated as a worthless shell that salvation leaves behind.

First Corinthians 15 makes the bodily promise explicit:

> “So will it be with the resurrection of the dead: What is sown is perishable; it is raised imperishable.” (1 Corinthians 15:42, BSB)

The Greek uses *phthora* and *aphtharsia* in that contrast. The body is sown in corruption and raised in incorruption. English adjectives convey the condition; the Greek nouns name it directly.

Verse 50 says that the perishable cannot inherit the imperishable. Paul immediately explains the needed change through the resurrection. He does not answer the problem by declaring bodily existence itself a mistake.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The resurrection is God's answer to death and decay, not merely a reassurance that the dead remain important to us. Christ's victory concerns the body. The person who dies is the person God raises, transformed for a life no longer ruled by corruption.

That hope also refuses to make peace with moral ruin. Galatians calls Christians to sow to the Spirit and persevere in doing good. The promise of a renewed creation does not make today's neighbor or today's choices irrelevant.

We hold these truths together by attending to the different contexts. God rescues his creatures from what destroys them, and he calls sinners away from the desires that bring ruin.

## So What

At the bedside or the grave, you do not need to call decay good in order to trust God. Scripture names its bondage and promises release.

Until that day, care for what is fragile. Resist what corrupts. The future promised in Christ gives present love a reason to endure, because the final word over his people will be life.
