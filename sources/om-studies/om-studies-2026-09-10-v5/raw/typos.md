The Old Testament can feel like someone else's history until Paul tells a Christian congregation that the wilderness events were written for them. He is not asking the Corinthians to pretend they were Israelites crossing the sea. He is showing them how God's earlier acts instruct the people gathered to Christ.

The word behind that connection is more ordinary than the term *typology* makes it sound.

## The Word

**τύπος** (*typos*) means *mark*, *impression*, *pattern*, or *example*. Something leaves a recognizable form, or a form serves as the pattern by which something else is understood.

John 20:25 uses the noun for the nail marks in Jesus' hands, and the BSB retains *marks*. That concrete use helps us hear the word before it becomes a term in a Bible-study method. A *typos* can be something recognizable and definite, not merely a resemblance someone has imagined.

## Range of Meaning

A mark, a model, and a historical example are related uses, but they are not identical. Hebrews 8:5 uses the word for the pattern shown to Moses for the tabernacle. First Corinthians 10:6 uses it for events that serve as examples and warnings.

In Romans 5:14, Paul calls Adam “a pattern of the One” (Romans 5:14, BSB). The connection there concerns the action of one man and its consequences for many. Paul identifies the pattern and then explains how Christ's gift surpasses Adam's trespass.

## Where You’ll Meet It

Romans 5 does not make Adam and Christ interchangeable. Adam's disobedience brings condemnation; Christ's obedience brings righteousness and life. The correspondence includes a decisive contrast. A type can direct us to Christ precisely by showing what he overcomes.

First Corinthians 10:6 and 11 turn Israel's wilderness history into instruction for the church. Verse 6 uses the noun; verse 11 uses the related adverb *typikōs*. The warnings against idolatry, presumption, and rebellion are part of the application.

First Peter 3:21 uses another related word, *antitypos*, when connecting Noah's deliverance through water with Baptism. It is a correspondence Peter himself supplies. The passage directs attention to salvation through Christ's resurrection, not to an ingenuity test for the reader.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The apostles teach us to read the Old Testament toward Christ while respecting what actually happened. Adam, the exodus, and the tabernacle retain their historical reality. Their significance is not exhausted by what a modern reader could reconstruct without the New Testament.

That gives both confidence and discipline. We may follow connections the biblical writers make. We need not assign a secret meaning to every number, color, or household object. The text's argument, not the interpreter's cleverness, governs the reading.

[Skia](/greek/skia/) considers the related image of a shadow, while [Plēroō](/greek/pleroo/) follows the language of fulfillment. Together they help explain why the church keeps reading Israel's Scriptures after Christ has come.

## So What

When you meet an Old Testament passage again in the New Testament, stay with the connection long enough to see what the apostle does with it. Sometimes it promises. Sometimes it warns. Often it does both.

You are reading the history of the God who has acted for you in Christ. The earlier pages belong to that same saving purpose, and the apostles teach you how to hear them.
