## What It Means

*ʿĒd* means "witness": one who testifies to what is true. It is a legal word. A matter is established by two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15), and the Eighth Commandment forbids bearing false witness against your neighbor (Exodus 20:16).

A witness does not have to be a person. A heap of stones can be a witness between Jacob and Laban (Genesis 31:44-48), a song can be a witness against Israel (Deuteronomy 31:19), and a stone can be a witness that heard the words of the Lord (Joshua 24:27). Whatever stands as evidence can be called a witness.

## Where You Meet It

In chapter 16 Job uses the word twice, once against himself and once for himself.

First, his own ruined body becomes evidence:

> You have bound me, and it has become a witness; my frailty rises up and testifies against me. (Job 16:8, BSB)

His friends look at his wasted flesh and draw the obvious conclusion: God must be punishing him. The evidence is visible and it is damning.

Then, a few verses later, Job looks past the evidence on earth:

> O earth, do not cover my blood; may my cry for help never be laid to rest. Even now my witness is in heaven, and my advocate is on high. (Job 16:18–19, BSB)

The second word, translated "advocate," is *śāhēd*, a rare word for witness with an Aramaic flavor. Its relative appears in the Aramaic name Laban gives the heap of witness in Genesis 31:47. Job continues: "My friends are my scoffers as my eyes pour out tears to God. Oh, that a man might plead with God as he pleads with his neighbor!" (16:20-21).

"Do not cover my blood" recalls Abel, whose blood cried out from the ground (Genesis 4:10). Job wants his cry to stay unburied until someone answers it.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Who is this witness? Many interpreters believe Job is appealing to God himself: from the God whose anger tears him (16:9) to the God who will testify for him. Others see a heavenly figure distinct from God. The text does not settle it.

Luther gives us language for the first reading. He distinguished between God hidden, the God we meet in wrath and events we cannot interpret, and God revealed, the God who has bound himself to us by his promise. Job flees from God as he experiences him to God as he trusts him to be.

In Christ, both readings meet. The witness in heaven is God, and yet he is a person distinct from the Father. Jesus says that he testifies about himself and the Father who sent him also testifies (John 8:18). Revelation calls him the faithful witness (Revelation 1:5). John writes that if anyone sins, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous (1 John 2:1), and Hebrews says he always lives to intercede for those who come to God through him (Hebrews 7:25).

And Job's cry about unburied blood finds its answer too. Hebrews says we have come to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to sprinkled blood that speaks better than Abel's (Hebrews 12:24). Abel's blood cried for justice. Christ's blood speaks for mercy.

## So What

The evidence against you may be real. Your conscience, your record, and your circumstances can all testify against you, the way Job's leanness testified against him. But the case is not decided by the evidence on earth. There is a witness in heaven, and he speaks for you. That is what you hear every time the absolution is spoken: not the evidence against you, but the verdict of the one who testifies on your behalf.
