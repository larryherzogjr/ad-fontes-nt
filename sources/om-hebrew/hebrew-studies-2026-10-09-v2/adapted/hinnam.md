## What It Means

*Ḥinnām* is an adverb meaning "for nothing." Depending on context that can mean without payment, without cause, or to no purpose. The word is built on *ḥēn*, "favor" or "grace," with an old adverbial ending. In Hebrew, the word for "gratis" is formed from the word for grace.

The range shows up quickly in the Old Testament. Laban asks whether Jacob should work for him *ḥinnām*, without wages (Genesis 29:15). A servant woman goes free *ḥinnām*, without payment (Exodus 21:11). The psalmist is hated *ḥinnām*, without cause (Psalm 35:19; 69:4).

## Where You Meet It

The book of Job turns on this word. When the Lord commends his servant, Satan answers with a question:

> Satan answered the LORD, “Does Job fear God for nothing? (Job 1:9, BSB)

That is the accusation behind the whole book. Job's faith, Satan says, is a transaction. God has paid him in sheep, camels, and children, and Job pays God back in piety. Stop the wages and the worship stops too.

One chapter later, the Lord throws the same word back. Job still retains his integrity, "even though you incited Me against him to ruin him without cause" (2:3). The BSB translates *ḥinnām* "for nothing" in 1:9 and "without cause" in 2:3. Both are good English, but they hide the echo. In Hebrew, the accuser says Job's faith is not *ḥinnām*. The Lord says Job's suffering is.

The word keeps surfacing. Job complains that God multiplies his wounds *ḥinnām* (9:17). Eliphaz, hunting for the hidden sin that must explain everything, accuses Job of taking pledges from his brothers *ḥinnām* (22:6). The friends need Job's suffering to have a cause. The book insists it did not have the cause they assumed.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

Satan's question cuts deeper than it first appears. It asks whether anyone loves God for nothing. About fallen human nature, the honest answer is no. The old Adam in every believer bargains. He serves for wages and sulks when they stop.

Job does not answer the accuser by proving his love is pure. What he does at the ash heap is receive: "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the name of the LORD." (1:21). That is faith with empty hands.

The real answer to the accuser runs the other direction. It is not that Job loves God for nothing. It is that God loves for nothing. The ancient Greek translators rendered *ḥinnām* in Job 1:9 with *dōrean*, "as a gift, freely." That is the adverb Paul uses when he says we are justified *dōrean*, as a gift, by God's grace (Romans 3:24). It is the adverb he uses when he says that if righteousness came through the law, Christ died *dōrean*, for nothing (Galatians 2:21). And when Jesus applies the psalmist's words to himself, saying that he was hated without reason (John 15:25, quoting Psalm 69:4), the Greek is again *dōrean*.

Isaiah puts the whole thing in one sentence: Israel was sold *ḥinnām*, and will be redeemed without money (Isaiah 52:3). The one who was hated for nothing redeems for nothing. See [χάρις](/greek/charis/) and [δικαιόω](/greek/dikaioo/).

## Where People Get It Wrong

**The accuser's theology.** The prosperity gospel is Satan's question turned into a program. It treats faith as an investment and blessing as its return. Its mirror image is just as common: when loss comes, people assume someone must have done something to deserve it. That was the friends' theology. Both assume that faith and blessing are wages. The book of Job rules out both.

**Reading the book as a test Job passed.** Job is not the hero who proves that disinterested love for God is possible if you try hard enough. The book ends with him in dust and ashes, needing God to speak and needing a sacrifice (42:6-8). What survives the accuser's test is not the quality of Job's love but the faithfulness of Job's God.

## So What

When the conscience asks, "What did I do to deserve this?" or the accuser asks, "What is your faith worth once the blessings are gone?", do not answer by pointing at your faith. Answer by pointing at the God who justifies freely and the Christ who was hated for nothing and died for you. Faith is not the wage you pay. It is the empty hand that receives.
