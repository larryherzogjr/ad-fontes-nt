## What It Means

*Tām* comes from a root meaning "to be complete" or "to be finished." A *tām* person is whole: undivided, sound, all of a piece. The related noun *tummâ* means "integrity," and the related adjective *tāmîm* describes a sacrificial animal as "unblemished" (Exodus 12:5). *Tāmîm* is also said of Noah (Genesis 6:9) and required of Abram (Genesis 17:1).

The word is broader than moral perfection. Jacob is called an *ʾîš tām* (Genesis 25:27), translated "a quiet man" in the BSB. Song of Songs uses "my flawless one," from the same root (Song 5:2). The word describes wholeness of life and heart, not a flawless record.

## Where You Meet It

The book opens with it:

> There was a man in the land of Uz whose name was Job. And this man was blameless and upright, fearing God and shunning evil. (Job 1:1, BSB)

The BSB translates *tām* as "blameless." The Lord repeats the description twice (1:8; 2:3) and adds that Job "still retains his integrity," using *tummâ* (2:3). Job's wife picks up the same word in her despair: "Do you still retain your integrity? Curse God and die!" (2:9).

Then the word becomes a point of argument. Bildad insists that God will not reject a *tām* man (8:20), so Job's suffering must mean he is not one. Job answers that he *is tām*, and that God destroys the *tām* and the wicked alike (9:20-22). Later he vows not to give up his integrity until he dies (27:5).

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

*Tām* does not mean sinless, and the book makes that plain from its first scene. The same verse that calls Job *tām* is followed by this:

> And when the days of feasting were over, Job would send for his children to purify them, rising early in the morning to offer burnt offerings for all of them. For Job thought, “Perhaps my children have sinned and cursed God in their hearts.” This was Job’s regular practice. (Job 1:5, BSB)

The *tām* man lives at the altar. Later he asks God why he does not pardon his transgression (7:21) and speaks of the sins of his youth (13:26). His integrity is not a clean record. It is a whole heart that fears God, and fearing God includes seeking his mercy for sin.

This is the Lutheran distinction between standing before people and standing before God. Before people, Job really is upright; chapters 29 and 31 describe a life of justice, generosity, and faithfulness in his callings. Before God, he stands by sacrifice. The book's final vindication comes the same way it began: with burnt offerings, seven bulls and seven rams, and Job interceding for his friends (42:8).

The New Testament word that corresponds most closely is *amōmos*, "without blemish." It is said of Christ, the Lamb without blemish or spot (1 Peter 1:19), and then of us, whom Christ presents holy and blameless before God (Colossians 1:22; Ephesians 1:4). Blamelessness is Christ's gift before it is our description. Compare [τέλειος](/greek/teleios/) and [ἅγιος](/greek/hagios/).

## Where People Get It Wrong

**Blameless means sinless.** Perfectionist readings take words like *tām* and conclude that a real believer no longer sins, or that a Christian who still struggles is not really a Christian. Job 1:5 alone refutes this. The *tām* man is the man who offers sacrifices.

**Integrity as a bargaining chip.** The opposite error is the friends' error turned around: Job is *tām*, therefore God owes him better treatment. Job himself drifts toward this at times, and God confronts him for it, asking whether Job would put God in the wrong to justify himself (40:8). Integrity is real, but it is never leverage against God.

## So What

When Scripture calls believers "blameless" (Philippians 2:15; 1 Thessalonians 5:23), do not hear it first as a demand that crushes you. Hear it first as what God declares and gives in Christ, and then as the shape your life takes in your callings. The Christian is *simul iustus et peccator*: at the same time righteous and a sinner. Both halves are true, and neither cancels the other. See [σάρξ](/greek/sarx/).
