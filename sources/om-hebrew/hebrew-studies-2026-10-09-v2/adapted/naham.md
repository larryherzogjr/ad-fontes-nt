## What It Means

*Nāḥam* covers two meanings that sound unrelated in English but belong together in Hebrew. In one form it means "to comfort" or "to console." In another it means "to be sorry," "to relent," or "to change one's course." Both describe a deep inner turn: grief moved toward relief, or a course of action reversed.

The comforting sense is the one in Isaiah's great opening: "Comfort, comfort My people," which is *naḥămû naḥămû* (Isaiah 40:1). The relenting sense is used of God himself. He was sorry that he had made man (Genesis 6:6). He relented from the disaster he had threatened (Exodus 32:14; Jonah 3:10). And in a single chapter Scripture says both that the Lord regretted making Saul king (1 Samuel 15:11) and that he is not a man that he should change his mind (15:29), using the same verb.

## Where You Meet It

Comfort frames the whole book of Job. When Job's friends hear of his calamity, they come "to go and sympathize with Job and comfort him" (Job 2:11). Comfort is *nāḥam*. And to their credit, they sit with him on the ground for seven days without speaking (2:13).

Then they start talking, and the comfort curdles. Job's verdict:

> “I have heard many things like these; miserable comforters are you all. (Job 16:2, BSB)

"Comforters" is *nāḥam* again. Later Job asks how they could possibly comfort him with empty words (21:34).

At the book's turning point, after the Lord speaks from the whirlwind, the same verb appears in Job's own mouth:

> Therefore I despise myself, and I repent in dust and ashes.” (Job 42:6, BSB)

"Repent" is *nāḥam*. And in the epilogue, after the Lord has spoken, after the sacrifice, after Job has prayed for his friends, his family finally does what the friends could not. They come, eat with him, and comfort and console him (42:11), using the same pair of verbs as 2:11.

The book begins with comfort that failed and ends with comfort that arrived. In between, the turn happens in 42:6, and the verb is the same.

**A translation note.** Job 42:6 is famously difficult. The verb translated "despise" has no object in Hebrew; the BSB supplies "myself," while others supply "my words" and translate "I retract." The second half can be read "I repent in dust and ashes" or "I am comforted concerning dust and ashes," that is, about being mortal. But the verse's double meaning is a real feature of the Hebrew, not an accident.

## What Confessional Lutherans Hear

The Augsburg Confession says repentance properly has two parts: contrition, the terror of a conscience that knows its sin, and faith, which believes the gospel and is comforted (Augsburg Confession XII, 3-5). Hebrew puts both in one word. The *nāḥam* of sorrow and the *nāḥam* of comfort are not two unrelated events.

But notice the order in Job. Job's contrition does not produce his comfort. God's word does. The comfort in chapter 42 comes after the Lord declares that Job, not his friends, has spoken rightly (42:7-8), after the burnt offering, and after the intercession. Comfort is spoken to Job from outside him.

The ancient Greek translators often rendered the comforting sense of *nāḥam* with *parakaleō*, the verb behind *paraklētos*, the Comforter. Isaiah's "Comfort, comfort My people" becomes *parakaleite* in Greek, and Jesus promises that those who mourn will be comforted with the same verb (Matthew 5:4). See [παράκλητος](/greek/parakletos/) and [μετάνοια](/greek/metanoia/).

## Where People Get It Wrong

**"Job repented, so the friends were right."** Some read 42:6 as Job finally admitting the hidden sin his friends accused him of. The very next verse rules that out: the Lord tells Eliphaz that he and his friends have not spoken rightly about God, as Job has (42:7). Job repents of speaking beyond what he knew: "Surely I spoke of things I did not understand, things too wonderful for me to know." (42:3).

**"God changes his mind like we do."** When Scripture says God relented, it does not mean he was surprised or corrected. The same chapter that says he regretted making Saul king says he does not change his mind as a man does (1 Samuel 15:11, 29). God's relenting is his mercy keeping its promise to answer repentance, not his will wavering.

## So What

The friends did the right thing for seven days. They sat in the dirt and said nothing. They failed the moment they started explaining. Comfort is not an explanation of suffering. It is presence, and then a word from God spoken to the sufferer.

That is a test worth applying to our own care for people who are hurting. The church does not comfort by solving the problem of evil at a bedside. It comforts by staying, and by speaking the word that forgives and promises. Anything else risks earning Job's verdict: miserable comforters.
