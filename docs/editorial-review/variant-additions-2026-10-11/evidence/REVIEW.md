## candidate-105: Many Called, Few Chosen? The Ending of Matthew 20:16

Reference: Matthew 20:16

Payload SHA-256: `8e6200d14bbca9b79d7899b262b51495ff7bc53f7f7841738917bd423aed71be`

### Source observation

The shorter form ends with the reversal of first and last. BSB, the BLB publisher draft and Nestle 1904 stop there. MSB, Young’s Literal Translation, Robinson–Pierpont 2018 and Boyd’s Textus Receptus compilation continue with the saying about many being called and few being chosen. The difference is the presence of a closing clause, not a missing verse: every displayed edition contains Matthew 20:16 [C1].

The BSB note identifies the longer wording with BYZ and TR; the MSB note identifies its omission with CT. Those are the publishers’ abbreviated descriptions. The seven named edition rows let the reader check the actual wording without treating those groups as interchangeable manuscripts [C2].

In the pinned Sinaiticus transcription, the first-hand verse ends with the first becoming last. The additional called-and-chosen saying is not in this verse record. That establishes this witness’s recorded wording, not the origin of the shorter or longer form [C3].

### Interpretation

The shorter ending keeps the reader’s attention on the laborers’ reversal and the landowner’s generosity. The longer ending adds a further distinction between calling and choosing. It therefore changes how the parable’s closing words lead into reflection, even though both forms retain the reversal itself.

The same called-and-chosen saying appears in Matthew 22:14 in all seven frozen editions [C1]. Readers should distinguish its presence there from the question of whether it also closes Matthew 20. Similarity between passages is a reason to compare their contexts; it does not, by itself, prove that a particular scribe copied one ending into the other.

For teaching, read the whole laborers’ parable before turning to the additional clause. A teacher can acknowledge the different endings without making the shorter edition deny election or allowing one sentence to replace the parable’s treatment of the landowner’s generosity. This draft makes no judgment about which ending Matthew originally wrote.

## candidate-106: Carried Up into Heaven: The Double Brackets in Luke 24:51

Reference: Luke 24:51

Payload SHA-256: `4bdddcfa821ec19b80d0961c981ce95efcb6dbcf20e8eb2b392c3803f5d586a6`

### Source observation

Every displayed edition prints the clause describing Jesus being carried up into heaven. Nestle 1904 places that clause inside a pair of double square brackets; the other six editions print it without those editorial markers. The BLB’s separate brackets around an English supplied word earlier in the sentence are a different convention. Nestle’s marked clause must not be presented as a whole-verse omission or as an absent clause in its main text [C1].

The pinned Codex Sinaiticus Project transcription supplies a separate witness-level comparison. Its first-hand verse ends after Jesus parts from the disciples. A correction attributed to hand ca adds the ascension clause. The transcription preserves the addition as a correction rather than folding it into the first hand [C3].

This is a directly visible editorial-bracketing study supported by a particular manuscript’s recorded correction. There is no BSB or MSB publisher note at this anchor in the frozen releases. Neither an edition’s brackets nor this one manuscript’s history supplies a complete apparatus.

### Interpretation

With the clause, Luke’s Gospel explicitly describes the ascension at this point in its closing scene. Without it, the verse describes Jesus’ departure while blessing the disciples. That is a meaningful difference in what the particular sentence says.

The surrounding verses still describe the blessing, the disciples’ return to Jerusalem and their worshipful response. Acts 1:9–11 supplies a separate, explicit ascension account in all seven frozen editions [C1]. That neighboring evidence helps readers avoid turning a question about the wording of Luke 24:51 into the claim that an entire edition lacks the ascension. It does not settle the original wording of Luke’s Gospel.

Read Nestle’s printed clause and its markers together: the words are there, and the editorial qualification is there too. The Sinaiticus evidence then illustrates why first-hand text and later corrections must be described separately. This comparison does not assign motives to the corrector or endorse an originality judgment.

For readers, the practical task is small and precise: notice where the extra phrase occurs, then read the surrounding account of the Son and his saving work. The edition rows establish the difference at this verse. They do not settle its original wording by a vote of translations.

## candidate-109: By God’s Grace—or Apart from God? Hebrews 2:9

Reference: Hebrews 2:9

Payload SHA-256: `27ddaf3cc1fa263f4f7da5155e7ff860ccf817269fd84fa3f78ea23329613ed7`

**Evidence limitation:** All seven displayed editions read by the grace of God. The alternative is discussed in Origen’s Commentary on John, Book I, section 40, in the cited historical English translation; it is not printed or supplied in a publisher note by any displayed edition. ### Source observation

All seven displayed editions read by the grace of God or its Greek equivalent χάριτι θεοῦ. The frozen BSB and MSB chapters contain no publisher note at Hebrews 2:9 supplying the apart-from-God alternative. This is therefore an externally supported textual discussion, not a disagreement demonstrated by the seven main-text rows and not a publisher-note-only study [C1].

Origen’s Commentary on John, Book I, section 40, provides a primary historical discussion in the English translation by Allan Menzies reproduced by New Advent. It discusses the wording without God and explicitly notes copies of Hebrews containing the grace wording. The draft paraphrases that discussion; it does not import Origen’s quotation as an eighth Scripture edition or infer the text of a surviving manuscript from his report [C4].

The pinned Sinaiticus transcription reads χαριτι followed by the abbreviation for God. It supports the grace reading in this witness and supplies no evidence for apart from God [C3]. No structured attestation for another manuscript is claimed.

### Interpretation

By the grace of God connects Jesus’ tasting death with God’s gracious action. The alternative changes that connection and requires an account of how the phrase relates to the rest of the sentence. Origen’s discussion relates the wording to the scope of those for whom Christ dies; citing that discussion does not endorse his wider interpretation [C4].

Readers should begin with the actual displayed text and the surrounding movement from Psalm 8 to Jesus’ suffering and glory [C1]. A patristic author’s discussion can preserve evidence of a textual question beyond the app’s edition set, but it cannot be counted as a new app edition or as proof of the reading of a manuscript we have not examined.

This article leaves the competing reading’s origin and final interpretation unresolved. It neither asserts a rupture within the Trinity nor assigns a doctrinal motive to a copyist. Its purpose is to identify the question honestly while keeping the local edition evidence, the historical report and editorial judgment distinct.

## candidate-110: Freed Us or Washed Us? Revelation 1:5

Reference: Revelation 1:5

Payload SHA-256: `b38638455d7d4391deed81badc2fd30a56fdc034aeb72648f43ed14ecb86fcfd`

### Source observation

BSB says Christ has released us from our sins; the BLB publisher draft uses releasing. MSB has washed, and Young’s Literal Translation uses bathe. The displayed Greek editions show the underlying difference: Nestle 1904 has λύσαντι, while Robinson–Pierpont 2018 and Boyd’s Textus Receptus compilation have λούσαντι. The extra omicron distinguishes the two verb forms; the full verse also contains other wording differences, which should not be folded into this verb comparison [C1].

The pinned MSB note reports that CT has released. The manuscript groups behind that abbreviation are not reconstructed here. These are named edition readings and a retained publisher report, not a tally of independent witnesses [C2].

The Sinaiticus first-hand transcription contains λυϲαντι, corresponding to the release verb. Its exact unaccented transcription and lunate sigma are preserved in the evidence. This plate documents that one witness and does not independently verify support claimed for other manuscripts [C3].

### Interpretation

The release wording presents rescue from sins; the washing wording presents cleansing from them. Both displayed forms connect Christ’s action with his blood. The imagery changes, so a teacher should notice the difference rather than treating released and washed as interchangeable English synonyms for one Greek word.

Revelation 7:14 separately uses the imagery of washing robes in the Lamb’s blood in all seven frozen editions [C1]. That context can help explain why cleansing language is familiar in this book, but it does not prove that a scribe imported it into Revelation 1:5. The similarity of the two Greek forms likewise permits discussion of the textual question without establishing a particular copying event.

The choice of imagery matters to close reading, while the saving action of Christ remains explicit in both displayed forms. This article makes no originality judgment and does not manufacture a story about why the wording changed. Its evidence consists of the edition split, the publisher’s note and the separately identified Sinaiticus reading.
