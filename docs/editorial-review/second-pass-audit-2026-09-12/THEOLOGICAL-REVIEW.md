## 1. The *extra Calvinisticum* paragraphs in `theotes`

### 1.1 What the article says

Four passages carry the argument (all in "What Confessional Lutherans Hear" and "Where People Get It Wrong"):

1. "The *genus maiestaticum* … teaches that the divine attributes are communicated *to the human nature* of Christ — so that the human nature, in the personal union, shares in the divine majesty, omnipotence, omniscience, and omnipresence."
2. "Lutheran theology rejected this with the slogan *Logos non extra carnem* — 'the Logos is not outside the flesh.' … If the WHOLE fullness dwells BODILY, there is no 'extra' Logos floating around outside the incarnate Christ. The whole Logos is the whole Christ, in His body."
3. "The Reformed *extra Calvinisticum* protects the transcendence of the divine nature (it cannot be circumscribed by a human body). The Lutheran *theotēs sōmatikōs* protects the unity of the incarnate Person."
4. "If the whole fullness of deity dwells in the body, then the divine nature is not 'also outside' the body in the way the Reformed tradition has wanted to say. The Lutheran reading honors the *sōmatikōs* by refusing to add an 'also outside.'"

### 1.2 What the Confessions actually say

The Formula of Concord does not object to the *extra* by confining the Logos. It objects to the *inference* the Reformed drew from it: that because the divine nature is everywhere and the human nature is (they said) only in heaven, the Son of God is somewhere *without* his assumed humanity. The Lutheran answer is the reverse of confinement — it extends the presence of the humanity, not restricts the presence of the deity.

- FC SD VIII affirms without qualification that the divine nature retains its properties in the union, including omnipresence and infinity, and that the two natures are not mixed, changed, or made equal in essence (SD VIII 6–10, 18–19; Ep VIII 6–8). Nothing in the Formula says the Logos is "not outside the flesh."
- What the Formula rejects is the separation: "the divine nature … is never separated from the human nature" and that Christ's divine nature is not present in a way that leaves the humanity behind (SD VIII 11–12, 78–85). SD VIII 85 quotes Luther's 1528 *Confession Concerning Christ's Supper*: wherever you put God, you must also put the humanity; they do not let themselves be separated. That is the Lutheran objection, stated precisely — the Logos is *nowhere without the assumed humanity*, which is not the same as the Logos being nowhere outside the body.
- The Formula also refuses the naïve version of the article's sentence 1. It explicitly rejects that the human nature is "locally extended" or "diffused" through all creatures, and it does not teach a natural omnipresence of the humanity (SD VIII 92; Ep VIII 17). The presence of Christ's humanity is "wherever he wills," by virtue of the personal union and for the sake of the Supper — the Chemnitz *multivolipraesentia*, which the Formula deliberately preferred over Brenz's stronger ubiquity language. "Shares in … omnipresence," unqualified, states the Swabian position rather than the Formula's.

Two historical points worth knowing before revising:

- *Extra Calvinisticum* is a Lutheran polemical label from the seventeenth century; the doctrine itself is patristic and medieval (the Logos, as God, is not enclosed by the body — Augustine, John of Damascus, Aquinas), and Luther's own Christology assumes it. The Reformed formula *totus, non totum* — the Logos is wholly in the flesh but the flesh is not all there is of the Logos — is a formula Lutherans can and did accept for the divine nature as such. The dispute is downstream: whether the humanity is present with the Logos beyond the local mode.
- *Logos non extra carnem* is not a confessional slogan and, taken as written, denies what the Confessions affirm. As printed, sentence 2 ("there is no 'extra' Logos floating around outside the incarnate Christ") and sentence 4 ("the divine nature is not 'also outside' the body") will be read by any Reformed reader — and by careful Lutheran ones — as denying the omnipresence and infinity of the divine nature, i.e., as a Christology in which the incarnation circumscribes God. That is not the Lutheran position, and the Formula rejects it. The article's own sentence 3 half-recognizes the problem ("it cannot be circumscribed by a human body") and then contradicts it in sentence 4.

The Colossians 2:9 exegesis is not the problem. *Sōmatikōs* grounds the personal union and the Lutheran reading of the Supper legitimately. The problem is that the article moves from "the whole fullness dwells bodily" to "the fullness is not also elsewhere," which the text does not say and the Confessions deny.

### 1.3 Recommendation

Classification for your ledger: probable-error, S2 (a central formulation that can be read as materially misleading), needs a confessional rewrite rather than a word swap. Suggested minimal corrections:

- Sentence 1: replace "shares in the divine majesty, omnipotence, omniscience, and omnipresence" with "shares in the divine majesty — so that the man Christ, by virtue of the personal union and not by any natural expansion of his humanity, can be present where he wills to be, and can give what is his in the Supper (FC SD VIII 78–85, 92)."
- Sentence 2: delete the slogan *Logos non extra carnem* and the "floating around" sentence. Replace with: "Lutheran theology does not deny that the Son of God, as God, is everywhere. It denies that he is ever anywhere *without* the humanity he has assumed. In Luther's words, quoted in the Formula, wherever you put God, you must put the humanity too; they do not let themselves be separated (FC SD VIII 85)."
- Sentence 4: replace "the divine nature is not 'also outside' the body in the way the Reformed tradition has wanted to say" with "the Lutheran objection is not to the Logos being everywhere but to a Logos who is somewhere *apart from* his flesh. The Reformed 'also outside' is acceptable to Lutherans as a statement about the divine nature; it is unacceptable as a statement that the Son is present where the man Christ is not."
- Sentence 3 can stand if the two above are fixed; consider changing "the transcendence of the divine nature" to "the integrity of the divine nature."
- The "category mistake" paragraph's tone toward the Reformed is fair and should be kept.

Cross-record consequences: `anthropos` and `hypostasis` cite FC VIII for the *communicatio idiomatum* without these errors and need no change; `soma` grounds the Real Presence in the words of institution and the personal union, not in a confined Logos, and is consistent with the corrected wording. The three *genus* labels (PASS2-049) should be re-attributed to later dogmaticians in the same edit.

Reviewer note: this is a theological-precision correction, not a change of position. Every replacement above is the Formula's own teaching; nothing concedes the Reformed inference.

## 2. Kolb–Wengert and Luther quotations: sources and rights

### 2.1 The problem as it stands

The audit found three unnamed translations of AC IV across `charis`, `ergon`, and `dikaioo`; long unattributed quotations from AC VII (`ekklesia`), AC XXVIII (`episkopos`), and the Large Catechism (`epiousios`); two wordings of the Nicene clause (`monogenes`); Luther's *Freedom of a Christian* thesis in the LW 31 wording without attribution (`eleutheria`); and confessional page references that were not verified (`iesous`, `metanoia`). Only `iesous` and `metanoia` give full Kolb–Wengert citations.

### 2.2 Recommendations

1. **Adopt one edition and say so once per article.** Your standing convention is Kolb–Wengert (Fortress, 2000). Every confessional quotation should carry, on first use in each article, "Book of Concord, ed. Kolb and Wengert, [document] [article].[paragraph], p. [n]." Paragraph numbers are stable across editions and let a reader with Tappert or the Triglot find the passage; page numbers alone do not.
2. **Verify before changing — a physical checklist.** I could not confirm page numbers against a copy. The items that need a hand on the book: AC IV (Latin and German run in parallel; decide which column you quote and keep it corpus-wide — `charis` and `dikaioo` look Latin, `ergon` looks like Tappert/Triglot Latin), AC II, AC VII/VIII, AC XVII, AC XXI, AC XXVIII, Ap II, Ap IV, Ap XIII 3, Ap XXI, Ap XXIV, SC Creed II (cited as pp. 354–355) and Baptism (cited as p. 360), LC Creed II (cited as pp. 433–440 — check whether that range runs into the Third Article), FC SD VIII 78–92, FC SD XI 13 ("book of life"), Treatise 23–24. The `eikon` correction (Ap II, original righteousness) and the `theotes` rewrite above should be made against the page, not from memory.
3. **Rights.** Kolb–Wengert is a copyrighted translation (Augsburg Fortress). Short quotations with attribution are customary scholarly use, but the corpus currently quotes several sentences at a time in some articles with no attribution at all, which is the worst of both worlds. Options, in order of preference: (a) keep Kolb–Wengert, attribute every quotation, and keep each to a sentence or two, paraphrasing the rest; (b) for the free web edition, quote the public-domain Triglot/Bente–Dau (1921) text — which is what bookofconcord.org reproduces and which two articles already link — and cite Kolb–Wengert paragraph numbers alongside; (c) request permission from Fortress for the longer block quotations (`ekklesia`, `episkopos`, `epiousios`). Your existing policy that free resources use the NET and sold books the ESV suggests a parallel rule here: public-domain confessional text for the free application, Kolb–Wengert for the books, with paragraph numbers in both.
4. **Luther.** The `eleutheria` thesis is the LW 31 (Lambert/Grimm) wording, copyrighted; the 1896 Wace–Buchheim rendering ("A Christian man is the most free lord of all, and subject to none; a Christian man is the most dutiful servant of all, and subject to every one") is public domain if you want to avoid the question. The Ninety-Five Theses (`metanoia`, first thesis), the Heidelberg Disputation (`doxa`), and the Worms statement (`suneidesis`) should each name their translation. The Luther quotation in `parousia` ("if I knew the world were ending tomorrow…") is correctly flagged as "reported saying"; it should stay that way — it is not attested in Luther's works.
5. **Nicene Creed.** Use one English text corpus-wide and name it (Ambassador Hymnal or Kolb–Wengert pp. 22–23). `monogenes` currently mixes two.

Treat all of this as part of the correction, not polish: a confessional quotation without an identified edition is unverifiable, and an unattributed multi-sentence quotation from a copyrighted translation is a rights exposure the application does not need.

## 3. One frequency convention for the application edition

### 3.1 The situation

The 250 articles contain roughly 270 occurrence claims. Most are correct against Nestle/NA lemma counts, but the corpus never says which edition or which counting unit it uses. Some claims are lemma counts, some surface-form counts, some are scoped to the Pastorals without saying so (`authenteo`), and some appear to include compounds silently (`chara`). Meanwhile the application's own occurrence index runs over the pinned Nestle 1904 main text (7,940 of 7,942 verses; MRK.16.99 and two apostrophe-mismatch verses excluded), so a reader who clicks through from an article to the explorer can meet a different number than the article gives.

### 3.2 Recommendation

**Yes — state one convention, and state it once.** A single sentence in the explorer's about/limitations text and in each article's frontmatter-driven footer:

> "Occurrence counts in these articles refer to the lemma as indexed in the pinned Nestle 1904 main text (release `n1904-2026-09-05-m2-v1`); they exclude the appended shorter ending of Mark and two verses whose analysis is unavailable. Counts in other editions, or of individual inflected forms, may differ by a few."

Then:

1. Regenerate every headword count from the index and replace the article figure where it differs, labelling the change in `CORRECTIONS.json` as a convention alignment, not a factual error.
2. For cognate counts the index can produce (the "word family" lists), do the same.
3. For counts the index cannot produce — LXX counts (`apostolos`, `eirene`, `baptizo`), Hebrew counts (`hyios`, `eirene`), Pastorals-scoped counts, phrase counts (`didaskalia`), and book-internal counts (`chara`, `mathetes`) — either label the scope in the sentence ("in the Pastoral Epistles"; "in the LXX") or hedge ("about," "over"). Exact bare totals without a recoverable scope should not survive into v1.
4. Rule for wording: "appears N times" means lemma, Nestle 1904 main text, New Testament; anything else must say what it is.

If you decide against a corpus-wide statement, then the fallback is the reverse rule: every exact total must carry its own edition label or a hedge, which is more work and less readable. The convention is the better choice.

## 4. Linear review of the 243 whole-verse fallbacks

### 4.1 Why it is still owed

The audit found 33 fallback-produced seams (25 S3/S2 adaptation seams, 8 S4 presentation seams) by detecting grammatical symptoms — stranded lead-in words, lowercase starts, doubled verbs, duplicated verses. That method only catches fallbacks that broke the sentence around them. A fallback that replaced a phrase with a whole verse and happened to remain grammatical — but now quotes more than the argument uses, or shifts the quoted clause away from the Greek being glossed — leaves no symptom for a scan to find. The `no-finding` dispositions in `COVERAGE.json` mean "no lens fired," not "every fallback in this file was read and is sound." The manifest records 243 fallbacks; the audit examined perhaps 40 of them in context. The remaining ~200 have not been read by anyone since the adaptation ran.

### 4.2 Recommended procedure

1. **Pull the list.** The adaptation candidate manifest (`dad1b34f…`) and the validator report (`ebf8611f…`) should carry the 243 fallback replacement keys with their article, original phrase, and replacement verse. Export them as a worksheet: slug · section · original quotation · replacement · surrounding sentence.
2. **Read every one in context**, in filename order, with the sentence before and after. Classify each: (a) clean — the verse quotation reads correctly and the argument still rests on the words quoted; (b) grammatical seam — fix the lead-in; (c) gloss mismatch — the Greek phrase is now glossed with a whole verse; restore the phrase; (d) argument drift — the article's point depended on the dropped words (the `parrhesia`/`rhema` type); restore or rewrite; (e) duplication — the verse is already quoted nearby; remove one.
3. **Prioritize the 114 *Just Enough Greek*-format articles.** All 33 seams found were in that format; the 136 Explorer-format articles were written for the BSB and drew few fallbacks.
4. **Fix the rule, not only the instances.** The `smallest-supported-whole-verse-v1` rule should be replaced for v1 by a rule that refuses to substitute when the original quotation is shorter than a clause (say, under eight words) or sits inside a running sentence rather than after a citation dash; those cases should be routed to hand adaptation. Otherwise every future re-adaptation will reintroduce the class.
5. **Record it as a candidate release.** The worksheet plus your decisions become a `CORRECTIONS.json` for `om-studies-…-v8`, with the same immutable-candidate, hash-approval process the v7 promotion used.

Estimated effort: 243 items at two to three minutes each is roughly a day of careful reading. Given that five of the twelve S2 findings in the audit came from this class, it is the highest-value remaining task before a public v1.
