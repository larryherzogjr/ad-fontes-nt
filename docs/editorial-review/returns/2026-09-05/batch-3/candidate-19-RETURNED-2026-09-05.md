### A. Public title

**Acts 15:34 and What Happened to Silas**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Acts 15:33 has Judas and Silas sent off in peace from Antioch. Some Bibles then add a line saying that Silas decided to remain there. Only two of the seven editions compared here print it: Young's Literal Translation and Boyd's Greek Textus Receptus [S4, S7]. The Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible, Nestle's 1904 Greek edition, the Majority Standard Bible and the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine text all lack it [S1, S2, S3, S5, S6].

As at Luke 17:36, the Byzantine editions omit alongside the critical ones, and both Berean footnotes are careful about it: they attribute the sentence to the Textus Receptus, not to the Byzantine text [S1, S3]. MSB's footnote also carries a separate matter — a wording difference at verse 33 itself, where the two editions read "to the apostles" and "to those who had sent them" [C4]. Two different kinds of difference in one note; only the second concerns whether a whole sentence stood in Luke's account.

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

The sentence answers a question the chapter otherwise leaves hanging. Verse 33 sends Silas away; verse 40 has Paul choosing Silas to go with him [C3]. A reader working straight through wonders when Silas came back. Where verse 34 is printed, it explains that he stayed. Where it is not, the account simply moves on, and the reader is left to assume he returned or never left.

That is a real difference in reading experience, and it is worth saying plainly rather than smoothing over. It is also the kind of gap narratives often leave. Luke does not say when Barnabas and Mark reached Cyprus either, and no one thinks something is missing.

What cannot be settled here is whether Luke wrote the explanation or a later hand supplied it. Both are conceivable and the comparison of seven printed editions decides neither. A teacher can point out the tension between verse 33 and verse 40, note that some editions resolve it and some do not, and leave the historical question open — which is where the evidence actually leaves it.
