### A. Public title

**The Angel at the Pool: John 5:3–4**

### B. Public prose — source observation

John 5 describes the sick lying in the colonnades at Bethesda. Some Bibles continue: they were waiting for the moving of the water, because an angel came down at times and stirred the pool, and the first one in was healed. The Majority Standard Bible, Young's Literal Translation, Robinson–Pierpont and Boyd's Textus Receptus print all of that [S3, S4, S6, S7]. The Berean Standard Bible and the Berean Literal Bible print none of it, ending verse 3 at "the paralyzed" and going straight to verse 5 [S1, S2].

Nestle's 1904 Greek edition does something different again: it prints the material but encloses it in editorial markers, opening partway through verse 3 and closing at the end of verse 4 [S5, C3]. That is a third option — neither adopting nor removing, but flagging.

Two points of precision. First, the difference does not start at verse 4; it starts inside verse 3, with the clause about waiting for the water to move, and BSB's own footnote says so [S1]. Second, what divides the editions here is the Greek, not English style [C1].

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

What the passage loses or gains is an explanation. Without it, John reports a crowd of sick people by a pool, and the man Jesus meets says he has no one to put him in the water when it is stirred — so the custom is still visible in verse 7, in every edition [C4]. With it, the reason for the stirring is supplied, and attributed to an angel.

So the healing narrative stands either way, and so does the detail that the sick were waiting for the water to move. What differs is whether John's Gospel explains the phenomenon or leaves it as something everyone at the pool took for granted.

That distinction is worth keeping clear, because this passage sometimes gets discussed as though a miracle were at stake. It is not. Jesus heals the man by his word, with no reference to the pool at all, and that account is identical across all seven editions. The disputed material concerns a popular belief about the water, not the miracle John actually reports. What no one can determine from this comparison is whether the explanation stood in John's own text; that requires evidence of a kind seven printed editions do not supply.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** Nestle's angle marks are worth understanding, because they are a third possibility most readers never meet. An editor who brackets a passage is saying neither "this is Scripture" nor "this is not," but "the evidence is divided and I am showing you where." Modern editions do the same with square brackets in a few well-known places. A bracket is a piece of editorial honesty, not a verdict, and it is a good sign that an edition is telling you what it does not know.

Notice also where the mark begins. It does not open at the start of verse 4, where the verse number would lead you to expect it, but partway through verse 3 — because the disputed material does not respect the verse division. Verse numbers were added to the New Testament in 1551, centuries after the differences they now appear to organise, so it should not be surprising when a difference falls across one rather than inside it.
