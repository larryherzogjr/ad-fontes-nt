### A. Public title

**Acts 24:6–8 and a Difference That Ignores the Verse Numbers**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Tertullus is prosecuting Paul before Felix. In some Bibles the speech runs longer: after saying they seized Paul, it adds that they would have judged him by their own law, that the commander Lysias took him from their hands by force, and that Lysias ordered the accusers to come before Felix. Young's Literal Translation and Boyd's Greek Textus Receptus carry all of that [S4, S7]. The Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible, the Majority Standard Bible, Nestle's 1904 Greek edition and the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine text do not [S1, S2, S3, S5, S6] — another place where the Byzantine editions omit alongside the critical ones.

The verse numbers describe this badly, and it is worth saying why. The extra material does not fill a missing verse 7. It begins partway through verse 6, runs through verse 7, and ends partway through verse 8 [C2]. BSB's footnote sets it out in exactly that shape [S1]. Editions that lack it therefore have a shorter verse 6 and a shorter verse 8 as well as no verse 7.

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

What the longer form adds is Tertullus' account of how Paul came to be in Roman custody, with a complaint about Lysias' intervention. In the shorter form, Tertullus says they seized Paul and moves straight to inviting Felix to examine him.

The charge against Paul is unchanged either way, and so is Felix's response. What the longer form supplies is a grievance about jurisdiction — the claim that the Jewish authorities would have dealt with the matter themselves had the commander not intervened. A reader of the shorter text learns of Lysias' intervention anyway, from the surrounding narrative rather than from Tertullus' mouth.

This passage also belongs to something larger. Acts survives in an ancient family of manuscripts whose text runs roughly ten percent longer than what the modern critical editions print — the **Western text** of Acts, described at length in *Ad Fontes – Volume One* by Larry Herzog Jr. [C6]. Longer readings of this kind are its characteristic feature, and that is a claim about a whole textual tradition rather than about this verse alone.

The other lesson here is about reading. Verse numbers are a finding aid added in 1551, more than a millennium after the text they organise [C4], and differences between editions do not politely arrange themselves inside their boundaries. When a footnote says a verse is missing, it is worth checking whether the neighbouring verses are also shorter. Here they are. What this comparison cannot tell you is which form Luke wrote, and nothing in these seven editions settles it.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** If you compare two Bibles at this passage and count verses, you will conclude that one has an extra sentence. If you read the two side by side, you will find that three verses differ. Counting numbers is not the same as comparing text, and this is one of the clearest places in the New Testament to see the difference.

**Proposed for readers — what "Western text" means.** It is a family of manuscripts, not a place and not a denomination. In Acts especially, this tradition preserves a text noticeably fuller than the one modern critical editions print: extra explanations, extra names, extra motives. Scholars have argued for two centuries about whether it is an early expansion of Luke's account or an early form of it, and the question is not settled. Meeting the name here is useful mainly so that the next longer reading you find in Acts is recognisable as part of a pattern rather than an isolated oddity.
