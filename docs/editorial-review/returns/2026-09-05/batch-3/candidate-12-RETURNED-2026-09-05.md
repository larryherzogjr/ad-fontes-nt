### A. Public title

**Luke 17:36 and a Verse the Received Text Does Not Agree On**

### B. Public prose — source observation

After the two women grinding grain, some Bibles add a matching line about two men in a field, one taken and one left. Only two of the seven editions compared here print it: Young's Literal Translation and Boyd's Greek Textus Receptus compilation [S4, S7]. The Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible, Nestle's 1904 Greek edition, **the Majority Standard Bible and the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine text** all lack it [S1, S2, S3, S5, S6].

That last pairing is worth pausing on. Readers often picture two camps — old manuscripts on one side, the traditional text on the other — but here the Byzantine editions stand with the critical ones. Both Berean footnotes say so precisely: they attribute the verse to the Textus Receptus alone, not to the Byzantine text [S1, S3]. Both also point to Matthew 24:40, where a similar line appears in the Olivet discourse [C4].

Boyd's edition goes further and divides the Textus Receptus itself. His footnote records that of the three printed editions he collates, Stephanus' of 1550 does not have the verse, while Elzevir's and Scrivener's do [C2].

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

So "the Textus Receptus reads" is, at this verse, an incomplete sentence. Three printed editions all called by that name do not agree, and a reader comparing a King James Bible with a Greek text labelled Textus Receptus may find them differing for that reason alone. An edition is a set of decisions by an editor, not a fixed thing a Bible either follows or departs from.

The saying itself is not in doubt as a saying of Jesus. Verse 35 and verse 37 stand in all seven editions [C3], and both publishers note that a similar line appears in Matthew's version of the discourse. What differs is whether Luke recorded the field alongside the mill.

A teacher can show the class the footnote in Boyd's edition and let the point make itself: even inside the tradition most associated with a single received text, editors have disagreed. What a teacher cannot do from this comparison is say which edition preserves what Luke wrote. That is a question about manuscripts, and none has been examined here.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** Two labels do a lot of work in these discussions and neither is as tidy as it looks.

*Textus Receptus* names a family of printed Greek New Testaments beginning with Erasmus in 1516 and running through Stephanus, Beza, the Elzevirs and Scrivener. They differ from one another, and Luke 17:36 is one of the places where they do.

*Byzantine* or *Majority* text names something else again: the reading found in the bulk of surviving Greek manuscripts, reconstructed in modern editions such as Robinson–Pierpont. It overlaps heavily with the Textus Receptus but is not identical to it, and here it parts company.
