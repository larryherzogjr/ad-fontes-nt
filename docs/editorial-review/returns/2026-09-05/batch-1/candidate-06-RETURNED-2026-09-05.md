### A. Public title

**Mark 7:16 and a Sentence You Have Heard Before**

### B. Public prose — source observation

After Jesus says that nothing entering a man from outside can defile him, some Bibles add a single line: if anyone has ears to hear, let him hear. The Majority Standard Bible, Young's Literal Translation, Robinson–Pierpont and Boyd's Textus Receptus all print it as verse 16 [S3, S4, S6, S7]. The Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible and Nestle's 1904 Greek edition do not, and their text runs straight from verse 15 to verse 17 [S1, S2, S5].

Both Berean editions record the difference in a footnote — BSB at verse 15, attributing the line to the Byzantine text and the Textus Receptus; MSB at verse 16, noting that the critical text lacks it [S1, S3]. Unlike some other places in this collection, neither publisher points you anywhere else. The difference is in the Greek: the editions that print the line and those that do not disagree about whether the sentence stood there, not about how to render it.

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

The line is a summons to pay attention, and it is one Jesus uses more than once in the Gospels. Whether it stands here changes the pacing of the paragraph rather than its content: with it, the saying about defilement closes on a call to listen, and the disciples' private question in verse 17 follows a public challenge. Without it, verse 15 ends flatly and verse 17 does the same work by showing that the disciples had to ask.

There is not much more to be got from this one, and it is better to say so than to inflate it. No teaching rises or falls here. The honest summary is that a familiar refrain is printed in some editions at this spot and not in others, that both publishers tell you so, and that the reason lies in the underlying Greek copies rather than in anyone's translation policy. What a teacher should avoid is treating a short line as a test case for whether a Bible is trustworthy. A one-clause difference that changes no claim is exactly the kind of variation that gets recruited into arguments it cannot support.
