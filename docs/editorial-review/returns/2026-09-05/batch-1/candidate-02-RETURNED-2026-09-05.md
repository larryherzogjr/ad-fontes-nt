### A. Public title

**Why Some Bibles Have No Matthew 17:21**

### B. Public prose — source observation

In the Berean Standard Bible, Matthew 17 runs from verse 20 straight to verse 22. Verse 21 is not in the main text, and the same is true of the Berean Literal Bible and Nestle's 1904 Greek edition [S1, S2, S5]. Four of the seven editions compared here do print it: the Majority Standard Bible, Young's Literal Translation of 1898, the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine Greek text of 2018, and the Greek Textus Receptus compilation of Robert Adam Boyd [S3, S4, S6, S7]. The sentence reads, in MSB's wording, that this kind does not go out except by prayer and fasting.

Notice how the two publishers describe it. BSB's footnote at verse 20 says the Byzantine text and the Textus Receptus include the verse; MSB's footnote at verse 21 says the critical text does not [S1, S3]. Those are two publishers' claims about groups of printed editions, and here they agree — which is not always the case in this book. Both also point the reader to Mark 9:29 [S1, S3].

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

Follow that cross-reference and the picture settles. A closely similar saying stands at Mark 9:29 in all seven editions — though Mark 9:29 has a difference of its own, since BSB and Nestle print only "by prayer" while MSB, YLT, Robinson–Pierpont and Boyd add "and fasting" [S1, S3, S4, S5, S6, S7, C2]. So a reader whose Bible lacks Matthew 17:21 has not lost the saying about prayer, and a reader whose Bible has it has not gained something found nowhere else.

What that does *not* settle is how the difference arose. The two passages resembling each other is an observation; concluding that one was copied into the other, in either direction, is a historical claim needing evidence this comparison cannot supply. A teacher can say plainly what each edition prints, and can point to Mark 9. A teacher should not say that Matthew 17:21 was "removed," or that it was "added," on the strength of a comparison of printed Bibles.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.**

Two words are worth defining, since both publishers use them. The *Textus Receptus* is the family of printed Greek New Testaments that began with Erasmus in 1516 and stood behind the King James Version. The *Byzantine* or *Majority* text is a different thing: the reading found in the bulk of surviving Greek manuscripts, reconstructed in modern editions such as Robinson–Pierpont. They overlap heavily but are not the same, and they do not always agree — at Acts 8:37 the Byzantine editions side with the critical text and omit, while only the Textus Receptus prints. Here they happen to agree.
