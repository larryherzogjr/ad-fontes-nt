### A. Public title

**One Letter in Romans 5:1: Have Peace, or Let Us Have Peace**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Compare the four English editions in this collection at Romans 5:1 and you will find nothing to compare. All of them say that, having been justified by faith, *we have peace with God* [C2]. Compare the Greek editions and they differ — by one letter. Nestle's 1904 text reads ἔχωμεν; Robinson–Pierpont and Boyd's Textus Receptus read ἔχομεν [C1].

Omega for omicron changes the mood of the verb. ἔχομεν is a statement: we have peace. ἔχωμεν is a summons: let us have peace. In the manuscripts the two were pronounced alike and written nearly alike, which is why editors have to decide rather than simply read.

Both Berean editions tell you about it. Each translates the statement and footnotes the summons as "Or let us have" [C3]. That is a good instance of a general point: a difference in the Greek can be invisible in every English translation on your shelf and still be there. Here the footnote is the only thing that shows it.

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

The two readings pull in different directions, and it is worth being exact about how far.

Read as a statement, the verse declares a result: justification by faith has been granted, and peace with God follows from it. Read as a summons, it exhorts the justified to lay hold of a peace they have been given. Neither reading makes peace something we achieve. Both stand on the same clause — *having been justified by faith* — which is a completed action in either case, and neither reading makes that justification depend on what the hearer then does.

That last point is what a teacher most needs to make. Someone who learns that the Greek may read "let us have peace" can conclude too quickly that Paul is making peace with God conditional on our effort. He is not, on either reading: the participle has already settled how the justified came to be justified.

Which mood Paul wrote is a genuine question on which editors differ, and the comparison of these seven editions does not answer it. What can be said is that every English edition here translates the statement, that both Berean editions disclose the alternative, and that the doctrine of justification by faith is not what is at stake between them. Lutherans confess that we receive forgiveness and are justified before God by faith for Christ's sake, not for our own works — Augsburg Confession IV — and that confession stands whichever mood Paul wrote [C6].

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** This verse is the best short demonstration of something worth internalising: **you cannot detect Greek variants by comparing English Bibles.** Four English editions here agree word for word, and behind them sit Greek texts that differ. If your method for finding differences is to lay translations side by side, you will find some real ones — and you will miss this one entirely, along with a great many like it.

The footnote is doing the work instead. It is worth reading footnotes for this reason and not only when a verse looks odd.
