### A. Public title

**For Thine Is the Kingdom: The End of the Lord's Prayer**

### B. Public prose — source observation

The Lord's Prayer as Matthew records it ends, in some Bibles, with the words we say in church: for Yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever, Amen. The Majority Standard Bible, Young's Literal Translation, Robinson–Pierpont and Boyd's Greek Textus Receptus all carry it as part of verse 13 [S3, S4, S6, S7]. The Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible and Nestle's 1904 Greek edition end the verse at "deliver us from the evil one" [S1, S2, S5].

Two things are worth separating carefully. The verse number is present in every edition; what differs is where the verse stops [C2]. And the editions are divided over the Greek: the closing words stand in the Byzantine and Textus Receptus editions and not in the Nestle text [C1].

There is also a translation question in the neighbourhood, and it is a different question. Whether the last petition asks deliverance "from the evil one" or "from evil" is a matter of rendering the same Greek, not of which Greek stands there. Both Berean editions footnote it, and the Majority Standard Bible keeps the two matters in separate notes [C3].

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

Anyone who has prayed the Lord's Prayer in a Lutheran service has said these words, and they are printed in our hymnals. That is worth stating plainly, because a reader who finds the line footnoted in his Bible may wonder whether the church has been praying something it should not.

It has not, and the reason is not that the textual question comes out one way. As Larry Herzog Jr. puts it in *Ad Fontes – Volume One*, the doxology is the church's response to the Lord's Prayer in liturgical use [C4]. A congregation answering the prayer Christ taught with words of praise drawn from Scripture is doing something the church has always done, whether or not Matthew wrote those words at the end of verse 13.

What the difference does change is a question of description: whether the doxology is part of the text Matthew set down or the church's addition to it in worship. That is a real question, and it is not settled by comparing seven printed editions. A teacher can say what each edition prints, can point out that the words are in the hymnal either way, and should stop short of telling a class which the apostle wrote.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** Notice that three different questions meet in this one verse, and that keeping them apart is most of the work.

*Where does the verse end?* The editions differ, and the difference is in the Greek.

*How should the last petition be translated?* "From the evil one" or "from evil" render the same words; that is a translation question and has nothing to do with the first.

*What does the church pray?* A third question again, and one the first two do not settle. Liturgy draws on Scripture without being a transcript of it.
