### A. Public title

**Mark 1:1 and Two Words in Brackets**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Mark opens: the beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. All seven editions compared here carry that phrase [C1]. But Nestle's 1904 Greek edition sets the last two words inside editorial markers — Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ ⟨Υἱοῦ Θεοῦ⟩ — rather than printing them plainly [S5, C2].

The publishers explain why. Both Berean footnotes report that some modern critical editions omit the phrase while others keep it: on their account the Editio Critica Maior, the Nestle text and the Byzantine and Textus Receptus editions have it, while the SBL Greek New Testament and Westcott and Hort's edition do not [S1, S3, C3].

So this is not a case of old manuscripts against the traditional text. The traditional text and much of the critical tradition agree here, and the disagreement is among modern editors — which is what Nestle's markers are recording. Note also that the difference is not visible in English style: every English edition on the list translates the phrase, and the question is whether the Greek carried it [C1].

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

A bracket is not a deletion. When an editor marks words this way he is saying that the evidence is divided and that he is showing you where, rather than deciding silently. It is an act of disclosure, and a reader is better served by an edition that marks its uncertainties than by one that hides them.

What hangs on the phrase is less than it looks. That Jesus Christ is the Son of God is not a claim Mark makes only in his first sentence. It is what the voice from heaven says at the baptism, what the centurion says at the cross, and what the Gospel is arranged to demonstrate throughout. A reader whose edition brackets two words in verse 1 has lost nothing of Mark's confession.

Nor does the church's confession rest on this phrase. Lutherans confess with the whole church that the Son of God is true God, begotten of the Father from eternity, and true man born of the virgin Mary — the Nicene Creed's article on the Son, taken up in Augsburg Confession III [C5]. That confession is drawn from the whole witness of Scripture, and two words marked in Mark's first line neither establish it nor endanger it.

What a teacher should avoid is the reflex that a bracketed phrase about Christ's divine sonship is an attack on it. The editions that mark the phrase and the editions that print it plainly agree about what Mark's Gospel teaches; they disagree about how Mark's first line read. Which of them is right is a question about manuscripts, and nothing in this comparison of seven printed editions decides it.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers — what editorial markers mean.** Printed Greek New Testaments sometimes enclose words in brackets or angle marks. The convention says: *the editors judge that the evidence here is genuinely divided.* It is neither an endorsement nor a rejection. Different editions use different marks and apply them by different standards, so a bracket in one edition is not automatically the same judgment as a bracket in another, and what a given mark means has to be read from that edition's own introduction rather than assumed.

Two practical consequences. First, a bracket tells you where to look rather than what to conclude. Second, an edition that uses them is being more candid with you, not less reliable.
