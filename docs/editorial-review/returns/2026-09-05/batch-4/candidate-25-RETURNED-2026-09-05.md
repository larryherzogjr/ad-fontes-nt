### A. Public title

**Boast or Burn: One Word in 1 Corinthians 13:3**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Paul lists what love's absence empties out: giving away everything, and then handing over his body — either *to be burned*, or *that I may boast*. The editions divide over which.

The Majority Standard Bible and Young's Literal Translation read "to be burned," and so do the Greek editions here: Nestle's 1904 text, Robinson–Pierpont and Boyd's Textus Receptus, differing among themselves only over the verb's form [C1]. The Berean Standard Bible and the Berean Literal Bible instead render the boast: "exult in the surrender of my body," "that I may boast" [C2].

One thing about this comparison should be said openly. **No Greek edition displayed here carries the reading BSB and BLB translate** [C2]. Both publishers acknowledge the situation in their footnotes, BSB reporting that the editions it names read "burned," MSB reporting that NA and WH read the boast [C3]. So at this verse the Greek you can see is not the Greek behind two of the English texts you can see — a limit of the comparison, not a fault in the translations.

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

The difference is a single word, and in Greek the two are close: καυθήσωμαι and καυχήσωμαι differ by one letter in the middle. What they say is not close at all.

*That I may be burned* imagines martyrdom — the most extreme self-giving a reader could picture — and says that even that, without love, profits nothing. *That I may boast* imagines the opposite motive, and says that a surrender made for the sake of glory profits nothing either. The first reading measures love against the greatest sacrifice; the second measures it against the purest self-interest.

Paul's argument survives both, and it is the same argument: without love, the act gains the actor nothing. But the rhetorical shape shifts, and a preacher working from one reading will build a different paragraph than one working from the other. That is a real difference and it should not be smoothed away.

What cannot be settled here is which Paul wrote. Editors have divided, the English editions in this collection have divided with them, and no comparison of printed texts resolves it. A teacher can lay the two readings side by side, show that the point about love holds under either, and leave the question where the evidence leaves it.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** Two lessons sit in this one verse.

The first is that a variant can be one letter wide and still change the picture. καυθήσωμαι and καυχήσωμαι are a theta against a chi. Nothing about the size of a difference tells you about its weight, in either direction — some long passages change little, and some single letters change a good deal.

The second is about the limits of any comparison tool, this one included. At this verse the Greek editions on display do not include the text lying behind two of the English editions on display. That is not a flaw in the English translations, which are following an edition simply not shown here. It is a reminder that a comparison shows you what it contains, and that what it contains is a selection.
