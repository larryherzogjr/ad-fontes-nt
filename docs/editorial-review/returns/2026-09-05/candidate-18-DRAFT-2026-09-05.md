### A. Public title

**Why Some Bibles Skip From Acts 8:36 to Acts 8:38**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Read Acts 8 in the Berean Standard Bible and you go straight from verse 36 to verse 38. Verse 37 is not there. The same is true in the Berean Literal Bible, the Majority Standard Bible, Nestle's 1904 Greek edition, and the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine Greek text of 2018 [S1, S2, S3, S5, S6]. Two of the seven editions compared here do print the sentence: the Greek Textus Receptus compilation of Robert Adam Boyd (2022), and Young's Literal Translation of 1898, which sets it inside square brackets [S4, S7]. "Textus Receptus" names the family of printed Greek New Testaments that began with Erasmus in 1516 and stood behind the King James Version [C7].

The difference lies in the Greek, not in English style: Boyd's edition and Robinson–Pierpont disagree about whether the sentence is there at all [S6, S7]. Nothing is hidden, though. BSB and MSB each attach a footnote at verse 36 reporting the Textus Receptus reading and printing it out [S1, S3]. What a reader meets is a missing verse *number*, with the words moved into a note.

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

The effect on the story is real but contained. With or without verse 37, Acts 8 says that Philip preached Jesus to the man reading Isaiah 53, that the man saw water and asked what was stopping him from being baptized, that Philip baptized him, and that he went away rejoicing (vv. 35–39) [S1]. Where the exchange is printed, the narrative pauses for a stated condition and a stated confession. Where it is not, the request and the rejoicing carry that weight without a quoted creed.

The verse is often recruited into arguments about who may be baptized. It cannot settle them, in either direction. The man in the chariot is an adult hearing the gospel for the first time; the passage says nothing about anyone else, and a sentence whose printing is disputed is a poor foundation for a doctrine. Lutherans locate the power of baptism in God's word and command rather than in the recipient's confession — the teaching of Augsburg Confession IX and the fourth part of Luther's Large Catechism, resting on other texts entirely [C6]. Whether verse 37 appears in your Bible does not move it.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers** (subject to a separate display decision) — the first three sections below. **Internal reviewer note** — the last two.

**Why the number stays even when the words go.** Chapter and verse numbers are not part of the original text; the New Testament verse divisions in ordinary use were introduced in the sixteenth-century printed editions, at a point when the Textus Receptus was the text being printed. When later editors concluded that a sentence did not belong in the running text, they did not renumber the whole book. That is why an edition can print verse 36, then verse 38, and leave a gap where a number used to be. A blank space in a verse list is a numbering artifact; it is not a claim that something was cut out of your Bible this year [C8, flagged for verification].

**What the printed editions can and cannot tell you.** Comparing seven printed editions tells you what seven publishers decided to print. It does not tell you which reading is older, or which direction the change ran. Two editions printing the same words may simply be drawing on the same predecessor, which is exactly the case with BSB and MSB here — they are related Berean editions, and their footnotes at 8:36 are word-for-word identical [S1, S3, C4]. Counting editions is not counting manuscripts.

**How old is the exchange?** At least one thing can be said without a manuscript apparatus. Around AD 180, Irenaeus of Lyons retold this episode and had the eunuch say that he believed Jesus Christ to be the Son of God, citing Acts 8 [C5]. So the confession was known and quoted in the second century. Note what that does and does not show: in the Latin text of Irenaeus that survives, the eunuch's confession appears, but Philip's condition ("if you believe with all your heart") does not. Early quotation of part of a passage is not the same as evidence for the whole sentence as later printed, and a father quoting a line does not by itself establish which copies of Acts contained it.

**Internal note — Boyd's footnotes are informative by their absence.** Boyd's introduction states that his edition collates Stephanus 1550 (ST), Elzevir 1624 (ELZ) and Scrivener 1881 (SCR), adopting whatever two of the three support and footnoting the third [C7]. In this release he footnotes Acts 8 at 8:13, 8:19 and 8:28 — and not at 8:37. Within those three printed Textus Receptus editions, then, verse 37 is not a point of disagreement. That is a claim about three printed books of the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries, and about nothing else.

**Internal note — what was not verified, and what should be before this is published.**

1. **No critical apparatus was consulted.** NA28, UBS5 and the ECM were not examined. Every manuscript-level claim commonly attached to this verse — which papyri and uncials omit it, which minuscules contain it, the dating of Codex Laudianus (E, 08), the Old Latin and Syriac evidence — is therefore *unverified here* and is deliberately absent from the public prose. If a later revision wants to name witnesses, an apparatus must be checked first and the witnesses cited individually.
2. **The Erasmus story is deliberately omitted.** The account that Erasmus took this verse from a manuscript margin is widely repeated, but the sources I could reach disagree on the manuscript's modern Gregory–Aland number (2816 and 2818 both appear, alongside the older designation 4ap), and I did not resolve the identification. Rather than print a siglum I cannot stand behind, I left the whole episode out. It would be a good addition once verified against Metzger's *Textual Commentary* directly.
3. **YLT's brackets are not explained by the sources examined.** The 1898 prefaces reproduced at CCEL do not define a bracket convention [C9]. Robert Young states there that he adhered to the received text and regarded textual criticism as a separate discipline from translation — which makes a whole-verse bracket by the translator himself less likely, but does not settle who inserted it or what it means. Within the bundled corpus, YLT's brackets are word-level in 108 of 111 bracketed segments and whole-clause in exactly three: Acts 8:37, Mark 15:3, and the passage spanning 1 John 5:7–8. All three are famously disputed. *My inference*, not a documented convention, is that the whole-clause brackets flag doubtful material. The public prose says only that YLT prints the sentence in brackets, which is all the snapshot supports. Do not upgrade this to "YLT marks it as doubtful" without documentation from a printed 1898 copy.
4. **The verse-numbering history in the reader-facing paragraph above needs a citation** before publication. The substance is standard, but I did not verify it against a history of the printed New Testament in this session; it is marked [C8, flagged].
