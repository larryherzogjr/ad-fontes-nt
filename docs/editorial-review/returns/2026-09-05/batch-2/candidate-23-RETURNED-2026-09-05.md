### A. Public title

**Romans 16:24 and a Blessing Given Twice**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Paul blesses the Roman church at 16:20 — the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you — and then returns to greetings from Gaius, Erastus and Quartus. In some Bibles a second, nearly identical blessing follows at verse 24. The Majority Standard Bible, Young's Literal Translation, Robinson–Pierpont and Boyd's Textus Receptus print it [S3, S4, S6, S7]. The Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible and Nestle's 1904 Greek edition do not [S1, S2, S5]. The two are close but not identical: the second adds "all" and an Amen [C2].

Here the usual shorthand breaks down, and the publishers are the ones who say so. BSB's footnote attributes the verse to "SBL, BYZ, and TR"; MSB's to "SBL, MT, and TR," adding that other critical editions omit it [S1, S3]. SBL is itself a modern critical edition. So this is not a case of the critical editions on one side and the traditional text on the other — modern editors differ among themselves [C3].

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

As Larry Herzog Jr. observes in *Ad Fontes – Volume One*, this is a benediction doubling one Paul has already given a few verses earlier, and its pastoral weight is essentially nil [C5]. That is the right size for it. The blessing is not in question: it stands at 16:20 in all seven editions on this list [C2]. What differs is whether Paul closed the letter by giving it a second time.

The reason it is worth a note anyway is the company it keeps. This is the one place in this collection where the modern critical editions do not agree with each other, and that is more instructive than the verse itself. A reader who has learned to sort Bibles into two camps — old manuscripts against the traditional text — will find the sorting fails here. Editors weigh evidence and reach different judgments, and an edition is a set of decisions rather than a side in a dispute.

What cannot be said from this comparison is which way the doubling ran. That the same blessing appears twice is an observation; explaining how it came to appear twice requires evidence of a kind seven printed editions do not supply.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** If you want one place to see that "the critical text" is not a single thing, this is it. Nothing in this note asks you to prefer one; the point is that the map most readers carry — ancient manuscripts on one side, traditional text on the other — is too coarse to describe what editors actually do.
