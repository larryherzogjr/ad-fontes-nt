### A. Public title

**Luke 23:17 and the Custom That Explains Barabbas**

### B. Public prose — source observation

At Luke 23:16 Pilate says he will punish Jesus and release him. At 23:18 the crowd shouts for Barabbas instead. Between them, some Bibles print an explanation: that Pilate was obliged to release one prisoner to the people at the feast. The Majority Standard Bible, Young's Literal Translation, Robinson–Pierpont and Boyd's Textus Receptus have it as verse 17 [S3, S4, S6, S7]. The Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible and Nestle's 1904 Greek edition do not [S1, S2, S5].

Both Berean editions footnote it, BSB at verse 16 and MSB at verse 17, and both point to Matthew 27:15 and Mark 15:6 [S1, S3]. MSB's footnote also gives a more literal rendering of the sentence it prints — a translation matter sitting in the same note as a textual one. Keep the two apart: how to render a verse and whether it stood in the Greek are different questions, and only the second is what divides these editions [C4].

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

The sentence is an aside, not an event. It explains a custom rather than reporting an action, and its job is to tell a reader why a prisoner is suddenly on offer. Where it is absent, that job is done elsewhere: Mark 15:6 reports the same custom, in all seven editions on this list, without a footnote anywhere [C2]. A reader working through Luke alone will meet the crowd's demand with less preparation; a reader with the four Gospels in hand is not missing anything.

The narrative does not stumble either way. Pilate's offer, the crowd's counter-demand, and Barabbas himself are all in the passage regardless.

What a teacher should resist is the temptation to make an explanatory aside carry weight it never had. Nothing about Pilate's conduct, the crowd's guilt, or the innocence Luke has him declare three times depends on whether verse 17 is printed. And whether Luke wrote the sentence or a later hand supplied it from the other Gospels is a historical question this comparison cannot decide, in either direction.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** Not every difference between editions is the same kind of thing. Some concern a claim, some a whole scene, and some — like this one — concern a piece of background a writer may or may not have stopped to supply. Sorting a difference into the right kind before asking what is at stake in it will save a good deal of unnecessary alarm.
