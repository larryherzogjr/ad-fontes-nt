### A. Public title

**The Three Heavenly Witnesses: 1 John 5:7–8**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Some Bibles read, at 1 John 5:7, that there are three that bear witness in heaven — the Father, the Word and the Holy Spirit — and that these three are one; and then, at verse 8, that there are three that bear witness on earth. Others read only that there are three that testify: the Spirit, the water and the blood.

Of the seven editions compared here, **two** carry the longer reading. Boyd's Greek Textus Receptus prints it, and Young's Literal Translation prints it inside square brackets that open in verse 7 and close in verse 8 [C1, C2]. The other five — the Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible, the Majority Standard Bible, Nestle's 1904 Greek edition and the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine text — read the short form [C1].

That division is worth noting carefully. This is not the critical editions against the traditional text. **The Byzantine editions read the short form too.** Both Berean publishers attribute the longer reading to the Textus Receptus and to particular named editions, and neither attributes it to the Byzantine text [C3].

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

The doctrine of the Trinity does not rest on this sentence, and it is worth saying so plainly to a reader meeting the difference for the first time.

Lutherans confess with the whole church one God in three persons, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, of one divine essence and power, co-eternal — the Nicene and Athanasian Creeds, and Augsburg Confession I [C4]. That confession was framed, taught and defended from the whole witness of Scripture. An edition that prints the short form of 1 John 5:7 confesses the Trinity exactly as an edition that prints the long one, and the churches using such editions confess it in the same words every Sunday.

The manuscript picture is set out by Larry Herzog Jr. in *Ad Fontes – Volume One*. On his account none of the great early Greek manuscripts carries the Comma — Sinaiticus and Vaticanus from the fourth century, Alexandrinus and Ephraemi Rescriptus from the fifth — and the first Greek manuscript to contain it in any form dates to the fourteenth century. The Greek copies that do have it are few and late: Codex 629 from the fourteenth century, Codex Montfortianus (minuscule 61) from the early sixteenth, Codex Ravianus from the sixteenth and produced essentially as a copy of a printed edition of Erasmus, and a handful of later ones [C6].

That is a considerable body of evidence and it points one way. It is also not the whole of the story — the passage has a long history in the Latin west and a tangled one in the printed editions — and this short note does not attempt the whole of it.

What the longer reading does add, where it stands, is an unusually compact statement of the three persons in a single sentence. Losing it from the running text of an edition is a loss of that convenience, not of the doctrine.

What a teacher should resist is the two errors that meet here. One treats the shorter reading as an attack on the Trinity; it is not, and the Byzantine editions reading the short form are not suspected of any such thing. The other treats the longer reading as obviously spurious and its defenders as foolish; the question of what John wrote is a historical one, and this comparison of printed editions does not decide it.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers — what a bracket is doing here.** Young's Literal Translation prints the longer material and encloses it in square brackets running across the verse boundary. Brackets in a printed edition conventionally mean that the editors regard the evidence as divided and are showing you where, rather than deciding silently. They are neither an endorsement nor a deletion.

One caution specific to this edition. Young's brackets are not used only for that purpose: across this collection they far more often mark English words supplied where the Greek has none — "[is]", "[them]" — and only a few times enclose a whole clause, as here. So a bracket in Young's should be read by its scope. A word in brackets is a translator's supplement; a clause or verse in brackets is something else. What that something else signifies in Young's own conventions is not documented in the material examined for this note.
