### B. Public prose — source observation

The account of the woman brought to Jesus by the scribes and Pharisees runs from John 7:53 to 8:11. Every edition compared here prints it in full [C1]. Six print it without any mark. Nestle's 1904 Greek edition prints it and encloses the whole passage in editorial brackets [C2].

Something else is visible if you open the footnotes, and it is unusual. Within these twelve verses the publishers record a remarkable number of small differences — the Majority Standard Bible alone notes eleven, and the Berean Standard Bible three more, naming a range of printed editions that read differently at one point or another [C3]. ### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

That density is the most informative thing here, and it is worth understanding rather than counting.

Where a passage's wording varies this much between editions, editors are working with a tradition that is less settled than usual. The scale of the variation is not itself an argument about whether John wrote the passage — that is a separate question, and the comparison of seven printed editions does not answer it. But a reader who notices that this passage's text wobbles where the rest of John's does not has noticed something real, and it is better explained than left as an impression.

The manuscript evidence is set out by Larry Herzog Jr. in *Ad Fontes – Volume One*. On his account the two earliest substantial papyri of John, P66 and P75, do not contain the passage — P66 running unbroken from John 7:52 to 8:12 — and the two great fourth-century uncials, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, both move directly from 7:52 to 8:12 with no break, marker or blank space. Codex Alexandrinus and Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus, fifth-century, likewise lack it [C7].

What is not in doubt is what the passage says. Jesus does not condemn the woman; he tells her to go and sin no more. Both halves of that stand identically in all seven editions, and no publisher note touches either. Lutherans confess that law and gospel are to be distinguished and both rightly preached — the law convicting, the gospel forgiving — as the Formula of Concord sets out in its article on law and gospel [C8]; whichever way the textual question falls, that distinction is taught throughout Scripture and does not stand or fall with this narrative.

A teacher can say that the passage is printed everywhere, that one edition marks it, that its wording varies more than most, and that where it stood in John — or whether it stood in John — is a question requiring evidence this comparison does not supply. What a teacher should avoid is treating the bracket as a verdict, in either direction, or treating a reader's attachment to the passage as if it settled anything.
