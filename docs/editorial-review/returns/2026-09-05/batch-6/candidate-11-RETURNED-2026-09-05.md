### B. Public prose — source observation

Mark 16:9–20 tells of the risen Christ appearing to Mary Magdalene, the commission to preach to all creation, and the ascension. Every edition compared here prints the whole passage [C1]. Six print it without any mark. Nestle's 1904 Greek edition prints it too, but sets the entire block inside editorial brackets, opening at verse 9 and running to the end of the chapter [C2].

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

A bracket is not a deletion. When an editor marks a passage this way he is saying that he judges the evidence divided, and showing you where. He has printed the passage; he has also told you what he thinks about it. That is disclosure, and an edition that does it is being more candid with you rather than less reliable.

What is at stake, if the passage were not Mark's own conclusion, is how the Gospel ends — with the women silent and afraid at verse 8, or with the appearances, the commission and the ascension. That is a real question about the shape of Mark's book, and it is a larger one than any single verse in this collection.

The evidence about which readers most often ask is set out by Larry Herzog Jr. in *Ad Fontes – Volume One*. On his account the earliest Greek witnesses lacking the longer ending — Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, the Old Latin k, the Sinaitic Syriac and the Sahidic — are independent witnesses from different regions rather than a single line of copying. He also cites two fathers who report on the copies in front of them: Eusebius of Caesarea, early in the fourth century, says the longer ending is absent from almost all the accurate copies and present only in some; Jerome, early in the fifth, says it is missing from almost all the Greek codices known to him [C6].

That is evidence, and it is worth weighing rather than counting. It is also not the whole of the case, and this note does not pretend to give the whole of it.
