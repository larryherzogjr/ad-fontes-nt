### A. Public title

**Matthew 23:14 and the Woe That Moves**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Matthew 23 is a series of woes against the scribes and Pharisees. Two of them are in question here, and the editions handle them in three different ways.

The Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible and Nestle's 1904 Greek edition carry one woe at verse 13 — that they shut the kingdom of heaven in men's faces — and have no verse 14 at all [C1].

The Majority Standard Bible and the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine text carry both woes, but in the order: defrauding widows at verse 13, shutting the kingdom at verse 14 [C2].

Young's Literal Translation and Boyd's Greek Textus Receptus also carry both, in the opposite order: shutting the kingdom at 13, defrauding widows at 14 [C2].

And Boyd footnotes both verses to record that Stephanus' 1550 edition orders them the other way round from the two editions he collates it against [C3]. The Textus Receptus is not of one mind here either.

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

So a reader comparing Bibles at this passage may find a verse missing, or present, or present in a different place — and all three impressions would be accurate reports of what different editions print.

What is not in question is the content of either woe. The charge of shutting the kingdom stands in all seven editions. The charge about widows' houses and long prayers stands in four of them here, and both publishers point out that it appears in Mark and Luke as well [C4]. Nothing Jesus is recorded as saying disappears from the New Testament on any of these arrangements.

What differs is how many woes Matthew lists at this point and in what sequence. That is a real question about Matthew's text and it is not answered by this comparison.

The useful thing for a teacher is the shape of the case rather than its outcome. Counting missing verses is a poor way to compare editions, because a verse can be absent in one, present in another, and present-but-elsewhere in a third. Only reading the passages side by side shows that.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** Three different kinds of difference meet in these two verses: something absent, something present, and something present in a different order. It is worth separating them, because they raise different questions. Absence asks whether a saying belongs at this place. Order asks how a writer arranged material both editions agree he wrote. Neither is answered by the other.

This is also a good place to see that "the Textus Receptus" is a family rather than a single book. Stephanus' edition of 1550 orders these woes one way; the editions Boyd collates against it order them the other. A reader comparing a King James Bible with a Greek text labelled Textus Receptus may find them differing for that reason alone [C3].
