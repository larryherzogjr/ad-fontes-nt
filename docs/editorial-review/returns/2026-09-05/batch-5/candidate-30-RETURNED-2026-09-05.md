### A. Public title

**Does 3 John Have Fourteen Verses or Fifteen?**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Third John ends with the writer saying he hopes to come and speak face to face, followed by "Peace to you," greetings from the friends, and an instruction to greet the friends there by name.

Every edition compared here contains all of that, word for word. What differs is where verse 14 stops. Nestle's 1904 Greek edition ends verse 14 at "face to face" and makes the closing greetings verse 15, so the letter has fifteen verses. The Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible, the Majority Standard Bible, Young's Literal Translation, Robinson–Pierpont and Boyd's Textus Receptus put the whole ending in verse 14, so the letter has fourteen [C1].

Both Berean editions note it in the same words: some translators begin a new verse 15 after "face to face" [C3]. Nothing here is a difference in the Greek text at all. It is a difference in how editors divided a text they agree about.

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

This is the clearest case in the collection of a difference that is not a textual difference. No word is added, dropped, moved or altered. Two editors drew a line in different places on the same sentence.

That makes it useful, because it shows what verse numbers are. They are a reference system, added to the New Testament in the sixteenth century, roughly fifteen hundred years after the letters were written [C5]. They serve the reader, not the text. Where a book is short and its closing runs on, editors have to decide whether the farewell is one unit or two — and reasonable editors have decided differently.

For a reader the practical consequence is small but worth knowing. If a commentary or a hymnal cites 3 John 15 and your Bible stops at 14, you have not lost a verse. Look at the end of verse 14 and you will find the words. The reverse also holds: a reference to the end of 3 John 14 may point at something your edition numbers 15.

Nothing about the letter's teaching, its greetings or its close depends on which way an editor divided them.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** Numbering differences are not rare and they are almost never interesting in themselves. What makes them worth one note is that they produce exactly the symptom people find alarming — a verse number that exists in one Bible and not another — with none of the substance. Learning to recognise the symptom saves a good deal of unnecessary worry, and this letter is the cleanest place in the New Testament to learn it.
