### A. Public title

**Tree or Book: The Warning at the End of Revelation**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Revelation closes with a warning against adding to or taking away from the words of the prophecy. There are two differences among the editions inside that warning, and they are unrelated to each other.

The first is a noun. Six of the seven editions say that God will take away the person's share *in the tree of life* — the Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible, the Majority Standard Bible, Young's Literal Translation, Nestle's 1904 edition and Robinson–Pierpont. Boyd's Greek Textus Receptus alone reads *from the book of life* [C1]. The Majority Standard Bible footnotes the Book of Life reading and attributes it to the Textus Receptus [C3].

The second is the mood of a verb. Nestle's edition and Boyd's Textus Receptus both state it flatly — God *will* take away. Robinson–Pierpont reads an optative: *may* God take away, a wish rather than a prediction. The Majority Standard Bible renders the wish and footnotes the other [C2, C3]. Both differences are in the Greek, not in English style.

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

There is an irony in finding textual differences inside a warning about tampering with a text, and it is worth naming rather than tiptoeing around. But the warning is about adding to and taking from the prophecy — not about the ordinary work of copying, comparing and printing that stands behind every edition on your shelf. An editor deciding between two readings his manuscripts offer him is not the person John is warning.

What changes between the readings is real but bounded. "Tree of life" ties the loss to Revelation's own imagery, the tree in the city that appears a few verses earlier. "Book of life" ties it to the register of the redeemed named throughout the book. Both images are Revelation's; the question is which one John used here.

The verb makes a subtler difference. As a statement, the sentence announces what God will do. As a wish, it invokes it. Either way the sanction stands and its severity is unchanged.

What no one can determine from this comparison is which readings John wrote. A teacher can show both, note that six editions agree on the tree and that the Textus Receptus stands alone on the book, and leave the historical question where the evidence leaves it — including resisting the temptation to make the warning itself an argument about which edition to trust.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** This verse is often quoted in arguments about Bible translations, on the reasoning that an edition lacking some words has "taken away" from Scripture. Two things are worth holding onto.

First, the warning is addressed to anyone who would tamper with the prophecy, and the editions in this comparison are not doing that. They are printing what their manuscripts give them and telling you, in footnotes, where those differ. Disclosure is the opposite of the thing warned against.

Second — and this is the sharper point — the verse cannot settle the argument it is recruited into, because the verse is itself one of the places where the editions differ. Whichever side you argue, you have to decide first what this verse says. It is not available as a neutral referee.
