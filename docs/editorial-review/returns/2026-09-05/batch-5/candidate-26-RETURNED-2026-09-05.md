### A. Public title

**God Appeared, or He Appeared: 1 Timothy 3:16**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Paul quotes what looks like an early hymn: great is the mystery of godliness — and then the first word of the hymn differs between editions.

Robinson–Pierpont and Boyd's Textus Receptus read Θεός: *God was manifested in the flesh*. Nestle's 1904 edition reads Ὃς: *who was manifested in the flesh* [C1]. The English editions follow. The Majority Standard Bible and Young's Literal Translation say "God appeared"; the Berean Standard Bible says "He appeared" and the Berean Literal Bible "[He] who appeared" [C2]. Both Berean editions footnote the alternative, and both keep that note separate from a second one about whether to render "vindicated by the Spirit" or "in spirit" [C3].

In the hand used by early Greek copyists, the two words are written very nearly alike. *God* was abbreviated as a sacred name, and the difference between the two forms comes down to a horizontal stroke through a letter and a line above it [C4]. That is a fact about how Greek was written, not a claim about what happened at this verse.

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

A reader who finds "He appeared" where his memory says "God was manifest" may reasonably feel something has been taken away. It is worth answering that directly rather than hurrying past it.

Under either reading, the sentence declares the incarnation, and its subject is Christ. Under the Byzantine and TR reading it names him God outright. Under the critical reading the pronoun looks back to the mystery Paul has just announced and the hymn describes the same Person — appearing in flesh, vindicated by the Spirit, taken up in glory. No one reading this verse in any edition on the list could think the subject was anyone but Christ.

The manuscript record for this word is unusually legible, and Larry Herzog Jr. sets it out in *Ad Fontes – Volume One*. In Codex Sinaiticus the original scribe wrote ΟΣ — *who* — with no stroke and no overline; a first corrector left it as written, and a later corrector, working some centuries afterward, added the stroke through the omicron and the line above that make it ΘΣ, *God*. Codex Alexandrinus and Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus show the same correction layer. On his account the early hands wrote *hos* and later correctors changed it to *theos*, while the reading with *God* is carried by the great mass of Byzantine manuscripts, by the printed Greek New Testament from Erasmus onward, and by the King James tradition [C7].

That is evidence about which reading is older. It is not evidence that anyone was tampering. A corrector who makes a devout reading explicit is doing something different from a forger, and the manuscripts preserve both layers side by side for anyone to examine — which is why the case can be described at all.

The doctrine is not carried by this word in any case. Lutherans confess with the whole church that the Son of God is true God, begotten of the Father from eternity, and true man born of the virgin Mary — the Nicene Creed's article on the Son, taken up in Augsburg Confession III [C5]. That confession rests on the whole witness of Scripture, and the same collection includes John 1:18, where the critical edition is the one calling the Son *God* and the traditional text says *Son* [C6]. Neither tradition is trying to raise or lower Christ.

Which word Paul wrote is a genuine question, and this comparison of printed editions does not settle it.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.**

*Nomina sacra.* Early Greek manuscripts abbreviated a small set of sacred words — God, Lord, Jesus, Christ — writing the first and last letters with a line drawn above. It saved space and marked the words as holy. One consequence is that some of these abbreviations are visually close to ordinary short words, and a reader comparing two editions is sometimes looking at a difference of a stroke and a bar. That is a general feature of how these manuscripts were written [C4]; it is not, by itself, an account of what happened at any particular verse, and this note does not offer one.

*Read this verse with John 1:18.* The two are the clearest pair in this collection for seeing that neither textual tradition consistently strengthens or weakens the confession of Christ. At 1 Timothy 3:16 the traditional text reads *God*. At John 1:18 the critical text does. A reader who takes either verse alone will draw a conclusion the other verse contradicts.
