### A. Public title

**Why 2 Corinthians Ends at Verse 13 in Some Bibles**

### B. Public prose — source observation

Paul closes 2 Corinthians with a holy kiss, greetings from the saints, and the benediction naming Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Every edition here contains all of it. They divide it into verses differently.

Nestle's 1904 Greek edition prints the kiss and the greeting together as verse 12, and the benediction as verse 13 — so the letter ends at 13:13. The other six editions print three verses, ending at 13:14 [C1]. Both Berean editions footnote that texts vary in the numbering of these last verses [C3]. No words are added or dropped by this; the same sentences are grouped under different numbers.

A second and separate difference sits in the benediction itself. The Majority Standard Bible, Young's Literal Translation, Robinson–Pierpont and Boyd's Textus Receptus close with *Amen*. The Berean Standard Bible, the Berean Literal Bible and Nestle's edition do not [C2]. That one is about a word rather than a number, and the publishers flag it separately [C3].

### C. Public prose — local significance and interpretation

Two lessons sit here, and the first is the more useful.

Verse numbers are a convenience laid over the text, not part of it. They were introduced in the sixteenth century, and where an editor judged the sentence divisions differently the numbers came out differently — with no disagreement whatever about what Paul wrote. If you look up 2 Corinthians 13:14 and your Bible stops at 13, nothing is missing. The benediction is there, numbered 13.

The second concerns the Amen. Liturgical endings attract an Amen, and the word appears at the close of many New Testament letters in some editions and not others. Whether Paul wrote it here is a real question and the editions differ; what a congregation says at the end of a benediction is a separate matter and is not settled by it.

Neither difference touches what the benediction says. The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, the love of God and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit stand identically in all seven editions [C5] — which is worth noticing, since this is the verse from which the church's most familiar trinitarian blessing is drawn.

### D. Optional further detail / teacher note

**Proposed for readers.** If a cross-reference sends you to a verse your Bible does not have, check whether the neighbouring verse contains it before concluding anything. Numbering differences are the commonest cause of a missing verse number that turns out not to be missing text at all. This chapter is a clean example: one edition simply groups the closing sentences into two verses where the others use three.
